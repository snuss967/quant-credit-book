# Chapter 3: Cognitive Rewiring, From PJT RSSG to Systematic Desk Head

## Cold Open: The Brambleworth RFQ

*Friday, 2:58 p.m. Lender call Monday at 9:00 a.m. The desk smells like cold coffee and DV01. (Brambleworth Packaging, its notes, the desk and everyone on it are fictional; the chime is real.)*

The RFQ arrives on the all-to-all platform with the little chime that Theo Marsh has already learned to hate: **client sells \$15mm, Brambleworth Packaging 9.25% senior secured notes, B3.** Eight dealers in competition. Two minutes on the clock.

The ticket sits in a grey box in the corner of his second monitor, and everything about it has been designed to tell him as little as possible. The counterparty field says "Client," with a two-word tag the platform attaches to the account: *Distressed Fund*. The size field says 15,000,000. A countdown bar, green for now, shrinks from the right. Beneath it a line of small type reports that the request went to eight dealers, which means seven other desks are looking at the same grey box at the same moment, each deciding, as he is, whether to answer at all. Most won't. In the electronic auctions of 2010 and 2011 only 15% to 29% of the dealers a client queried ever responded, and fewer in high yield (§3.2.3); the platform shows him how many were invited, never how many will turn up. Three weeks ago, at PJT, a decision about fifteen million dollars of a B3 credit would have taken a team, a week and a forty-page deck. Here it takes a hundred and twenty seconds and one number.

Theo does what six years at PJT RSSG trained him to do. He opens the credit agreement. He has the PDF bookmarked at the definition of "Permitted Investments," because that is where the unrestricted-subsidiary basket lives and where the bodies are buried. He gets as far as clause (q) of the definition, which incorporates by reference the "Available Amount," which is defined forty pages earlier, which—

"Theo." Ines Okafor has not looked up from her screen. "What's the pot, what are the odds, and who at this table knows more than you?"

"The pot is fifteen million face. The odds depend on whether the sponsor can drop the IP into an unrestricted sub before the—"

"No. The odds depend on why this person is selling fifteen million at two fifty-eight on the Friday before a lender call. You have a hundred and ten seconds. The credit agreement will still be there Monday. The client won't."

He tries anyway, because she asked three questions and he has answered none of them. *The pot.* Fifteen million face at 87 is about thirteen million dollars of cash, but that is the size of the ticket, not the size of the pot. The pot is what he stands to win or lose, and that depends on how long he will be stuck holding fifteen million of a bond whose prints on the TRACE tape he has never once looked at. He does not know Brambleworth's average daily volume. He does not know whether fifteen million is two days of it or two months. *The odds.* He has a feel for the credit, the feel of someone who has read the indenture twice, but a feel is not a number. When he tries to turn "probably refinances, unless the lender call goes badly" into a probability he gets something between 0.6 and 0.9, which is not an answer; it is a confession. *Who at this table knows more than you.* The counterparty field still says "Client" and the tag still says *Distressed Fund*. Whoever it is chose this hour, this size, this Friday. The tag and the choice are the only things about them he can see, and he has no idea how to read either.

Across the aisle, Dr. Kasimir "Kaz" Lind spins their chair around. "Let's just assume Gaussian. Mid is 87, daily vol is a point and a half, quote 86 and a quarter, done."

"The seller isn't Gaussian," says Ines. "The seller is a person. Persons have reasons."

Marguerite "Mags" Dunleavy, who has been pricing distressed paper since 1996, does not look up either. "Back in my day the seller had a name. His name was Stan. Stan called three dealers and sold to the one who hadn't read the 8-K. Now Stan is an algorithm and he calls eight of you at once."

Theo pulls the USB stick out of his pocket, the one with his old 13-week cash flow model on it, and holds it for a moment, for emotional support. Then he puts it back. He bids 85½, three-quarters of a point behind Kaz's Gaussian. He is covered: someone else paid 85¾. He has no idea whether that was a good outcome.

The bar runs out. The platform tells him he was cover, the grey box goes greyer, and the desk returns to its Friday-afternoon noise as though nothing happened, which, for everyone except Theo, nothing did.

He replays the hand on the walk home, and somewhere around the third block he discovers that he cannot say what "good" would even mean. Good because the bond falls on Monday? Then he would have been right, but for no reason he could name. He did not bid 85½ because he knew how the lender call would go. He bid it because three-quarters of a point behind Kaz felt like the kind of humility a person three weeks into a job ought to show. If the bond drops, he was lucky in the direction of his nerves. Good because he did not trade? He was covered by a quarter point, which in the language of the platform means he came second. In a poker room second is folding. In a pitch it is the firm that does not get hired. Nobody on the desk is paid for coming second. Good because the bid was "fair"? Fair against what? Against Kaz's Gaussian, which Ines dismissed in a sentence? Against the 87 mid, a composite of quotes from people who did not have to trade on them? Against what the bond will be worth in a year, a number nobody on the desk will ever learn, because the desk will not own it in a year?

At PJT he knew when he had been right. The plan was confirmed or it wasn't; the fulcrum landed where he said it would or the other side's banker ate his lunch at the valuation hearing. The feedback took months, but it arrived as a verdict. Here the feedback is instant, a chime and a grey box, and it is not a verdict at all. He made a decision under uncertainty with a hundred and ten seconds on the clock, the decision had an outcome, and the outcome tells him almost nothing about whether the decision was any good. What keeps him up is not that he might have been wrong. It is that he does not know what right would look like.

Neither do you, yet. **The question this chapter answers: what is a bid, and how do you know afterward whether it was a good one?**

The answer comes in pieces, and they arrive in the order Theo needs them. A bid is a posterior, the mean of a distribution conditional on someone having chosen to trade with you, and not a markup on a screen price (§3.1 and §3.2). You learn whether it was a good bid only from the hand history, and only after you strip out what the whole market did while you held the bond (§3.3). The posterior moves when a Form 8-K lands, by an amount you can compute and usually overestimate (§3.4). What you know that moves it faster than the tape is an inventory with expiry dates, the RSSG Edge Ledger (§3.5). And the most expensive thing the person across the table can know is that a liability management transaction is coming (§3.6, with Serta in §3.7). Two long stories carry the weight: SVB Financial Group's holding-company notes in the days around the seizure of its bank in March 2023, and Serta Simmons's first-lien term loan in June 2020. Between them sits a Tuesday morning when a markout report changed overnight without a single trade, because that, too, is the job.

## 3.1 From Survival Questions to Distributions

The RSSG question set has a shape. Will the company file? When does the liquidity run out? Where is the fulcrum? Who provides the DIP, and does it roll up the prepetition loans? Who holds the blocking position in each class? Each of these questions is about a *state of the world*, and each answer is a scenario with a narrative and a waterfall. You have answered them for years with a 13-week cash flow model, a capital structure table, and a credit agreement annotated in three colors of highlighter.

The desk asks one question, over and over, in three seconds: *given that someone just asked me for a bid, what is the conditional distribution of this bond's value over the time I will hold it?* That question has two pieces the RSSG question lacks. It has a **time index** (the holding horizon $h$, measured in days, not in the months it takes a plan to be confirmed) and it has a **counterparty** (the person who sent the RFQ, whose decision to send it is itself information). Strip those two pieces away and the questions are the same. The banker has been pricing distributions all along. The vocabulary was just "upside" and "downside."

A distribution, in the sense Chapter 1's box gave it (§1.3.1), is a list of outcomes with weights that add to one, and a market price is its mean in disguise. Back the weight out of a price and you get the raffle organizer's odds, not yours: a *risk-neutral* probability, written $\mathbb{Q}$, with a charge for bearing bad-times risk folded in, as against $\mathbb{P}$, what actually happens (the box is in §1.5; §3.4.2 prices the gap).

Moyer makes this explicit in a footnote that every RSSG analyst should tape to the inside of a credit agreement binder.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.407:* "Those with a penchant for probabilities will discern that if the upside/downside ratio is 15/25, the implicit expected probability that the refinancing will occur is better than 50/50 (62.5%, to be exact). In an efficient market, the probability-weighted value of each outcome should be equal: 15 × 0.625 = 25 × 0.375."
>
> **Annotation.** The setup is a distressed issuer with two pari passu bonds: one matures in three months and trades at 85, the other matures in three years and trades at 60. If the near bond is refinanced it pays 100; if not, the company restructures and the near bond collapses to the level of its pari passu sibling. Solve $85 = 100\,p + 60\,(1-p)$ and you get $p = 25/40 = 0.625$. This is a two-point distribution, and its mean is the price. The 2026 desk reads more into it. The probability is a $\mathbb{Q}$-probability (it is backed out of a price, with no discounting and no risk premium), so the $\mathbb{P}$-probability that the refinancing happens needs a view on how much the market charges for bearing this binary, and that charge is large for exactly the binaries a restructuring banker cares about. The downside of 60 is not a constant either: it is the price of the long bond *conditional on no refinancing*, and that price is itself a random variable with a recovery distribution underneath it. And, most important for this chapter, the footnote's "efficient market" is a market with no counterparty. On the desk, the price at which *you* can trade is the mean of the distribution conditional on someone choosing to trade with you. That conditioning is the whole job.

### 3.1.1 The two-point distribution and the variance it hides

> **Math, Gently.** *Variance and standard deviation.* The mean tells you where a raffle's outcomes balance; the variance tells you how far from that balance point they sit. Take each outcome, measure its distance from the price, square it (so that misses on either side both count, and big misses count a lot), and average the squares using the same weights you used for the mean. Take a bond priced at 90 that will pay either 100 or 70, so the good outcome carries a weight of two-thirds: it sits 10 points above the price and the bad one 20 points below. Weight the squared distances and add, $\tfrac23 \times 10^2 + \tfrac13 \times 20^2 \approx 66.7 + 133.3 = 200$ "points squared." Nobody trades in points squared, so take the square root to get back to points: the *standard deviation* is $\sqrt{200} \approx 14.1$ points, the typical size of the surprise when the outcome arrives. Why does the product $p(1-p)$ appear in the formula below, and why does it peak at a coin flip? Picture the two outcomes at the ends of a seesaw with the price as the fulcrum. If one end is nearly certain ($p$ near 0 or 1), the price sits almost on top of it, the surprise is almost always tiny, and $p(1-p)$ is close to zero. At $p = \tfrac12$ the fulcrum sits in the middle, every outcome is a full half-gap away, and $p(1-p)$ reaches its maximum of $\tfrac14$. A coin flip is the most uncertain a two-outcome bet can be. The formulas follow immediately.

Write the near bond's value at resolution as $V \in \{V^+, V^-\}$ with $\mathbb{Q}(V = V^+) = p$. The price is the mean,

$$
P = p\,V^+ + (1-p)\,V^-,
$$

and the variance is

$$
\operatorname{Var}(V) = p(1-p)\,(V^+ - V^-)^2 .
$$

For Moyer's numbers, $\operatorname{Var}(V) = 0.625 \times 0.375 \times 40^2 = 375$ points squared, a standard deviation of about 19.4 points on a bond priced at 85. Notice what the variance depends on: the *gap* between outcomes and the product $p(1-p)$, which is maximized at $p = \tfrac12$. A restructuring banker who moves the market's $p$ from 0.5 to 0.9 has not just changed the price; they have cut the variance from $0.25\Delta^2$ to $0.09\Delta^2$. Information is variance reduction. Remember that sentence. It is the reason a dealer charges a spread at all.

Two-point distributions are not a textbook toy, and the ex-RSSG reader has walked past them without naming them. On the afternoon of 25 April 2023, five weeks after SVB Financial Group filed for Chapter 11, its \$350mm of 3.5% notes due 2025 changed hands at 69 and its \$500mm of 1.8% notes due 2031 at 57 (MarketAxess prints, as reported by Debtwire on 26 April 2023). Both were senior unsecured obligations of the same holding company, issued under the same 2010 indenture, sitting in the same bankruptcy class with the same claim on the same estate. The petition had accelerated all seven tranches. In a liquidation a dollar of claim is a dollar of claim, give or take a few months of accrued interest, and the maturity printed on the note stops mattering the day the case is filed. So how can two claims with the same recovery trade twelve points apart? Hold the puzzle. It has an answer, the answer is a two-point distribution, and it arrives at the end of §3.1.3.

### 3.1.2 Three outcomes, and the law of total variance

Real distressed situations have more than two endings, and the shape of the list matters as much as its average: "70% sunny, 30% storm" and "mild with drizzle" can share an average rainfall and call for different umbrellas (§2.2.2's box tours the shapes a desk meets; Chapter 5 owns them). Take a stylized B3 issuer with three scenarios over the next year: a clean refinancing ($k = 1$), an out-of-court distressed exchange or uptier ($k = 2$), and a Chapter 11 filing ($k = 3$). Let scenario $k$ have probability $p_k$ and, within it, a value distribution with mean $\mu_k$ and standard deviation $\sigma_k$. The price is the mixture mean and the variance splits by the law of total variance:

$$
P = \sum_{k} p_k\,\mu_k, \qquad
\operatorname{Var}(V) = \underbrace{\sum_k p_k\,\sigma_k^2}_{\text{within-scenario}} + \underbrace{\sum_k p_k\,(\mu_k - P)^2}_{\text{between-scenario}} .
$$

Plug in illustrative numbers: $p = (0.55, 0.25, 0.20)$, $\mu = (100, 70, 45)$, $\sigma = (0.5, 6, 12)$ points *(illustrative)*. The price is $55 + 17.5 + 9 = 81.5$. The within-scenario variance is $0.1375 + 9 + 28.8 \approx 37.9$. The between-scenario variance is $0.55(18.5)^2 + 0.25(11.5)^2 + 0.20(36.5)^2 \approx 487.8$. Total variance $\approx 525.7$, a standard deviation of about 22.9 points.

Ninety-three percent of the variance is the question "which scenario are we in?" That is the RSSG question. The recovery distribution *inside* Chapter 11, the thing the plan-valuation fight is about, contributes only seven percent. This is the first aha of the chapter, and it explains why the ex-RSSG hire is valuable on a systematic desk: the expensive uncertainty in high-yield credit is overwhelmingly about discrete states, and a banker who reads the documents and the docket can shift probability mass between states faster and more accurately than a model fit to spread time series. Kaz's "assume Gaussian" collapses a three-humped mixture into one bell curve with the right mean and the right variance and the wrong everything else: a Gaussian puts its mass at 81.5, the one price at which this bond will almost certainly *not* settle. Figure 3.1 draws the two side by side.

### 3.1.3 Horizon: the desk does not hold to resolution

Between events a price is close to a random walk, the drunk leaving the bar whose hundred steps leave him about ten from the door (boxes in §1.5 and §6.1.1), so its typical move grows like $\sqrt{h}$: a bond that wiggles 1.5 points a day moves about $1.5 \times \sqrt{3} \approx 2.6$ points in three days, not 4.5. A lender call inside the hold is not a step but a trapdoor. A dealer who buys the bond on Friday does not care about its value at resolution next year; they care about the value at which they can sell it in $h$ days. Let $\mathcal{F}_t$ be the public information at $t$. The relevant uncertainty is the variance of the *price change* over the horizon,

$$
\sigma_V^2(h) = \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\mathbb{E}[V \mid \mathcal{F}_{t+h}] - \mathbb{E}[V \mid \mathcal{F}_t]\right),
$$

which is at most $\operatorname{Var}(V)$ and equals it only if everything resolves within the horizon. If the scenario will be revealed within $h$ with probability $\rho(h)$ and otherwise the price drifts with diffusive volatility $\sigma_{\text{d}}$ per square-root day, a reasonable first approximation is $\sigma_V^2(h) \approx \rho(h)\,\operatorname{Var}_{\text{between}} + h\,\sigma_{\text{d}}^2$. A lender call on Monday is precisely an event that pushes $\rho(h)$ toward one for a weekend horizon. That is why Ines asked about the calendar before she asked about the covenant: the Friday before a lender call is the moment $\sigma_V$ for a 3-day hold jumps from "a point and a half" to "a good fraction of twenty."

"So one $\rho$ and I am done?" Kaz asks. "One event, one $\rho$," says Ines. "Give me a lender call on Monday, a forbearance deadline on Wednesday and an 8-K whenever the company feels like it, and your $\rho$ is three questions pretending to be one, each changing which of the others matters." Mags adds the other half without looking up: "And if the bond prints twice a week, your $\sigma_{\text{d}}$ is the vendor's noise, not the bond's. Count the events, then ask whether the mid is a price or a guess. Then do the formula."

Now the SVB puzzle from §3.1.1: two notes, one claim, twelve points apart. The answer is that the market was pricing two states, not one. In most states of the world the case ends in a liquidation, or a plan that pays every senior unsecured claim the same cents on the dollar, and there the 2025s and the 2031s are the same claim. But in April 2023 there was a live theory, reported by Debtwire, that the estate might be solvent (the FDIC returns the roughly \$2bn the holding company had on deposit at the failed bank, the non-bank businesses sell for real money) and that a plan would then reinstate the notes or pay them in full. The precedents everyone cited were Washington Mutual, which Mags tells in §3.4, and Charter's 2009 reinstatement. In a reinstatement state maturity matters again. A 3.5% note with under two years to run is worth close to par; a 1.8% note with nearly eight years to run is worth only what its thin coupon is worth discounted at a 2023 yield. At an illustrative 6% yield the reinstated 2025s are worth about 96 and the 2031s about 74, a 22-point gap that exists in one state and no other. Write each price as a two-point mean, $69 = 96p + (1-p)R$ and $57 = 74p + (1-p)R$, subtract one from the other, and $22p \approx 12$, so $p \approx 0.55$ and the common recovery in the other state is $R \approx 36$ *(illustrative; everything after the two prices rests on the assumed 6% yield)*. The inferred numbers are a fiction worth having. They say the twelve-point gap was a bet on the reinstatement state with a floor in the mid-30s, close to where the hedge funds that bought in March had paid, and they say that the expensive question in SVB's holding-company notes, as in Moyer's refinancing, was "which state?" and not "what recovery within the state?" The reinstatement state never arrived; §3.4 has the ending.

### 3.1.4 The USB stick gets a job

Every scenario probability in the mixture is a function of something the banker already models. The probability of a clean refinancing depends on how many weeks of liquidity the company has left, how much of the revolver is available, and whether the sponsor has a reason to defend the equity. Theo's 13-week model computes the first of these, so it gets demoted from emotional support to a covariate:

$$
\texttt{liquidity\_runway\_weeks} = \frac{\text{cash} + \text{revolver availability}}{\text{weekly operating burn}} .
$$

"Congratulations," Ines says when Theo shows her. "Your spreadsheet is now a feature." It will be the first column of the Edge Ledger in §3.5, and it will come back, more rigorous each time, as a survival-curve input in Chapters 5 and 6, a state-space variable in Chapter 25, and a liquidity-runway term in the Priming Risk Score in Chapter 13.

*(A fictional composite of a credit market maker.)* What "your spreadsheet is now a feature" means in practice turns out to be a meeting. On a market-making desk a feature is not a number someone computed once; it is a column in a table that other code reads every morning, and the people who own that table have questions. The data engineer who runs the feature store, who has watched a hundred features born and half of them abandoned, asks three. When does it refresh? Theo says "when the 10-Q comes out." She points out that a revolver draw or a new term loan disclosed on an 8-K under Item 2.03 (creation of a direct financial obligation) changes the runway the day it is filed, not at quarter-end, so the refresh becomes "quarterly, plus every 8-K carrying Item 2.03." How is it stored? Point-in-time, she says: every value carries the date the desk *could have known it*, so that a back-test run next year sees what Theo would have seen this year, not the restated figure from the following 10-K. And what is it called? Theo proposes `runway`. There are already three columns called `runway`. It ships two weeks later as `liq_runway_wks_pit_v1`, with a one-paragraph definition that Theo rewrites four times. Nobody congratulates him on the name. That is how he can tell it was the important part.

The second input the banker has is less comfortable to write down: what other people know. Moyer is admirably blunt about it in the context of the same refinancing trade.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.265:* "who indicated that, while no decisions or commitments had been made, the bank would prefer to avoid a chapter 11 and was considering extending a \$500 facility, thereby enabling a refinancing of the ’04 seniors."
>
> **Annotation.** In Moyer's version of the trade the near bond trades at 85 and the far bond at 70, which prices a 50/50 refinancing. His investor believes the odds are better, and the reason given is a conversation with the bank. In 2002 this was offered as a description of how distressed investors form views. In 2026 it is a description of the information set that makes the person on the other side of your RFQ dangerous. Read it from the dealer's chair: somewhere in the market there is a holder whose posterior on the refinancing is 0.8 while yours is 0.5, and that holder's buy or sell decision is a function of the difference. Glosten and Milgrom turned this into a pricing rule in 1985, and §3.2 rebuilds it for RFQs. Note also the compliance subtext, which the 2024 SEC actions in §3.6 make explicit: information obtained from a lender relationship can be material non-public information, and a desk must model it as something *other people* may have, never as something it acts on itself.

## 3.2 The RFQ as a Poker Hand: Glosten–Milgrom for Request-for-Quote

An RFQ looks like a request for a price. It is better understood as a request for information that the requester is offering to pay for with a trade. When a client asks eight dealers for a bid on \$15mm, the client learns eight things about the market (or two, since most dealers will not answer; see below), and the dealer who wins learns one thing about the client: that the client wanted to sell at that price, right now, to them. Ben Dor's discussion of trader indications says the quiet part out loud, from the other side of the same exchange.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.121:* "This indicative quote is usually tighter than a market quote because a trader can use an attractive indication to generate inquiries (which provide the trader with information)."
>
> **Annotation.** Ben Dor raises this as a data-quality problem for liquidity cost scores: indications are biased tight because they are bait. Read it as microstructure and it is a theory of the RFQ. Information flows both ways across a quote. The dealer's indication buys inquiries, and the client's inquiry buys quotes. On a 2026 desk the indication is an axe published to the platforms' dealer-inventory feeds, the inquiry is an RFQ routed by an algorithm, and the information both sides extract is logged, timestamped and fed into models. Ines's version is shorter: "A quote is a question you pay to ask." Kaz objects that in equilibrium the dealer is *paid* to answer. "Only if you charge $\pi\sigma_V$," says Ines. "Otherwise you are the client's free data vendor." The rest of this section derives what $\pi\sigma_V$ means.

### 3.2.1 The binary model

> **Math, Gently.** *What it means to condition on someone choosing to trade with you.* A doctor at a walk-in clinic knows that about one adult in fifty in her town has the flu this week. Then a patient walks in. Before he says a word her estimate has already moved, because healthy people do not usually spend a Tuesday morning in a clinic waiting room. The fact that he chose to come in is evidence, and Bayes' rule (Chapter 1's box, §1.3.3) is the bookkeeping that turns her one in fifty into a *posterior*. A dealer is that doctor. The prior is the screen mid and the bond's ordinary uncertainty. The evidence is that someone chose to send *this* RFQ, and people who know the bond is about to fall are more likely to want to sell it than people who do not. Glosten and Milgrom made the bookkeeping exact for the simplest case, and the answer is startlingly compact: the bid sits below the mid by $\pi\sigma_V$, the probability that the sender is informed times the size of the move the informed sender knows about. If one sell RFQ in five comes from someone who knows ($\pi = 0.2$) and the move they know about is 5 points ($\sigma_V = 5$), the bid sits $0.2 \times 5 = 1$ point below the mid and the ask a point above. Three steps of Bayes get there, and they are set out below the equation.

Work under the desk's physical measure $\mathbb{P}$ (these are the dealer's beliefs, not risk-neutral prices). A client sends a sell RFQ for bond $i$. The dealer's prior on the bond's value at the end of the holding horizon is two-point: $V \in \{m + \sigma_V,\ m - \sigma_V\}$ with equal probability, so the prior mean is the mid $m$ and the prior standard deviation is $\sigma_V$. With probability $\pi$ the sender is **informed** and knows $V$; with probability $1-\pi$ the sender is **uninformed** and sends a sell RFQ with probability $\tfrac12$ for reasons that have nothing to do with $V$ (redemptions, a rebalance, a CLO manager trimming a CCC bucket). An informed sender sells only when $V = m - \sigma_V$.

Run Bayes' rule on this setup (three short steps, set out in the Full Derivation below) and ask what a competitive dealer bids if it expects to make exactly zero on average, which means bidding the expected value of the bond conditional on having been hit. The answer is the bid and ask

$$
b^{\text{GM}} = m - \pi\,\sigma_V, \qquad a^{\text{GM}} = m + \pi\,\sigma_V . \tag{3.1}
$$

The half-spread is the probability that the sender is informed times the uncertainty the informed sender is resolving. This is Glosten and Milgrom (1985), and it is the $\Lambda_{\text{tox}}$ term in the bid equation of Chapter 1, now with a formula attached. Notice what it does *not* contain: inventory, risk aversion, or funding cost. A dealer who is risk-neutral, has infinite balance sheet, and pays nothing to carry a position still quotes a positive spread, purely because some of the people who want to trade with them know more than they do. That spread is not a fee. It is an insurance premium against the informed fraction of the flow, collected from everyone, and paid out to the informed.

The joke, which is also the point: the uninformed seller pays $\pi\sigma_V$ for the privilege of trading in a market that contains informed sellers. The pension fund rebalancing on the first of the month is subsidizing the distressed fund that just left a lender call. Market making is the business of running that cross-subsidy at a profit of exactly zero, which is why desks that do it well care so much about the "exactly."

The derivation below also handles the lopsided bond a distressed desk usually faces, and its exact form is the one that prices Serta in §3.7. Figure 3.2 plots the charge it produces.

#### The Full Derivation: the Glosten–Milgrom bid

**Step 1: likelihoods.** The probability of seeing a sell RFQ in each state is

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{sell} \mid V = m+\sigma_V) = \frac{1-\pi}{2}, \qquad
\mathbb{P}(\text{sell} \mid V = m-\sigma_V) = \frac{1-\pi}{2} + \pi .
$$

**Step 2: posterior.** By Bayes' rule with the equal prior, the two likelihoods sum to $1 - \pi + \pi = 1$, so

$$
\mathbb{P}(V = m+\sigma_V \mid \text{sell}) = \frac{\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{1-\pi}{2}}{\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{1-\pi}{2} + \tfrac12\left(\tfrac{1-\pi}{2} + \pi\right)} = \frac{1-\pi}{2} .
$$

**Step 3: the zero-profit bid.** A competitive dealer who expects to make zero on average sets the bid equal to the expected value conditional on the trade:

$$
b = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[V \mid \text{sell}] = (m + \sigma_V)\,\frac{1-\pi}{2} + (m - \sigma_V)\,\frac{1+\pi}{2},
$$

which simplifies to the bid and ask of (3.1),

$$
b^{\text{GM}} = m - \pi\,\sigma_V, \qquad a^{\text{GM}} = m + \pi\,\sigma_V .
$$

**The asymmetric version.** Distressed bonds are rarely symmetric. Let the prior be $q = \mathbb{P}(V = V^+)$ with gap $\Delta = V^+ - V^-$, so $m = qV^+ + (1-q)V^-$. Repeating Steps 1–3,

$$
m - b = \Delta\,\frac{q(1-q)\,\pi}{\tfrac{1-\pi}{2} + \pi(1-q)} \;\approx\; 2\pi\,q(1-q)\,\Delta \qquad (\pi \text{ small}).
$$

With $q = \tfrac12$ and $\Delta = 2\sigma_V$ this collapses to (3.1). The small-$\pi$ form connects directly to §3.1: $q(1-q)\Delta^2$ is the variance of the two-point distribution, so the half-spread is roughly $2\pi \times \text{variance} / \text{gap}$. A bond near a binary event with $q$ near one half has the largest adverse-selection charge for a given gap, which is exactly the bond that ex-RSSG intuition flags as "this one is a coin flip and somebody knows which way it lands."

#### Units: the bug that bites

**Units.** Equation (3.1) is in price points if $\sigma_V$ is in price points. If your risk system gives you spread volatility $\sigma_s$ (in decimal per horizon), convert with spread duration: $\sigma_V \approx D^{\text{spr}}\,\sigma_s\,P$. For a bond at 87 with $D^{\text{spr}} = 3.5$ and a horizon spread volatility of 150bp, $\sigma_V \approx 3.5 \times 0.015 \times 87 \approx 4.6$ points *(illustrative)*. Mixing a spread-unit $\sigma_V$ with a price-unit $m$ is the single most common bug in RFQ engines written by coding agents, and it is silent: the bid is merely wrong by a factor of a few hundred.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The insurer and the pension fund.* The "uninformed seller" of the model is not an abstraction. It has an investment committee, a liability schedule and a regulator. Sit at a life insurer's fixed-income desk on the morning a B3 bond is downgraded and its NAIC designation slips a notch. The downgrade tells the insurer nothing the market does not already know; what changes is how much capital the position consumes, and the bond goes on the sell list for reasons that live entirely on the insurer's own balance sheet. A pension fund rebalancing back to its policy weights after a month of equity gains is in the same position: it sells because of a table of targets, not because of a view. From this seat the dealer's $\pi\sigma_V$ looks like a tax on innocence, and in a sense it is. The insurer cannot prove it is uninformed, so it pays the same adverse-selection premium per bond as the distressed fund that just left a lender call. What it *can* do is choose whom to ask, and the evidence says visible quotes change that choice. Hendershott, Li, Livdan, Schürhoff and Venkataraman (2026, *Journal of Finance* 81(4)), matching dealers' published quote runs to insurers' trades, find that dealers with no established relationship to an insurer take 23% of its trades when they are quoting the bond and 11% when they are not. A dealer that publishes a credible price can win uninformed business it never had a relationship with; a dealer that stays dark leaves the insurer to its usual counterparties. For the dealer, insurer flow is the low-$\pi$ business that pays for the high-$\pi$ business. For the insurer, the model suggests the cheapest way to lower its own $\pi$: be legible. A seller whose reasons can be read off a calendar (quarter-end, a designation change, a published rebalancing date) gives the dealer grounds to believe the sale is about the seller, not the bond.

### 3.2.2 Making $\pi$ a function of the RFQ

Chapter 1's Bayes box (§1.3.3) did this arithmetic in odds: each independent clue multiplies the odds by its likelihood ratio, and on the log scale the clues add, which is all a logistic regression is. The warning label is *independent*. A detective who counts the mud on the butler's boots and his brother's alibi as two clues, when one walk home through the garden explains both, has counted one clue twice.

A constant $\pi$ is a teaching device. In practice the probability that the sender is informed is a function of what the dealer can observe about the request. Let $x$ be the RFQ's feature vector and write the posterior as a logistic model, or equivalently, under conditional independence of the features, as naive Bayes in odds form:

$$
\pi(x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta^\top x)}}, \qquad
\frac{\pi(x)}{1 - \pi(x)} = \frac{\pi_0}{1 - \pi_0}\prod_{k} \text{LR}_k(x_k) .
$$

The features that belong in $x$ are easiest to meet the way Theo met them. After the close on Friday, Ines pulls the Brambleworth ticket back up and replays it one field at a time, the way she would replay a hand. Theo writes the fields down as she goes; the table is his page.

Of the counterparty: "A distressed fund selling into a lender call was the loudest clue on the ticket. You read it as a label. It was a likelihood ratio." Of the documents: "You read the Permitted Investments definition as far as clause (q). That's half a PRS. Next time, finish it on a Tuesday."

The row she stays on is the one Theo had taken for a formality. "Why eight?" He has no answer. "A client who asks the two dealers it trusts is hiding the request from six others," she says. "A client who asks eight wants a price more than it fears a leak. Which is this one?" Baldauf and Mollner (2024, *Journal of Political Economy* 132(5)) model that client, who limits how many dealers it contacts, and discloses as little as it can, because every extra contact adds competition and also leakage: a dealer that sees the request and loses can trade ahead of the client's next move. In that world a request to two dealers and a request to eight are not the same game with a different $n$; the client's choice is a clue, and the losers' information is part of the price. The leak is measurable, if small. A Tradeweb white paper (Caretti, Mahajan and Husveth, May 2026), studying investment-grade RFQs above \$1mm from January 2025 to April 2026, finds that all-to-all requests are followed by same-direction spread moves "in the order of 0.1 to 0.3 basis points" larger than for voice trades, with bigger effects for sells and for the less liquid half of bonds. It is vendor-authored and not peer-reviewed, and protocol choice was not randomly assigned, so it shows that the channel exists rather than its size on any one desk. It also means that a client who routes everything all-to-all will look a shade more toxic in the desk's markouts than an identical client who calls, for reasons that have nothing to do with what it knows.

Seven fields, seven clues, and the logistic model adds up their weights in log-odds, exactly as the detective did.

Each $\text{LR}_k$ is estimated from the desk's own labeled history. The natural label is the sign of the realized 20-day IDO markout, defined in §3.3. Under the symmetric model the link between the two is exact: a filled sell RFQ is followed by a down-move with probability $(1 + \pi)/2$, so a model for the probability of an adverse markout, $\hat{p}_{\text{adv}}(x)$, implies $\hat\pi(x) = 2\hat{p}_{\text{adv}}(x) - 1$, clipped to $[0, 1]$.

### 3.2.3 Many dealers: the winner's curse

> **Math, Gently.** *The winner's curse.* Three friends at a county fair each guess how many dollars of coins are in a jar, and then bid for the jar in a sealed auction, each bidding exactly their guess. Every guess is honest: on average each friend is right, and each is off by about a dollar in one direction or the other. The jar goes to the highest bidder, and the highest bidder is, almost by definition, the friend whose guess was most too high. Nobody did anything wrong, and the winner still overpays on average, because winning selected the most optimistic error. With three guessers whose errors are normally distributed with a standard deviation of one dollar, the winning guess is too high by about 85 cents on average (the expected maximum of three standard normal draws is $3/(2\sqrt{\pi}) \approx 0.85$). Swap dollars for points and the jar for a bond: a dealer that wins an eight-dealer sell RFQ won because its estimate of the bond was the highest of those that answered, so its estimate was probably high. The cure is not to guess better. It is to bid as though you already know you have won, shading by what winning would tell you. (Chapter 2's box in §2.3.2 sorted the dealers' quotes; this is what the sorting does to whoever comes first.) Milgrom and Weber's version is next.

An RFQ to $n$ dealers is a first-price auction, and a dealer only trades when its bid is the highest. Winning is information. Conditional on winning, the other $n - 1$ dealers' signals were lower than yours, and since everyone is bidding on a common value, that means your own signal was more likely too high. The equilibrium response, from Milgrom and Weber's (1982) auction theory, is to bid the expectation conditional on winning:

$$
b_n^{*} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[V \;\middle|\; s_i,\ \max_{j \neq i} s_j \le s_i\right] \;<\; \mathbb{E}[V \mid s_i],
$$

with the shading increasing in $n$. Combining this with (3.1) and the Avellaneda–Stoikov inventory term previewed from Chapter 18 gives the compact quoting rule this chapter's engine implements:

$$
b^{*}(x, n) = m - \pi(x)\,\sigma_V - w(n)\,\sigma_V - \tfrac12\,\gamma\,\sigma_{\text{IDO}}^2\,(2q + Q), \tag{3.2}
$$

where $w(n) \ge 0$ is a monotone increasing winner's-curse shading calibrated from cover prices, $\gamma$ is risk aversion, $\sigma_{\text{IDO}}^2$ is the residual variance after hedging rates and index beta, $q$ is current inventory and $Q$ is the RFQ size (positive when the dealer is buying). Here $\sigma_V$ is the dealer's *physical* uncertainty over the holding horizon, in price points, not an implied volatility (the Units note above, and a unit-tagged column in the Spec).

The empirical record supports both halves of the story. Hendershott and Madhavan (2015, *Journal of Finance*), using every U.S. corporate bond trade from January 2010 to April 2011 with 467,614 on MarketAxess, find that clients queried roughly 25 to 28 dealers per electronic auction, that only 15% to 29% of queried dealers responded (lower for high yield), that the modal number of investment-grade responses was three, and that about 60% of high-yield auctions drew two or fewer bids. Price impact rose with the number of dealers contacted, consistent with information leaking to everyone who sees the request. Fermanian, Guéant and Pu (2016), working with BNP Paribas's European corporate RFQ data, find that dealer quotes become more conservative as the number of requested dealers rises, that each dealer is less likely to answer as $n$ grows, and that the average number of answers clients wait for is "always around 2."

> **Poker Table.** "Everyone invites six dealers to the table and plays the first two cards they see," says Ines. "Be the first card or be the better one. Being the third is charity." The number of dealers in competition is the number of players at the table. The cover price, the second-best quote that a platform reports back to the winner, is the hand you didn't see, shown to you after the pot is pushed. A desk that logs its cover distance on every win is building the dataset that calibrates $w(n)$: if you routinely win by two points over the cover in eight-dealer auctions, you are not a great trader, you are the patsy who overpaid by two points. Theo's Friday 85½ was the cover; the winner paid 85¾. In a poker room that is called "folding correctly"; whether it was correct depends on where the bond is in five days, which is the subject of §3.3.

Theo's Friday had eight dealers in it, and he had no idea how many of them were people.

>
> **Annotation.** Man's point is about execution quality for a systematic investor: more responders means better fills and more data on which to build cost models, and the same page describes filtering "to only high-quality RFQ participants." Turn the telescope around. More algorithmic responders means more dealers who answer every RFQ with the same model, which means quote competition on the uninformed flow is brutal and margins on it approach zero. Hendershott and Madhavan found response rates of 15% to 29% in 2010–2011; a desk that today runs an auto-responder on every eligible RFQ has, in effect, set its own response rate to one and handed its adverse-selection defense to $\pi(x)$. That is fine if $\pi(x)$ is good. If it is a constant, the algorithm is the client's free data vendor at industrial scale. Adverse selection did not go away. It concentrated in what is left for humans: large, stressed, event-adjacent tickets in high yield and loans, exactly where $\pi(x)$ is large and exactly where the ex-RSSG reader's document knowledge moves the posterior. Automation turned market making into a two-tier business, and the second tier is the RSSG reader's home game.

## 3.3 Markouts: The Hand History

Every poker player who wins over a career keeps hand histories. Ines calls the markout "the hand history," and she means it literally: it is the record of every trade you did, replayed against where the market went afterward, so you can find out which of your decisions were good and which were merely lucky. A trader who does not look at markouts is a poker player who only remembers the pots they won.

### 3.3.1 Definition and sign convention

Markouts here are counted in basis points of *price*, hundredths of a percent of what the dealer paid, not of spread, because price is what the P&L is counted in (§1B.4 turns basis points into dollars; duration, in Chapter 4-B, converts spread to price).

For a dealer fill at price $p_0$ at time $t_0$, let $\eta = +1$ if the dealer bought and $\eta = -1$ if it sold, and let $m_t$ be a reference mid. The markout at horizon $h$, in basis points of price, is

$$
\text{MO}_h = \eta\,\frac{m_{t_0+h} - p_0}{p_0} \times 10^4, \qquad h \in \{1, 5, 20\} \text{ business days}.
$$

Positive is good for the dealer. A dealer that bought at 85.75 and sees the mid at 87 five days later has a markout of $+146$bp of price; one that sees the mid at 81 has $-554$bp.

The horizons need a justification, because equity desks measure markouts in seconds and the habit travels badly. Corporate bonds trade so rarely that a sub-day mid is mostly a model, and the time it takes a client to finish trading after a failed attempt is measured in days: Kargar, Lester, Plante and Weill (2025, *Sequential Search for Corporate Bonds*, FRB Philadelphia Working Paper 25-08) find, using MarketAxess inquiry data, that "it takes 2-3 days to complete a transaction after an unsuccessful attempt." A one-day markout therefore captures the immediate information content of the trade, a five-day markout captures the completion of the client's search and the dealer's typical unwind, and a twenty-day markout captures the slow information (the downgrade, the 8-K, the lender call) that an informed seller was actually trading on.

### 3.3.2 The decomposition

Add and subtract the mid at the time of the trade:

$$
\text{MO}_h \propto \underbrace{\eta\,(m_{t_0} - p_0)}_{\text{captured half-spread}} \;+\; \underbrace{\eta\,(m_{t_0+h} - m_{t_0})}_{\text{post-trade drift}} .
$$

The first term is what you charged. The second is what the market charged you back: adverse selection plus noise. Under the Glosten–Milgrom model with the zero-profit bid (3.1), if the reference mid converges to the true value by the horizon, then for a filled sell RFQ

$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{MO}_h \mid \text{fill}] \propto \mathbb{E}[V \mid \text{sell}] - b^{\text{GM}} = 0,
$$

because the half-spread $\pi\sigma_V$ exactly pays for the expected drift $-\pi\sigma_V$. If instead the dealer bids at the mid (it assumes $\pi = 0$), the expected markout is $-\pi\sigma_V$: the full adverse-selection cost lands in the post-trade drift. This gives the markout its diagnostic power. **A persistently negative long-horizon markout against a set of counterparties means your $\pi(x)$ for them is too low.** A persistently positive one means it is too high, and you are leaving trades, and franchise, on the table.

### 3.3.3 IDO markouts: strip beta before you accuse anyone

Theo's first markout report flags a pension fund as toxic: every bond it sold the desk in June fell over the next five days. Ines reads the report for ten seconds. "CDX widened twelve basis points that week and Treasuries sold off. You've just accused a pension fund of insider trading for owning beta."

What Ines read was the harbour, not the boat. If every boat in the harbour sits six feet lower in the morning, nobody's hull has a hole in it; if one boat drops six feet while the rest drop six inches, go and look for the hole. Beta is the tide and the residual is the hull (the box is in §1B.2), and the desk motto, "IDO or it didn't happen," applies to markouts as much as to alpha. Replace the raw mid with a beta-stripped reference:

$$
m^{\text{IDO}}_t = m_t - P_{t_0}\left(-D^{\text{spr}}_i\,\beta_i\,\Delta s^{\text{index}}_{[t_0,t]} - D^{\text{mod}}_i\,\Delta y^{\text{TSY}}_{[t_0,t]}\right),
$$

where $\Delta s^{\text{index}}$ is the change in the index spread (CDX or a cash index), $\beta_i$ is the bond's empirical spread beta to it (Chapter 8 builds this properly, and shows that for CCCs the analytical duration badly overstates the true sensitivity), and $\Delta y^{\text{TSY}}$ is the matched-maturity Treasury yield change. The bracket is the systematic price return predicted by the market moves, so subtracting it leaves the idiosyncratic part. The IDO markout is the markout computed against $m^{\text{IDO}}$. A client who sells you a bond the day before the whole market rallies is not informed; a client who sells you a bond the day before *only that issuer* gaps down is.

### 3.3.4 Counterparty scores, shrunk

> **Math, Gently.** *Shrinkage, or borrowing strength.* In the second week of April a hitter with four hits in his first eight at-bats is batting .500. Nobody believes he will finish the season at .500; the sensible forecast is much closer to the league's usual .250, nudged up a little for the hot start. That nudge-not-leap is *shrinkage*: when an individual estimate rests on very little data, pull it toward the average of the group, and pull harder the less data there is and the more alike the members of the group really are. The individual "borrows strength" from everyone else. The weight on the individual's own average is $n/(n + \sigma^2/\tau^2)$, where $n$ is how many observations it has, $\sigma^2$ is how noisy each observation is, and $\tau^2$ is how much true individuals differ. Tiny number: if a single fill's markout wobbles by 120bp and true clients differ by 30bp, then $\sigma^2/\tau^2 = 120^2/30^2 = 16$, and a client with four fills averaging $+200$bp gets a weight of $4/(4 + 16) = 0.2$ and a shrunk score of $+40$bp; only at sixteen fills do its own record and the crowd get an equal say. The estimator is next, and Exercise 6 runs the desk's harsher numbers.

You want a per-counterparty toxicity score to feed into $\pi(x)$. The naive estimate, the average IDO markout over that counterparty's fills, is noisy: a counterparty with three fills and an average of $-300$bp is probably unlucky, not informed. Treat the true counterparty effect as a random effect, $\theta_c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$, observed through $n_c$ fills with per-fill noise variance $\sigma^2_{\text{MO}}$. The posterior mean is the classic empirical-Bayes shrinkage:

$$
\hat\theta_c = \frac{n_c}{n_c + \sigma^2_{\text{MO}}/\tau^2}\;\overline{\text{MO}}_c .
$$

Estimate $\tau^2$ by the method of moments across counterparties: the cross-sectional variance of the counterparty means minus the average sampling variance $\sigma^2_{\text{MO}}/n_c$, floored at zero. When $\tau^2 = 0$ every counterparty is shrunk to zero, which is the correct answer when your data cannot tell them apart. Andersen and Bondarenko (2014) are the cautionary tale for the equity version of this exercise: flow-toxicity metrics can look predictive purely because of how they are built, so any score must be validated out of sample against markouts it was not fit on.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.120:* "If a trader were temporarily long inventory of a particular bond, then the trader might quote the bond with a tighter spread (i.e., a higher offered spread) to entice a bid, and vice versa."
>
> **Annotation.** Ben Dor's concern is that inventory-driven quotes make a bond look more liquid than it is. The markout analyst should worry about the mirror image: the dealer's own inventory contaminates the dealer's own markouts. A desk that is long and skewing its offer down to get out will sell at prices that look aggressive, and the subsequent markouts will look good because the price was set below where the market would otherwise clear. Conversely, a desk that is short and bidding up to cover will buy at prices that guarantee poor markouts against clients who did nothing wrong. Before you attribute a markout to the counterparty, control for your own inventory skew at the time of the quote, the $\tfrac12\gamma\sigma_{\text{IDO}}^2(2q + Q)$ term of (3.2). Otherwise the toxicity score partly measures your own position.

### 3.3.5 Survivorship: only fills have markouts

The markout sample is adversely selected by construction. You only observe markouts on RFQs you won, and you win disproportionately when your quote is the most generous, which is disproportionately when you are wrong. The fix comes from Fermanian, Guéant and Pu's outcome taxonomy: every RFQ ends as **Done** (you traded), **Covered** (your quote was second best), **Traded Away** (someone else won and you were not second), or **Not Traded**. Platforms typically report the cover price to the winner and, for some protocols, enough information to know you were covered. Compute *counterfactual* markouts for Covered and Traded Away RFQs from your own quote. If the RFQs you lost have systematically better counterfactual markouts than the ones you won, you are not unlucky: your bids are winning precisely the hands you should fold.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** (1) **Stale mids.** A bond that has not printed in ten days has a composite mid that is a model. Its markout is the error of your model against someone else's model. Flag markouts whose reference mid has no trade within the horizon and down-weight them. (2) **Mixed units.** Price markouts and spread markouts are not interchangeable across bonds with different durations; a 10bp spread markout on a 2-year bond and on a 10-year bond are different amounts of money. Store both, report price bp. (3) **Size-weighting.** A \$50k odd lot and a \$15mm block are not the same observation. Weight by notional, or model them separately. (4) **Your own footprint.** At the one-day horizon, the mid partly reflects your own hedging and unwind. A desk that dumps a block into the street and then measures its markout has measured its own market impact. (5) **Label leakage.** If $\pi(x)$ is fit on 20-day markouts and then evaluated on the same RFQs, the toxicity feature will look magnificent. Purge by time (Chapter 7).

**The hand, replayed.** Here is Theo's Friday with five days of hindsight *(illustrative numbers)*. Mid at the time was 87. The winner bought at 85.75, a captured half-spread of 1.25 points. The lender call on Monday went badly, and by the following Friday the mid was 81: a raw drift of $-6$ points. Over the same days CDX widened 20bp and Treasuries rallied 10bp. With $\beta = 1.3$, $D^{\text{spr}} = 3.5$ and $D^{\text{mod}} = 3.6$, the systematic move was about $87 \times (-3.5 \times 1.3 \times 0.0020 + 3.6 \times 0.0010) \approx -0.48$ points. So the IDO drift was about $-5.5$ points, and the winner's IDO markout was roughly $1.25 - 5.5 = -4.3$ points, or about $-500$bp of price. Theo's counterfactual markout at 85.5 would have been about $-4.0$ points. He lost the auction and won the hand. One hand is an anecdote, not a hand history; the hand history is the next five thousand, which is why §3.8 builds the engine. Figure 3.3 lays the arithmetic out as a waterfall.

#### Quant-Shop Life: A Market Maker, Tuesday, 7:40 a.m.

*(A fictional composite of a credit market-making desk. The routines rest on the public accounts and rules cited; the people, the firm and the numbers are invented and illustrative.)*

The markout report reaches Theo's inbox at 7:12 every morning, produced by a job that runs at 6:30 and that nobody on the desk has opened since it was written. On the third Tuesday after the Brambleworth hand he reads it over coffee, the way other people read the weather, and stops on the second line. The desk's five-day IDO markout for the previous week, the number he has started quoting in the morning meeting as though it were his own blood pressure, has moved overnight from $-38$bp to $-51$bp.

Nothing traded overnight. He checks. The blotter after Monday's close is empty. The fills in the report are the same 212 fills, at the same prices, at the same times. What moved is the other side of the subtraction: the reference mid.

He walks it over to the desk developer at the end of the row, a former physicist eating a granola bar with the calm of someone who already knows. "Composite vendor," she says before he has finished the sentence. "They restated a column overnight. Their high-yield mids for last Wednesday and Thursday were built off a stale contributor feed. They sent an email at two a.m." She turns her screen. The email is three paragraphs long, polite and specific, written in the tone of a company that has written this email before. On an episode of Jane Street's *Signals and Threads* podcast (Ep 29, 2 September 2026), Eric Mannes describes the same genre of correspondence: data vendors who, after the fact, "tell you, whoops, made a mistake." He is not talking about credit, and the email reads the same in every asset class.

"So our markouts got worse because their mids got better," Theo says.

"Our markouts got *different*. Whether they got better depends on which mid you think we could have known about on Wednesday." It is not a philosophical point. Recompute last week's markouts against restated mids and you are grading Wednesday's decisions with Tuesday's information. Mannes's word for what that kind of back-test needs is "a time machine"; his firm's answer, he says, is to save "all the data ... along with the time we received it." The desk developer has been arguing for the same thing for a year. The morning report does not do it: it joins each fill to the vendor's current version of the horizon-date mid, whatever that version happens to be.

There is a second reason last week's numbers move, and it is duller. TRACE requires a dealer to report a corporate-bond trade "as soon as practicable, but no later than within 15 minutes of the Time of Execution" (FINRA Rule 6730), with trades done before 8:00 a.m. ET or in the last fifteen minutes of the session rolling to the next open. Reports that miss the window arrive later, flagged *as/of*, together with cancels and corrections. Every as/of print in a bond the desk traded can move the reference mid for a horizon that has already passed. A markout is therefore not a number; it is a number as of a date. The operations analyst who clears the TRACE exception queue every morning, one of the many people on a market-making floor whose work never appears in a recruiting brochure, knows this better than any trader on the desk.

By nine there are three numbers for last week: the one Theo quoted yesterday ($-38$bp), the one in this morning's report ($-51$bp), and one computed against mids *as received at the time* ($-40$bp), which the desk developer produces by noon from a table she has been quietly maintaining in a side schema. Only the third answers the question a markout exists to answer: given what we could see when we quoted, were we right? The second answers a different and also useful question, how the bonds actually did. The first is simply out of date.

The afternoon brings the model-risk part of the day, which arrives as a form. Twice last week Theo overrode the quoting engine, skewing his bid on a retailer's bonds a quarter-point below the model because he had read an 8-K the engine had not. The firm's model-risk policy, like the bank guidance it borrows from (SR 11-7), treats overrides as data: where model output is "ignored, altered, or reversed," the guidance says a bank "should evaluate the reasons for overrides and track and analyze override performance." So each override is a row with a reason code and a free-text field, and every quarter someone in model validation will set the markouts of Theo's overrides beside the markouts of the model he overrode. He fills in the free-text field more carefully than anything he has written since his last fairness opinion.

None of this is interesting, and all of it is the job. At 4:30 an alert fires on the markout dashboard ("IDO markout drift > 2σ"), and before anyone looks at it, it clears. The desk developer does not look up. On the same podcast (Ep 20, 7 October 2024), the production engineer Liora Friedberg describes a tool that engineers built to measure what share of alerts were actually acted on and how many simply "close themselves." Tomorrow, the desk developer says, she will open a ticket to count how many of theirs do.

The payoff of the whole Tuesday is one line of specification. The engine in §3.8 should read every reference mid as received, stamped with the time it was received, never the vendor's latest restatement, because a markout is computed against what the desk could have known. It costs one column and one join. It is the difference between a hand history and a rewritten one.

## 3.4 The Bayesian 8-K: Updating a Default Distribution in Four Business Days (or Four Minutes)

### Four Business Days: SVB Financial Group, March 2023

*(The filings, dates and figures in this scene come from SVB Financial Group's Forms 8-K and its 2022 Form 10-K on EDGAR, the Federal Reserve's April 2023 review of the bank's supervision, and the press reports named in the text. The desk, the drill and every line of dialogue are fictional.)*

Every new hire on Ines's desk gets the same homework on their fourth weekend, and Theo's arrives, on the Saturday after the Brambleworth hand, as a folder with a sticky note on the front: *Read in order. Do not open a document before its filing time. Bid on Monday.* Inside are the 2022 Form 10-K of SVB Financial Group, the holding company of Silicon Valley Bank, filed on 24 February 2023, and the company's 8-Ks from the following month, each stamped with its filing date. There is nothing else: no price chart, no news, no hindsight. The drill is to be a dealer in the holding company's bonds in March 2023 with only what the public could read at each moment, and to say, at a stated time on a stated morning, what you would bid.

"I know how this ends," Theo says.

"You know how the bank ends," says Ines. "You don't know how the bonds end. Neither did anyone pricing them."

**Wednesday, 8 March.** The first 8-K reads like a hundred Theo has seen. The company has sold about \$21bn of its available-for-sale securities at an after-tax loss of about \$1.8bn, and it is raising \$2.25bn of capital to fill the hole: \$1.25bn of common stock, \$500mm of mandatory convertible preferred, and \$500mm of common from General Atlantic in a private placement. The Federal Reserve's review would later note that the company also disclosed that Moody's and S&P were considering negative rating actions. To a restructuring banker this is a balance-sheet repositioning with a capital raise attached, the kind of thing a company does precisely so that it never needs a restructuring banker. Theo writes "repositioning, funded" in the margin.

Kaz, invited to play the quant, has done what the quant does: run a structural model on the numbers as they stood before the announcement. "The market value of the equity covered the \$3.3bn of holding-company senior notes about 4.8 times. A one-year Merton default probability on those inputs is of order one in ten million." They hold up a sheet with a number in scientific notation *(approx.; a back-of-envelope calculation on pre-event inputs: asset value about \$19bn, asset volatility 33%, a 4.5% rate)*. "The notes are riskless. Let's just assume Gaussian."

"Keep that sheet," says Ines. "You'll want it for the post-mortem."

**Thursday, 9 March.** The shares close at \$106.04, down 60.4% on the day. Theo is allowed that, because it was public. Ines then pulls one page out of the folder and turns it face down. "This one you don't get." It is the Federal Reserve's review, published seven weeks later, recording that over \$40bn of deposits left the bank that Thursday and that management told supervisors to expect more than \$100bn to follow on Friday, which together would have taken most of the \$173bn of deposits the bank reported at year-end. "Nobody outside the building had it on Thursday night. The tape had it by Friday morning, in the form of a share price. The model never had it at all, because the model was about asset value, and this was about who could ask for their money back."

**Friday, 10 March.** California's banking regulator closes Silicon Valley Bank and appoints the FDIC as receiver. The holding company's 8-K that day is short, and one sentence in it does all the work: "The Company is no longer the parent company of the Bank." The equity offerings of Wednesday are terminated. Theo reads the sentence three times. In RSSG terms it is a deconsolidation event, and it changes the bonds from a claim on a bank group into a claim on whatever the holding company still owns once its largest asset has walked out of the door.

He knows what to do with that, and it is the first moment in four weeks that he has felt faster than everyone else in the room. The notes are senior unsecured obligations of SVB Financial Group alone, seven tranches issued under one 2010 indenture, \$3.3bn in all, and the holding company's right to share in a subsidiary's liquidation comes after the claims of the subsidiary's creditors. The bank's creditors include \$173.1bn of year-end deposits, 94% of them uninsured, and \$15.0bn of Federal Home Loan Bank advances. Whatever the bank is worth, the holding company's bondholders are behind all of that. So the bank's equity is not the asset. The asset is everything else.

**Saturday, 11 March.** Theo spends the morning in the back of the 10-K, in the condensed parent-company-only balance sheet that restructuring bankers read and almost nobody else does. At 31 December 2022 the holding company on its own had \$19,679mm of assets, of which \$15,456mm was its investment in the bank, now gone to the receiver. That left about \$4.2bn, of which \$2,258mm was cash and equivalents and the rest was stakes in the non-bank subsidiaries (SVB Capital, SVB Securities and the rest) and other holdings. Against that it owed \$3,370mm of long-term debt. He divides: cash over debt is 67 cents on the dollar, before anything is sold. He writes FLOOR: 67 on a sticky note and puts it on his monitor, and he is still looking at it with something like affection when Ines walks past.

"Where is the cash?"

"On the balance sheet."

"Where is it *deposited*?"

He looks at the note again. A bank holding company's cash has to sit somewhere, and the natural place for it to sit is at the bank. The 10-K's condensed statement does not say which bank. It does not have to, because nobody writing a 10-K in February imagines the bank being seized in March. If the cash was on deposit at Silicon Valley Bank, it is no longer cash; it is a claim against the FDIC as receiver, a claim that the FDIC may net against claims of its own. Six weeks later Debtwire would report that the FDIC had frozen the roughly \$2bn the holding company had on deposit at the failed bank. On Saturday, Theo only has the question. He crosses out FLOOR and writes *claim?* above it, which is less satisfying and more correct.

**Sunday, 12 March.** The Treasury, the Federal Reserve and the FDIC announce a systemic-risk exception: every depositor of the bank will be made whole. Kaz reads the statement and becomes the voice of the prior. "They protected the depositors. Nobody protected the holding company. The bondholders are explicitly not the people this statement is for. The prior should be close to zero."

Mags does not look up. "Back in my day there was a bank called Washington Mutual. September 2008. The regulators seize the bank and sell it to JPMorgan the same night, and the holding company is left holding a pile of claims and a lot of lawyers. Everybody said the holdco bonds were zero. The holdco came out of bankruptcy in 2012 and the bondholders got par plus interest." She lets it sit. "Price is what you pay. Recovery is what you get, eventually, after the lawyers. Sometimes the lawyers are good."

"One precedent is not a base rate," says Kaz.

"One precedent is a state," says Mags. "You don't need a base rate to know the state exists. You need one to know what it weighs."

That is the argument of §3.1 conducted in the vocabulary of a trading floor, and Theo, who has spent Saturday building the low state, realises that Kaz is pricing one hump of the distribution and Mags the other.

**Monday, 13 March.** The holding company files two 8-Ks. The first, under Item 8.01, says its board has formed a restructuring committee of five independent directors (Eric Benhamou, Tom King, Kay Matthews, Mary Miller and Kate Mitchell) to explore strategic alternatives for SVB Capital, SVB Securities and its other assets, and to address the "approximately \$3 billion of funded debt held by the holding company," which, the filing notes, "is recourse only to the Company and is not guaranteed by the Company's subsidiaries." It has hired Centerview Partners as financial adviser, Sullivan & Cromwell as legal counsel and Alvarez & Marsal as restructuring adviser. The second, under Item 5.02, reports that the committee "appointed William Kosturos of A&M as the Company's Chief Restructuring Officer."

Mags points out that Kosturos had held the same title at Washington Mutual, and laughs once, which is the most emotion anyone on the desk has seen from her since the 2020 lockdown.

#### Decision point: 9:02 a.m., Monday 13 March 2023

Ines sets the clock. It is 9:02 a.m. on Monday, 13 March 2023. Assume the restructuring-committee 8-K has been public for thirty-five minutes *(EDGAR accepted it at 8:27 a.m.; the Item 5.02 filing naming the CRO came only at 4:59 p.m. that Monday, so the drill moves that news into the morning)*. A sell RFQ arrives: **client sells \$10mm, SVB Financial Group 1.800% senior notes due October 2026**, the \$650mm tranche. Eight dealers in competition. Two minutes on the clock. The desk's composite mid, for the purpose of the drill, is 40 *(illustrative, not the actual print)*.

"Three questions," says Ines. "Same three as always. What is your prior? What is the likelihood ratio of 'restructuring committee plus three named advisers plus a CRO who did WaMu'? And what do you bid, and why?"

Theo starts with the prior, and the first thing he notices is that the question has changed shape. On the Friday of the Brambleworth hand the open question was whether the company would default. Here default is not in doubt: the holding company has lost its bank, and a Chapter 11 filing is a matter of days. The state variable is not *default or survive* but *which recovery*. A chaotic liquidation in which the deposit claim is lost and the non-bank subsidiaries are sold in a hurry is one hump. An orderly process in which the deposit comes back and the businesses are sold properly, perhaps even WaMu's par-plus-interest, is the other. So his "prior" is a weight on the good hump, and Kaz's near-zero and Mags's WaMu are both claims about that weight.

The 8-K is the evidence, and its likelihood ratio is the question of how much more often a filing like this one (an independent committee, a named financial adviser, a major law firm, a restructuring firm and a chief restructuring officer with holding-company experience, all within one business day of the bank's failure) appears in the orderly-recovery world than in the chaotic one. Theo thinks it is well above one. A board does not hire Centerview, Sullivan & Cromwell and Alvarez & Marsal over a weekend to run a fire sale; it hires them to run a process, and a process is what turns a pile of assets into a recovery. The committee's mandate names the businesses it means to sell. Kaz objects that every failing holding company hires advisers, so the filing is close to universal in both worlds and the likelihood ratio is near one. That is a real objection, and it is the base-rate argument of §3.4.3 in miniature: a likelihood ratio fitted across all companies that hire restructuring advisers is not the likelihood ratio for a holding company whose bank has just been seized.

Then Ines asks the question that is always hers. "Every one of these filings is public. So why is someone selling ten million at 9:02, and what does that tell you?"

Theo turns it over. Someone who had read every 8-K and done the Saturday arithmetic, and who believed the good hump was heavier than the price implied, would not be selling; they would be buying. The seller at 9:02 is more likely to be a holder that is not allowed to own what these bonds have become: an investment-grade mandate whose guidelines force it out of a defaulted or downgraded name, a fund facing redemptions, a risk committee that wants the line gone before lunch. That seller's reasons live on its own balance sheet, like the insurer's in §3.2.1. If that is right, the $\pi$ in Theo's bid is small, even though the uncertainty $\sigma_V$ is enormous.

"So the informed money is on my side," he says slowly. "The people who know more than me aren't sending the RFQ. They're waiting to buy from whoever wins it."

"Then what's the risk in the bid?"

"The winner's curse. Eight dealers, a huge $\sigma_V$, and the dealer who has thought about this least might be the one who bids highest." He pauses. "And the risk that I'm wrong about who the seller is."

Ines nods, which is the closest she comes to praise, and stops the clock without asking for the number. The number is §3.4.2's job.

#### The coda, in filings

The rest of the month arrived as Theo now knows it did. On Tuesday, 14 March, an 8-K under Item 2.01 set out the securities sale of the previous Wednesday: a book value of about \$23.97bn sold to Goldman Sachs for net proceeds of about \$21.45bn, a loss of roughly 10.5% of book on securities that had been thought of as the safe part of the balance sheet. On Friday, 17 March, the holding company filed for Chapter 11 in the Southern District of New York, case No. 23-10367. On 22 March an 8-K under Item 2.04 recorded that the filing had accelerated all seven tranches of senior notes, \$3.3bn of principal. On 28 March Nasdaq suspended the shares. By late April, Debtwire was reporting the 25 April prints of 69 and 57 that opened §3.1.1, a trader's remark that "a bunch of hedge funds bought these bonds in the 30s," and, citing the *Wall Street Journal*, that PIMCO, Centerbridge and Davidson Kempner had formed a group and retained PJT Partners as financial adviser.

Theo reads the last line twice. He was at PJT in 2023, working on other cases; he learned about the mandate the way the rest of the market did, from the press, and the company's November 2024 announcement would name PJT Partners as financial adviser to the Ad Hoc Group of Senior Noteholders. The fact is plain enough. Within weeks of the drill, some of the holders most likely to have done the Saturday arithmetic were reported to be organising on the *buy* side, and his old firm was their adviser. Whichever dealer bought ten million from a forced seller that Monday was likely to find its exit among buyers like these.

The recovery took longer than any trader's horizon. In early July 2024, after the IRS agreed to cut one tax claim from about \$649mm to \$43mm, the 1.8% notes due 2026 rose as much as 2.625 points to 63.625 cents (TRACE, as reported by Bloomberg's Jonathan Randles on 2 July 2024), and press reports put the expected recovery under the plan then out for a vote, for bondholders and other creditors owed \$3.5bn, at roughly 40% or better. The bankruptcy court confirmed the plan on 2 August 2024. It went effective on 7 November 2024, moving the estate's assets into a liquidating trust and paying senior noteholders in new common stock and trust units rather than cash, which is why no one can yet quote a final recovery to the cent. The deposit claim, the cash Theo had called a floor on Saturday, was litigated. In late August 2026, after a twelve-day bench trial, a federal judge in the Northern District of California ruled that the trust could not recover its \$1.71bn claim against the FDIC (American Banker, 9 September 2026). The floor had been a claim after all, and the claim lost.

"So who was right?" Theo asks. "Kaz, at zero, or Mags, at par?"

"Neither," says Ines. "Funds bought in the 30s, and sixteen months later the 2026s printed above 63 with the payout still to come in stock and trust units. The people who bought that week were paid for bearing a distribution with two humps and years of lawyers in it. That's the whole business." She takes the folder back. "Now do the arithmetic properly."

What Theo did with the folder is what an RSSG analyst does with every 8-K: read it not for what it says, but for what the company's lawyers were forced to say. "Entry into a Material Definitive Agreement" can mean a new customer contract or a forbearance agreement with the lenders, and the difference is on page three of Exhibit 10.1. The desk version of that skill is a likelihood-ratio table, a parser that fires within minutes of the filing hitting EDGAR, and a Bayes update that moves the default distribution by the right amount, not by the amount the headline suggests. The next three subsections build those pieces in turn, and §3.4.2 finishes the drill. Figure 3.4 first lays the fortnight out as the filings arrived.

### 3.4.1 The 8-K as a set of signals

Form 8-K requires a current report "to be filed or furnished within four business days" of a triggering event (Form 8-K, General Instruction B.1). The items that matter most to a credit desk:

| Item | Title (abridged) | Why the desk cares |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1.01 / 1.02 | Entry into / termination of a material definitive agreement | Forbearance agreements, amendments, cooperation-agreement-adjacent deals, RSAs |
| 1.03 | Bankruptcy or receivership | The event itself; by now the price already knows |
| 2.03 | Creation of a direct financial obligation | New money, including priming facilities raised out of court |
| 2.04 | Triggering events that accelerate or increase a direct financial obligation | Acceleration, cross-default; the item requires the amount and terms of the acceleration |
| 2.06 | Material impairments | Asset values in the recovery waterfall just moved |
| 3.01 | Notice of delisting | Equity cushion signal; often precedes a restructuring |
| 4.02 | Non-reliance on previously issued financial statements | The covenant compliance certificate you modeled may be fiction |
| 5.02 | Departure of directors or certain officers | CFO departures before a liquidity event are a classic tell |
| 7.01 / 8.01 | Regulation FD / other events | Catch-alls; cleansing disclosures after lender negotiations often land here |

Run SVB Financial's March through the rows and the table stops being a taxonomy. Item 1.03 lit first, on 10 March, for the receivership, and by the time anyone could read it the price already knew. Monday's two filings came under the catch-all 8.01 (the committee) and under 5.02, which was not a departure but an appointment, and for a credit desk the louder of the two: a CFO who leaves before a liquidity event is a tell; a CRO who arrives after one is a confession. The next day's Item 2.01, a row the table leaves out because it usually announces a deal nobody in credit cares about, gave the most precise mark anyone outside the company had yet seen on the balance sheet's hidden loss (the coda has the numbers). Items 1.03 and 3.01 came back together on 17 March, a week after the cushion had gone, and the 22 March Item 2.04 was the first 8-K from which a parser could have pulled the whole debt stack without reaching for the back of the 10-K. Read in order, the items describe a company moving from "we have a problem" to "here is the bill," and the likelihood ratio of each one depends on where in that sequence it lands. A 5.02 appointing a CRO in February would have been news. A 1.03 on 17 March was a formality.

Two details trip parsers. Items 2.02 and 7.01 are **furnished**, not filed, which changes the liability attached to them and, in practice, how carefully they are drafted. And companies do not always put content where the taxonomy says they should: material acceleration language can turn up under 8.01, which is why the parser reads the text and not just the item number.

Kaz, reading over Theo's shoulder, finds a "springing lien" in an exhibit and asks whether the lien is seasonal. Mags answers without looking up: "It springs into existence when the borrower trips a trigger, a downgrade or a leverage test. Like a mousetrap, except the mouse signed it and paid the lawyers."

### 3.4.2 The update

Default runs here on the exponential clock of Chapter 1's box (§1.5): a bus with no timetable, whose rate $\lambda$ is all you know, so that the chance it has still not come after $T$ years is $e^{-\lambda T}$ (§6.6 lets the rate move). Let the prior probability of default within horizon $T$ (in years) come from a physical hazard $\lambda$, for example an EDF-style model (Chapter 6):

$$
p = 1 - e^{-\lambda T} .
$$

An 8-K event $E$ has a likelihood ratio

$$
\text{LR}_E = \frac{\mathbb{P}(E \mid \tau \le T)}{\mathbb{P}(E \mid \tau > T)},
$$

the ratio of how often filings like this one appear before defaults to how often they appear at companies that do not default within $T$. Bayes' rule in odds form gives the posterior, and inverting the survival relationship gives the posterior hazard:

$$
\frac{p'}{1-p'} = \text{LR}_E \cdot \frac{p}{1-p}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p' = \frac{\text{LR}_E\,p}{1 - p + \text{LR}_E\,p}, \qquad
\lambda' = -\frac{1}{T}\ln(1 - p') . \tag{3.3}
$$

Translating the hazard move into a spread move requires leaving $\mathbb{P}$. The filing moves the physical hazard, but the spread is priced on the risk-neutral one, and the two differ for a reason of timing: defaults cluster in recessions, when a dollar of loss hurts most, so the market charges more for default than a sober forecaster expects, the way an insurance premium exceeds the expected claim (the boxes are in §1.5 and §5.1.1; §6.3 measures the gap). The credit triangle says $s \approx L\,\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}$, where $L = 1 - R$ is loss given default. Write $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}} = \kappa\,\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ with $\kappa > 1$ a time-varying ratio; then

$$
\Delta s \approx L\,\kappa\,(\lambda' - \lambda) .
$$

The lazy version, which lets the filing move the risk-neutral hazard one for one, quietly sets $\kappa = 1$ and understates the move by that factor.

**Worked example** *(illustrative)*. A B3 issuer has a one-year physical PD of 6%, so $\lambda = -\ln(0.94) \approx 0.0619$. It files an 8-K under Item 1.01 attaching a forbearance agreement, and the desk's calibrated likelihood ratio for that event type in this rating bucket is 2.5. Prior odds $0.06/0.94 = 0.0638$; posterior odds $0.1596$; $p' \approx 13.8\%$; $\lambda' = -\ln(1 - 0.1376) \approx 0.148$. With $L = 0.6$ and $\kappa = 2$, the implied spread move is $0.6 \times 2 \times 0.0862 \approx 0.103$, about 1,030bp. That sounds enormous, and for a B3 that just told the world it needed its lenders' permission to keep operating, it is about right. The trading question is not whether the spread should move 1,030bp; it is how much of that move was already in the price before the filing, which is a question about who knew first (§3.6).

**Back to the drill.** The same machinery prices Theo's SVB Monday, with one change of variable. At SVB the holding company's default was a matter of days, so the uncertain state was not *default or survive* but *which recovery*. Put the two humps at about 25 (a chaotic liquidation in which the deposit claim is lost) and about 65 (an orderly sale of the businesses with the deposit returned), all *(illustrative)*. The drill's composite of 40 then implies a weight of 0.375 on the good hump, since $25 + 0.375 \times 40 = 40$. Suppose Theo's own prior weight before the 13 March 8-K is 0.25 and his likelihood ratio for "restructuring committee, three named advisers and a CRO who has done this before" is 2. The odds go from 1:3 to 2:3, the weight to 0.4, and his posterior mean to $25 + 0.4 \times 40 = 41$. Kaz, starting near zero (say 0.05) and treating the filing as uninformative, sits at 27; Mags, starting at even odds, sits at 45. Now the seller. If Theo believes the 9:02 seller is forced rather than informed, say $\pi = 0.1$, the asymmetric Glosten–Milgrom charge with $q = 0.4$ and $\Delta = 40$ is $40 \times 0.24 \times 0.1 / (0.45 + 0.06) \approx 1.9$ points, and his bid before any winner's-curse shading is about 39. The fourteen points between Kaz's mean and Theo's are the price of reading the 8-K as evidence and of starting from a different prior; the two points of charge are the price of the seller; and the winner's curse in an eight-dealer auction with a forty-point gap is the reason to shade further. Which of them was right is not settled by where the bonds went that week. It is settled, as §3.3 says, by the hand history of many Mondays like it.

**Where the edge is, and is not.** The 8-K is public. By the time your parser fires, the fastest readers have read it. The edge is in two places: parse latency (minutes, not hours, for an exhibit buried in a 300-page PDF) and the calibration of $\text{LR}_E$, which requires a labeled corpus and, crucially, conditioning on the issuer. The phrase "liability management transaction" in an investment-grade telecom's 8-K usually describes a routine tender offer. The same phrase in a sponsor-backed B3's 8-K usually means somebody is about to be primed. The likelihood ratio of a token is conditional on the issuer's rating and its Priming Risk Score, which is why a bag-of-words model trained across the whole EDGAR universe is worse than useless for distressed names: it averages the LR of AT&T with the LR of a company whose lenders just hired restructuring counsel.

> **Trend Watch.** **"Liability management" goes mainstream in the 8-K corpus (2019–2026).** An EDGAR full-text search for the exact phrase "liability management transaction" in Form 8-K filings (queried October 17, 2026; counts are documents, including exhibits) returns 4 hits in 2019, 9 in 2020, 23 in 2021, 15 in 2022, 14 in 2023, 55 in 2024, 54 in 2025 and 37 in 2026 through October 16. The number of distinct filers runs 4, 6, 16, 12, 8, 24, 27 and 24. The 2024 step-up coincides with what Wachtell Lipton describes as the high-water mark of 38 LMEs in 2024 (24 in 2025; "Liability Management 2026: For Better or Worse," February 3, 2026). **What it means for the desk:** the filer list mixes investment-grade issuers running routine tender offers with sponsor-backed high-yield issuers running uptiers and drop-downs, so the raw count is a terrible signal and the conditional count is a good one. Condition on rating, sponsor ownership and PRS before you let the token into $\pi(x)$. Kaz's bag-of-words model will learn that "liability management" predicts default, then short every investment-grade issuer that ever ran a tender offer.

SVB's March filings never used the phrase. They said "restructuring committee," and the people who read them fastest were not dealers.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The distressed fund that bought in the 30s.* In the Glosten–Milgrom model of §3.2 the informed trader is an abstraction with a probability attached. In SVB Financial's holding-company notes in March 2023, the informed side of the market had addresses: the funds that, as the drill's coda reports, bought in the 30s and then organised. Its edge was not a secret, because every number it needed was in the 10-K and the 8-Ks. From the dealer's chair that fund is the $\pi$ in Ines's model, pointed the other way. It is not the client sending the sell RFQ; it is the buyer waiting behind whichever dealer wins it. The opportunistic credit seat looks different again. Apollo describes its own platform as a ladder of loan-to-value, with its Credit business at 0–50% LTV, "often more liquid and shorter holds ... dislocation-driven," Hybrid Value at 50–80%, and private equity above that (apollo.com, retrieved 18 October 2026). A dislocation buyer in that sense wants mispriced risk in a structure it can underwrite and, in time, exit. A fund buying a bankrupt holding company's notes in the 30s is a different animal, a vote-counter, whose return depends on its seat in a creditor group and its patience through a plan. Both are informed. Only one of them needs to win a vote to get paid, and a dealer quoting either should know which one it is talking to.

### 3.4.3 The base-rate trap

> **Math, Gently.** *Base rates, or why a good test can give mostly wrong answers.* A disease affects one person in a thousand. A test catches 99% of real cases and wrongly flags 2% of healthy people. You test positive. What is the chance you have the disease? Most people say about 99%. Count instead. Out of 100,000 people, 100 have the disease and 99 of them test positive; 99,900 do not, and 2% of them, about 1,998, test positive anyway. Of the 2,097 positives only 99 are sick, under 5%. The test is good. The disease is rare, and the rarity, the *base rate*, dominates the answer. Default models have the same problem in reverse. They are trained on samples stuffed with defaults (otherwise there would be nothing to learn from), so they learn a base rate far higher than the live book's, and every probability they produce is quietly calibrated to the wrong population. The fix is not to scale the output down in proportion; it is to redo the Bayes update with the right base rate, and Elkan's correction, (3.4), does exactly that.

The likelihood ratios come from a training corpus, and training corpora for default models are built with far more defaults than the live book contains, because otherwise there would be nothing to learn from. A model fit on a sample with default rate $S$ produces probabilities calibrated to $S$, and the live book has default rate $T$. The tempting fix is to scale, $p^{*} = (T/S)\,p$. Bohn and Stein show why this fails and give Elkan's (2001) correction, which in our notation is

$$
p^{*} = \frac{T\,(p - pS)}{S - pS + pT - ST} . \tag{3.4}
$$

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.253:* "This adjustment, in addition to being grounded a bit more rigorously in probability theory, has the attractive property that it can never exceed 1.0."
>
> **Annotation.** On the preceding page Bohn and Stein show that the simple $T/S$ scaling produces probabilities above one whenever the model output exceeds $S/T$, and that it overstates probabilities increasingly as model output rises when the true base rate is above the sample rate. For a desk updating PDs from 8-Ks, that is not an academic nicety. A stressed-name model with a sample default rate of 2% deployed into a 2025–2026 book where the all-in default rate including distressed exchanges was running several times its ex-DE level will, with naive scaling, assign probabilities above one to the names you most need to price. The modern wrinkle Bohn and Stein did not face is that $T$ itself depends on the default definition. Moody's reported a June 2025 speculative-grade default rate of 5.8% including distressed exchanges and 2.1% excluding them, so "the" base rate is a modeling choice. Pick the definition that matches the payoff you are pricing: a distressed exchange is a default for a CDS holder and for a left-behind lender, and a non-event for nobody.

#### The Full Derivation: Elkan's correction is a Bayes update

It is worth seeing where (3.4) comes from, because it is the same update as (3.3). Changing the base rate from $S$ to $T$ multiplies the odds by the ratio of prior odds:

$$
\frac{p^{*}}{1-p^{*}} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{T/(1-T)}{S/(1-S)} .
$$

Solve for $p^{*}$, multiply through by $S(1-S)$, and the denominator collects to $pT(1-S) + (1-p)(1-T)S = S - pS + pT - ST$. The base-rate correction *is* a likelihood-ratio update, applied to the whole model at once. The scaling shortcut is its first-order approximation for small $p$, which is why it works for IG and breaks for exactly the names this chapter cares about.

Three columns carry the economics, and Theo learns which three on the Monday of the SVB drill, when Ines makes him fill in a Ledger row for the holding company's notes as of 9:02 a.m. He starts with what he had by Saturday. The parent-only balance sheet, cash against debt, is a "finding the fulcrum" feature: built from the 10-K, refreshed quarterly, with a **decay horizon** of months, because it takes the rest of the market a while to do and it stays true until the next balance sheet. That horizon decides where the feature lives in the stack. A feature with a half-life of months belongs in the portfolio model and can be refreshed overnight; Theo's Saturday arithmetic was a portfolio view, not a quote input. The restructuring-committee 8-K is a "knowing who the advisers are" feature, and its decay horizon is the opposite: days at best and minutes in practice, because every news terminal has it within hours. A feature with a half-life of days belongs in the RFQ engine's $\pi(x)$ and must be computed in real time, which is why Theo's likelihood ratio for the filing mattered at 9:02 and would have been worth almost nothing by lunch. Then comes the **refresh** column, which decides how stale a feature can be when it is used and is the source of half the bugs in production credit models. Theo's balance sheet was dated 31 December 2022, and by 11 March 2023 its largest line had been seized. The column said "quarterly"; the world had refreshed it on a Friday afternoon. A stale feature with a confident number in it is worse than a missing one. Last comes **who else has it**, which decides how much the feature is worth after costs. By Monday morning Theo has to assume that the deposit question Ines asked on Saturday is being asked on every distressed desk in the market, and within weeks a group of holders that had answered it had organised and hired an adviser. The feature was still correct. It was no longer an edge.

An edge cools like coffee left on the desk, losing a fixed fraction of what is left in each stretch of time rather than a fixed amount (the half-life box is in §1.8.1), so a hot signal and a slow one can trade places; Exercise 4 finds the day they do. To make the decay column operational, model the information coefficient as a function of horizon $h$ with half-life $h_{1/2}$:

$$
\text{IC}(h) = \text{IC}_0 \cdot 2^{-h/h_{1/2}} .
$$

A feature used at horizon $h$ earns $\text{IC}(h)$, not $\text{IC}_0$. That is why Theo's Saturday arithmetic and Monday's filing could not simply be added: features with different native horizons only compare once their forecasts sit on a common clock, which Paleologo spells out for equities and which applies unchanged to bonds.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Paleologo_Advanced_Portfolio_Management p.90:* "whenever forecasts for different stocks are at different horizons, they should be adjusted so that longer-horizon forecasts get adjusted downward compared to short-horizon ones"
>
> **Annotation.** Paleologo's procedure divides each forecast's expected return by its horizon, $\alpha = r/T$, so that a 20% return expected over a year and a 5% return expected over a month are compared as rates. In the Edge Ledger, the fulcrum analysis that says "this second lien is worth 40 in a plan eighteen months from now" and the flow signal that says "this second lien will be two points lower by Friday" are both alpha, and they cannot be added until they share a clock. The ex-RSSG reader's characteristic error is to treat the long-horizon view as the important one because it is the one that took ninety hours to build. Per unit of time, the five-day flow signal is often larger, and it is the one that tells you whether to bid for the paper today. The modern desk departs from Paleologo in one way: credit forecasts are horizon-dependent not just in scale but in sign, because the path to a good restructuring outcome often runs through a bad month.

The clock also explains why the RSSG rows last. Simple features decay faster than complex ones, because more people can compute them, and Man's survey of quant credit managers makes the point bluntly.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.30:* "On the one hand, simple models tend to experience faster alpha decay (as we have seen in the equity markets)."
>
> **Annotation.** The sentence continues with the other hand: sophisticated models risk overfitting and a loss of transparency. The Edge Ledger is a way to escape the dilemma rather than split it. The features in the RSSG rows are not complex in the statistical sense. A blocker flag is a single bit. They are complex in the *extraction* sense: computing them requires reading a document that most quant teams cannot parse, or understanding a process that most quant teams have never sat through. That kind of complexity decays slowly, because the barrier to entry is expertise, not compute. It is also exactly the kind of feature that an AI coding agent can help scale once a human has specified it correctly, which is the subject of Chapter 26 and the reason this book teaches you to write specs.

Man's own "Informed Investor" style factor (Man 2022, p.27) and Dynkin's equity-short-interest signal (Dynkin p.794: issuers with the highest short interest within their rating category "are the most likely issuers to be downgraded over the next three to 12 months") are the published, slow versions of the Ledger's flow rows. The Ledger's contribution is to make them fast and conditional: the same short-interest spike means something different for a name with a Serta blocker than for one without.

The decay horizon was how long you held a grudge." She taps the last row. "Stan's card never decayed." A pause. "Fine. Your spreadsheet holds a grudge better than I do. Add a column for who lied."

## 3.6 Who at This Table Knows About the LMT?

Ines's third question, "who at this table knows more than you?", has a specific and expensive answer in leveraged credit: the lenders who are about to do a liability management transaction to the lenders who are not. An LMT is the purest adverse-selection event in the asset class. A majority of a class negotiates privately with the borrower, and on announcement the paper held by the majority and the paper held by everyone else become, economically, different securities. Before announcement they trade at one price.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.228:* "Exchange offers are “coercive” when they compel cooperation by exposing nonparticipants to the risk of being made worse off."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer was writing about exit consents and old-fashioned exchange offers, where the coercion was that holdouts were left with a stripped indenture. The 2020s uptier and drop-down are the same idea with the coercion turned up: nonparticipants are not just stripped of covenants but primed, subordinated to new money and to the participants' exchanged debt. For the desk, "the risk of being made worse off" has a price, and the price is exactly the gap between the inside and outside paper after announcement. Before announcement, a holder who knows the deal is coming knows which side of that gap they will be on. That is $V$ in the Glosten–Milgrom model, and the participating lenders know it.

### 3.6.1 The anatomy of pre-LMT flow

The flow that precedes a non-pro-rata transaction has a recognizable shape, because the transaction needs votes. The shape is easiest to see in a real case told in order, and Saks Global's 11% notes are a recent one in which its four parts arrive as dated events. *(Facts below are from press reports, the company's releases and the bankruptcy record; the desk and its questions are the chapter's.)*

On 23 December 2024 Saks Global bought Neiman Marcus at an enterprise value of \$2.7bn, financed with \$2.2bn of 11.000% senior secured notes due 2029 (Jefferies was the initial purchaser), a \$1.8bn asset-based revolver, \$275mm of seller financing and about \$1.5bn of equity from investors including Amazon, Authentic and Salesforce. Five months later the notes were at 47.5.

**1. Accumulation to a threshold.** On 16 May 2025 Debtwire reported that a group of holders with about 51% of the notes (Lord Abbett, MacKay Shields, Arena and BlackRock were named, with Paul Weiss and Lazard advising) was weighing new financing that could leapfrog the rest of the holders through what the report called "malleable indentures." A \$121mm coupon was due at the end of June, and the flagship store, mortgaged for \$1.25bn, sat in an unrestricted subsidiary. The number that matters in that report is 51. "Required Lenders" under a typical credit agreement means lenders holding more than 50% of the loans; many indenture amendments, including lien releases, need two-thirds; sacred rights need each affected lender. A group that holds 45% and needs 50.1% must buy, and must buy in one tranche, which is why the run-up to a threshold leaves footprints. A group already at 51% has crossed the line that matters for most amendments. Whether it also had two-thirds for anything touching collateral was a question for the documents, not the tape.

**2. The left-behind signature.** What a detector looks for in the weeks before a deal like this is one-sided buying by the counterparty types prone to these deals (distressed and credit-opportunity funds, sponsor-affiliated accounts, prior cooperation-agreement signatories), concentrated in a single tranche, while the issuer's equity and CDS are relatively quiet. The buyers are not betting on the company. They are betting on the vote. Saks shows the limit of the comparison: for a privately owned retailer there is no listed equity tape to be quiet, so the "relatively quiet" half of the signature has nothing to read, and the detector has to lean on the bond flow alone.

**3. Silence where there used to be noise.** Holders who have been wall-crossed into negotiations are restricted. Their usual flow, axes and bids stop, and the absence is informative. This chapter has no Saks flow data and will not invent any. What the detector would have *asked* is simple to state: had the accounts that used to show bids and offers in the 11s stopped showing them in the weeks around 16 May?

**4. Post-announcement dispersion.** On 21 July 2025 the company launched an exchange offer asking holders to fund \$600mm of new notes issued by a special-purpose vehicle. For each \$1,000 of old notes, holders who had pre-committed to fund received \$106 of the new SPV notes plus \$894 of second-out notes; holders who funded new money in the offer received \$550 of second-out plus \$250 of third-out; holders who did neither received \$750 of third-out. About 91.9% had pre-committed, and exit consents stripped the old indenture's covenants. By 8 August about 98% had tendered, and only about \$51mm of old notes was reported to have stayed outside, holding a stripped bond. One tranche had become four securities, and the price of each depended on which door its holder had walked through in July. On announcement, inside and outside paper separate, and the outside paper's drop is the realized adverse selection on every dealer who was long it. The company did not pay more than \$100mm of interest that fell due at the end of December, and Saks Global filed for Chapter 11 in the Southern District of Texas on 13 January 2026. The plan, approved in early June 2026, cut funded debt from about \$3.4bn to about \$1.2bn and wiped out the existing equity.

#### Decision point: the last week of May 2025

Now put the desk in the last week of May 2025, after the Debtwire report and before the exchange offer is public. A client asks for a bid on a block of the Saks 11s and indicates it would sell at 48 *(illustrative)*. Kaz's model sees a bond a half-point above its last reported level. Mags sees a retailer with a coupon due in five weeks and its best asset in an unrestricted subsidiary. Ines sees a seller and asks who it is. The detector, if the desk had one, would not have answered with a number; it would have asked four questions. Is there a holder group above a voting threshold in this tranche? The press says yes, at about 51%. Is this request in the tranche the group holds? Yes; it is the only bond. Is the sender likely to be a group member, who is probably restricted and unlikely to sell, or an outsider, who has every reason to sell before the group decides what to do with the outsiders? And how far away is the next liquidity date? Five weeks. None of those questions says 48 is too high. All of them say that the bid is not a bid for a bond. It is a bid for an unknown allocation among securities that did not yet exist, and the dealer who buys at 48 has to know, before it hits the button, whether it expects to be inside the room in July or outside it.

"The question isn't whether 48 is cheap," says Ines. "It's which of the four securities you'll be holding in August."

Kaz asks the question a model builder always asks. "Would a detector have fired?"

"Not on this one. The group was at 51% before anybody could have looked. A group that already has the votes has nothing left to buy, so a detector of net buying has nothing to see, and it sits quietly through the most dangerous month the bond will ever have. Serta's majority was a bare one too." She taps the Debtwire printout. "When the group already holds the votes, the documents and the press do the work alone."

The informed accumulator is a *buyer*, and that turns the dealer's danger around. A dealer who sells to the group and stays short the outside paper does fine, because the outside paper will fall. The dealer who is hurt is the one who keeps *buying* outside paper from everyone else, building long inventory into the announcement. So the detector's output enters $\pi(x)$ for sell RFQs in the vulnerable tranche, and it raises a separate flag against building long inventory in the paper most likely to be left behind.

### 3.6.2 A sequential detector

Let $y_t$ be day-$t$ net buying of tranche $i$ by counterparties in the LME-prone set, scaled by trailing average daily volume. Under the null of no deal, $y_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_y^2)$ independently; under the alternative of accumulation toward a threshold, $y_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_y^2)$.

> **Math, Gently.** *Two bells, a running tally, and why the evidence is a straight line.* Picture two bell curves for a day's net buying in a tranche, scaled by its average volume. One is centred on zero: the ordinary world, in which buyers and sellers roughly cancel. The other is centred a little to the right, at $\mu_1$: the world in which a group is quietly accumulating. Both have the same width. Each day's number is a vote between the two bells, and the strength of the vote is the log-likelihood ratio, the logarithm of how much more likely that day's number is under the right-hand bell than under the left. Because the bells have the same width, their curved parts cancel when you take the ratio, and what is left is a straight line in $y_t$. A day of buying above $\mu_1/2$, halfway between the two centres, is a vote for accumulation; a day below it is a vote against; and the weight of the vote grows in proportion to the distance. Page's CUSUM is a running tally of those votes with one twist: it never goes below zero. A week of selling does not bank negative evidence against a deal that might start next Monday; the tally forgets the days that pointed the other way and starts climbing again from zero. When the tally crosses a threshold $h$, the alarm rings. Tiny numbers: if accumulation shifts the average day by one standard deviation ($\mu_1 = \sigma_y$) and the threshold is $h = 4$, the alarm rings about eight trading days after accumulation starts, and in a quiet tranche it rings falsely, on average, no more often than once every 55 trading days ($e^4 \approx 55$). The tally is the next equation, and its derivation sits right under it.

Each day's evidence is the log-likelihood ratio $\ell_t$, which, as the Full Derivation shows, is a straight line in the day's buying: $\ell_t = (\mu_1/\sigma_y^2)\,(y_t - \mu_1/2)$. Page's CUSUM accumulates evidence and resets when evidence turns against the alternative:

$$
S_t = \max\!\left(0,\; S_{t-1} + \ell_t\right), \qquad S_0 = 0, \qquad \text{alarm at the first } t \text{ with } S_t > h . \tag{3.5}
$$

#### The Full Derivation: Page's CUSUM

Under the null of no deal and the alternative of accumulation defined at the start of this subsection, the log-likelihood ratio of one day's observation is

$$
\ell_t = \ln\frac{\phi\big((y_t - \mu_1)/\sigma_y\big)}{\phi\big(y_t/\sigma_y\big)} = \frac{\mu_1}{\sigma_y^2}\left(y_t - \frac{\mu_1}{2}\right),
$$

where $\phi$ is the standard normal density; the quadratic terms in $y_t$ cancel, leaving a linear statistic. The tally of these daily ratios, reset at zero, is (3.5). Two properties make (3.5) the right tool. Its false-alarm average run length under the null is at least $e^{h}$ (Lorden, 1971), so a threshold of $h = 4$ gives at least 55 trading days between false alarms on a single tranche, and in practice many more. And its expected detection delay under the alternative is approximately $h / K$, where $K = \mu_1^2 / (2\sigma_y^2)$ is the Kullback–Leibler divergence per day; with $\mu_1 = \sigma_y$ and $h = 4$, about eight trading days. Eight trading days is short relative to the weeks it takes a group to accumulate, document and sign a transaction.

#### The prior: the documents set the fuse

Not every tranche is equally exposed. The Priming Risk Score from Chapter 13, $\text{PRS} \in [0,1]$, is the prior probability that the tranche's documents and holder base permit a non-pro-rata deal. Fold it in as prior log-odds: alarm when $S_t + \operatorname{logit}(\text{PRS}) > h_0$, that is, use the threshold

$$
h(\text{PRS}) = h_0 - \ln\frac{\text{PRS}}{1 - \text{PRS}},
$$

floored at a minimum so a single noisy day can never fire the alarm. A tranche with no blockers, a loose "open market purchase" exception and a concentrated holder base gets a low threshold. A tranche with a Serta blocker gets a high one. This is where the ex-RSSG reader's document knowledge enters the flow model: the PRS is the thing they know how to compute and the quant does not. Figure 3.5 runs one simulated tranche past both thresholds.

The training data has traps of its own. Event dates taken from the day the desk noticed, rather than from the 8-K or the press release, leak hindsight into every label. The LME-prone counterparty set drifts as funds change strategy, which is why it is re-estimated with a lag. And a sample built only from deals that happened has no null in it, because the accumulations that ended in a pro rata deal, or in nothing, are the null hypothesis.

> **Trend Watch.** **Defaults are now mostly negotiated (2024–2026).** Moody's reported that distressed exchanges were 64% of all defaults in the first half of 2025, about the same pace as 2024, "which itself was the highest annual share on record," and that the June 2025 speculative-grade default rate of 5.8% fell to 2.1% once the 51 distressed restructurings were removed ("US Credit Review & Outlook," 7 August 2025). Moody's later put distressed restructurings at roughly 65% of all 2025 defaults ("Lend, extend, and then...," May 18, 2026). Wachtell Lipton counts LMEs falling from a high-water mark of 38 in 2024 to 24 in 2025 while remaining about two-thirds of default activity by issuer count ("Liability Management 2026," February 3, 2026). The aftermath is not benign: in a sample of 1,173 borrowers with soft credit events since 1979, Moody's finds "about one in four soft credit events ultimately ends in hard default," and that more than 70% of eventual hard defaults after a distressed restructuring occur within two years ("Lend, extend, and then...," May 18, 2026). **What it means for the desk:** the pre-LMT state is no longer a once-a-cycle tail; it is a recurring regime that a meaningful slice of the high-yield and loan universe is in at any given time. A market maker in sponsor-backed paper without a pre-LMT detector is a poker player who has not noticed that a third of the table is playing as a team.

### 3.6.3 The information wall is a feature

The detector must only ever use information the desk is allowed to use: public data and its own flow. The SEC has made clear that lender-group membership is an information channel regulators watch. In its settled action against Sound Point Capital Management (August 26, 2024; \$1.8mm civil penalty), the SEC found that the firm regularly participated in lender groups and creditors' committees, sometimes received material non-public information about companies whose loans were in CLOs it traded, and lacked adequate written policies to address that risk. A month later it settled with Marathon Asset Management (September 30, 2024; \$1.5mm penalty) for lacking policies "reasonably designed to address" MNPI risk from ad hoc creditors' committee participation.

Read those orders as a modeling specification. Counterparties *on* a committee should be modeled as restricted: their silence is a feature. Counterparties *not* on the committee who trade the same tranche heavily just before an announcement are the live adverse-selection risk, because they are the ones reading the tape. And the desk itself, the moment it is wall-crossed, stops quoting the name, which is a compliance rule and also, conveniently, the only way to keep $\pi(x)$ honest.

From inside a trading floor the rule looks less dramatic than the SEC orders and comes up far more often. *(The routine described here is a fictional composite of practice on dealer desks; it describes no named firm.)* It starts with a phone call. A banker or a company's adviser calls the desk's designated contact, not the trader, and asks whether the desk would be willing to be "brought over the wall" on an unnamed situation in a stated sector for a stated period. The desk can say no, and often does, because saying yes costs it the right to trade. If it says yes, the name goes onto the firm's restricted list (some firms first use a confidential watch list, which lets compliance monitor trading before the formal restriction), and the pre-trade check in the order-management system starts doing its one job: every order and every RFQ response in the issuer's securities is blocked before it leaves the building, the auto-responder's included. That last clause matters to a systematic desk, because the block has to sit upstream of the quoting engine. An algorithm that keeps answering RFQs in a restricted name is the compliance breach, whatever the human traders do or do not know. The check does not stop the desk from *receiving* RFQs, which keep arriving, and the pattern of requests in a name the desk cannot quote is the most tantalizing data set on the floor and the one it must not use.

Loans add a second layer, because the syndicated loan market has always run on two kinds of lender. A *private-side* lender receives the borrower's non-public information (monthly financials, projections, lender-call materials) through the agent's data site and accepts that it is restricted from trading the borrower's public securities while it holds that information. A *public-side* lender declines the private materials so that it can keep trading the borrower's bonds and stock, and lives with knowing less. A loan market maker is usually public-side by design. When two parties trade a loan and one of them may know something the other does not, they often sign a *big-boy letter*: each acknowledges that the other may hold material non-public information, represents that it is a sophisticated party, and agrees not to sue over the asymmetry. The letter is a contract about adverse selection. It does not make the information go away, and it does not relieve anyone of insider-trading law or of the policy obligations the orders above describe; it allocates civil risk between two sophisticated parties. From the dealer's chair, a counterparty that asks for a big-boy letter is announcing, in writing, that its $\pi$ may be high.

The other half of the story is that the wall comes down, and when it comes down is often predictable. Restriction is dated. A creditor that signs a non-disclosure agreement to negotiate with a company usually does so on the understanding that the company will *cleanse* it by a stated date, publishing the material non-public information the creditor received, typically in an 8-K, so that the creditor is free to trade again. Wolfspeed in 2025 is a clean example from primary filings. The company had hired J.P. Morgan, Perella Weinberg and Latham & Watkins in February, did not pay the interest due on 2 June on its 2029 convertible notes and entered the thirty-day grace period, and negotiated with its creditors under NDAs. On 22 June it signed a restructuring support agreement with holders of more than 97% of its senior secured notes, with Renesas, the lender under its unsecured customer-deposit agreement, and with holders of more than 67% of its convertible notes. On 23 June the 8-K went up, and with it, as exhibit 99.2, the cleansing material the creditors had seen under their NDAs; the 8-K's own risk language cites "the substantial doubt about the Company's ability to continue as a going concern." The RSA's milestones were in the filing too: a petition by 1 July, a confirmation order within 75 days, and an outside date for the plan to go effective within four months of the petition. The company filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 in the Southern District of Texas on 30 June; the court confirmed the plan on 8 September, day 70 and inside the milestone; and the plan went effective on 29 September.

Almost every one of those dates was knowable before it arrived, and each is a feature. A grace period that starts on 2 June runs out at the start of July, so a negotiation under NDA with that deadline must either cleanse or fail by then. The cleansing 8-K is not news to the restricted holders: they have known for weeks, some of what they knew has already leaked into prices through the flow §3.6.2 watches, and running the filing through (3.3) as a fresh likelihood ratio counts twice what the tape already contains. It is news to everyone else, and it marks the moment the restricted population rejoins the flow. For a desk modelling $\pi$, that is when the "silence where there used to be noise" feature should switch off for the cleansed holders and when their trading becomes informative again. And the RSA's outside date tells the desk when the next wall, the plan's effectiveness and the distribution of new securities, is due to come down. The tradable feature is the calendar. A desk that knows when restrictions lapse can forecast when supply returns.

> **Poker Table.** "The player who suddenly stops betting is telling you something too," says Ines. Theo asks the Ad Hoc Group, which has formed in the corner of the trading floor as it always does, whether it is buying first-lien paper. The Ad Hoc Group answers in unison: "We are represented by counsel." Theo updates $\pi$ to 0.9 anyway. A press report that a lender group has retained counsel is a likelihood ratio with a timestamp. A Rule 2019 statement, if it comes to that, is a likelihood ratio with a docket number.

## 3.7 Case Study: Serta Simmons, June 22, 2020, Seen From a Market Maker's Blotter

Chapter 13 owns the full legal and game-theoretic autopsy of Serta. This section asks a narrower question: what did the uptier look like from a dealer's blotter, and what would the machinery of this chapter have done with it?

*(The desk, its 2020 blotter, the RFQ, Mags's position and all dialogue are fictional; her position is an outside holder's, with no group membership. The deal terms, document sections, party names and numbers come from the Fifth Circuit's opinion, the July 2026 remand ruling as reported in Mayer Brown's July 2026 note, and a January 2025 memo by Paul Weiss, which represented the excluded lenders.)*

Ines keeps the desk's old blotters the way other people keep tax returns, in a format nobody can open without asking her. On a slow Thursday in Theo's fifth week she opens June 2020.

"You've done SVB. That was a bank, and the surprise was the deposit run. This one is a mattress company, and it's harder, because almost nothing about it was a surprise to anyone who had read the documents."

Serta Simmons Bedding had refinanced in November 2016 with two syndicated term loans, \$1.95bn of first lien and \$450mm of second lien. Theo pulls up the first-lien credit agreement, which Ines has also kept, and goes where an RSSG banker goes first: the sacred rights. Section 2.18 is the pro rata sharing clause, the promise that every lender in the class is paid alike, and section 9.02(b) puts that promise among the terms that cannot be changed without the consent of each lender it affects. "So you can't do a non-pro-rata deal," he says. "Unless—" and he goes looking for the exceptions, because every sacred right has a side door. Section 9.05(g) lets the borrower and its affiliates buy back loans from individual lenders on a non-pro-rata basis in one of two ways: through a Dutch auction open to all lenders, which the agreement defines in loving detail, or "through open market purchases," which it does not define at all.

Kaz, who has drifted over with a coffee, puts a finger on the three words. "What is an open market? Is it a place? A price? A procedure?"

It is the best question anyone asks all afternoon, and a federal court of appeals took four and a half years to answer it.

Mags has an answer already. She was on the wrong side of this one: fifteen million of this first lien on a bank's book that June *(illustrative; Chapter 1 tells how she came to own it, Chapter 14 what she did next)*, left outside, and she remembers it the way other people remember a car accident. "Look at the document. It spends pages telling you exactly how a Dutch auction works: who is invited, how the price is set, how allocations are prorated. Then it spends three words on 'open market purchases' and never defines them. When a contract spends pages on one door and three words on another, the three words are not a second door. They're a window for buying a loan on the screen like anybody else. You don't march a majority of the class through a window." She is, it would turn out, close to the Fifth Circuit's own reasoning, which called the absence of a definition beside the meticulous auction definition a "deafening silence." "Under that document," she says, "the trade was unthinkable."

"And yet," says Ines, and scrolls.

The transaction that closed on 22 June 2020 is set out in the Docket Diary below, and Theo reads it twice. A bare majority of lenders put in new money ahead of everyone, swapped their existing loans into a new layer that also sat ahead of everyone, and did it at a discount: the first lien went in at a 26% discount to par and the second lien at 61%, according to Paul Weiss's memo. The participants titled their document the "Open Market Purchase and Cashless Exchange Agreement." Kaz reads the title aloud and says nothing, which for Kaz is a remark. The participating lenders, as the Fifth Circuit's opinion lists them among the appellees, were Barings, Boston Management and Research, Credit Suisse Asset Management, Eaton Vance and Invesco Senior Secured Management. On the other side were the Excluded Lenders and the LCM lenders, with Citadel Equity Fund appealing the plan's indemnity of the participants.

"Forget the court for a minute," says Ines. "It's May 2020. You're me. Who's buying first lien?"

"Whoever needs 50.1%," says Theo. "A group that's short of a majority has to buy, in this tranche, before it can do anything. So: concentrated net buying in the first lien by a few kinds of accounts, with the second lien and everything else quiet." It is §3.6.1, recited.

"Right shape. Now tell me whether our blotter showed it."

He looks. The blotter shows what one desk saw, which was a handful of trades in a loan that traded across many desks and through the agent's assignment process, and nothing in it distinguishes a vote being assembled from a CLO trimming a position. "I can't tell," he says.

"Neither could I. One desk sees a slice of the tape. In 2020 nobody on this floor had a detector, and our slice was too thin to tell a vote from a rebalance. That's not a confession. It's the specification for what we built afterwards."

#### Decision point: 2:40 p.m., Friday 19 June 2020

Then she stops scrolling. *Friday, 19 June 2020, 2:40 p.m. An RFQ from an account the desk has never traded with: client sells \$5mm of the Serta first-lien term loan, indicating 45* *(the account, the size, the time and the price are illustrative)*. The desk knows what is in the credit agreement and what is in its own blotter, and no more. Its two-point prior on the loan over the holding horizon is about 45 if no non-pro-rata deal happens and about 25 if one does and the desk's lot is left outside, with 70% on no deal *(illustrative)*. Ines puts three tickets on the screen. Bid 39, the mid of that prior, as though the seller were nobody in particular. Bid 36, what the asymmetric Glosten–Milgrom formula of §3.2.1 says for $\pi = 0.3$. Or decline to quote.

"Pick one," she says, "and tell me why, before you read the next page. And notice what the seller asked for. Forty-five is the no-deal price. Whoever this is wants you to pay as though the window were only a window."

> **Docket Diary.** **November 2016:** Serta Simmons Bedding refinances, borrowing \$1.95bn under a first-lien term loan agreement. **June 22, 2020:** the uptier closes. Serta receives \$200mm of new money as first-out super-priority debt; participating lenders exchange first- and second-lien loans (about \$1.2bn in total) into roughly \$875mm of second-out super-priority debt (Fifth Circuit opinion). On the first-lien side, the participants held about \$992mm (52.6%) of the class and exchanged it for roughly \$734mm face of new first-lien second-out debt; the excluded lenders held about \$895mm (47.4%) (Mayer Brown, July 2026). The credit agreement allowed non-pro-rata buybacks only through a Dutch auction open to all lenders or through "open market purchases." **January 2023:** Serta files for Chapter 11 in the Southern District of Texas, and the bankruptcy court upholds the transaction later that year. **December 31, 2024:** the Fifth Circuit reverses (No. 23-20181; opinion revised February 14, 2025), reading "open market purchase" as a purchase in the secondary market for syndicated loans and holding that a privately negotiated exchange with a subset of lenders falls outside it, breaching the sacred right of pro rata sharing. **July 7, 2026:** on remand, the bankruptcy court awards the excluded lenders \$261.13mm, measured at the June 22, 2020 breach date, plus New York statutory prejudgment interest at 9% per annum from that date. *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel, wishes to state that it has always supported pro rata treatment, in principle, for other people.*

Kaz, reading the Docket Diary, asks who enforces sacred rights. "Is there a priest?" Mags: "The Fifth Circuit, apparently. Four and a half years later, at nine percent simple." The arithmetic is worth doing, because it is the recovery distribution Kaz will later try to model as a Gaussian. Simple interest at 9% for the roughly 6.04 years from June 22, 2020 to July 7, 2026 adds about 54% to the award, taking it to roughly \$403mm *(our arithmetic; approx.)*. The excluded lenders' recovery on this claim was not a draw from a bell curve. It was a mass point at zero for four and a half years, then a judgment, delivered six years after the breach, whose size depended on two words in a 2016 credit agreement. "Open market purchase": the most expensive three words in finance, \$261.13 million before interest, according to a court that had six years to think about it.

The same day the Fifth Circuit ruled, a New York appellate court upheld Mitel's 2022 uptier (*Ocean Trails CLO VII v. MLN Topco*, App. Div. 1st Dept., December 31, 2024), because Mitel's exception permitted a "purchase" with no "open market" qualifier. "So the difference between a quarter of a billion dollars of damages and zero is two words?" asks Kaz. "Two words and a forum," says Mags. This is the strongest argument in the book for putting a document-reading human in the loop of a systematic model: the likelihood ratio of "sponsor-backed loan, 52% holder concentration, quoted in the 40s" is not a number until you have read the pro rata sharing section.

#### What the blotter would have shown

Run the chapter's machinery on the spring of 2020 *(stylized; the pre-deal price path below is illustrative, not taken from the record)*.

*The prior.* A first-lien term loan with no uptier blocker, an undefined "open market purchase" exception, a sponsor under liquidity pressure in a pandemic, and a holder base where one group could plausibly reach a majority: the PRS is high, so the CUSUM threshold $h(\text{PRS})$ is low.

*The flow.* Theo's recitation is the signature the detector looks for. Whether the actual flow in May and June 2020 looked like it is an empirical question this book does not answer; the point is that the detector is designed to ask it.

*The quote.* Ines's ticket carries the prior, so the mid is $0.7 \times 45 + 0.3 \times 25 = 39$. With a feature-implied $\pi(x) = 0.3$ for a sell RFQ in this tranche, the asymmetric GM formula gives a half-spread of

$$
m - b = 20 \times \frac{0.7 \times 0.3 \times 0.3}{0.35 + 0.3 \times 0.3} \approx 2.9 \text{ points},
$$

a bid near 36, against a desk that, assuming $\pi = 0$, would bid close to the 39 mid. Both bids lose money if the deal happens. The difference is that the first one is priced to lose money only as often as the model says it should.

*The hand history.* Any dealer that carried long inventory of the excluded paper into June 22, 2020 had its markout delivered in one print. The 2026 remand's breach-date valuation of the excluded loans at 25 cents on the dollar is the closest thing to an official markout this market produces.

*After the announcement.* The remand opinion's mitigation testimony gives a rare look at post-LMT liquidity. Barclays was described as the lead market maker in Serta debt. Up to \$186mm face of first-lien term loans traded between June 2020 and December 31, 2021, and defense experts argued the excluded lenders could have sold 29% to 43% of the observed volume, against holdings of about \$895mm. In other words, even a holder who wanted out could have sold at most about a tenth of the position over eighteen months. Left-behind paper is not just cheaper. It is stuck, and a market maker's inventory limit in it is set by that fact, not by a volatility estimate.

**The counter-technology.** Holders learned. Excluded-lender risk is now managed before the fact, through cooperation agreements in which a majority of a class agrees to negotiate only together, and through blockers written into new documents. (Cooperation agreements signed in this book so far: 0.) For the detector, a cooperation agreement is a regime change: it raises the bar for a non-pro-rata deal, which lowers the PRS, and it also creates a large restricted population whose silence the model must not mistake for calm.

Theo closes the blotter. "What did you do?"

"I bid 36," says Ines, "and I was covered. Somebody paid more. On the Monday, the loan I didn't buy sat in the excluded class, and the court would later value it at 25 cents on that date. Coming second was the best hand I played all year."

"So the GM bid was right."

"The GM bid was right at a twenty-day horizon." She taps the Docket Diary. "Now run it at six years." Theo does the arithmetic the remand makes possible. An excluded holder of \$895mm kept loans valued at 25 cents on the breach date, \$223.75mm, and in the win state added the award, about \$403mm with interest: roughly \$627mm in all, or about 70 cents on the face, before the award is allocated among the participants, collected and survives any appeal *(our arithmetic, from the figures above)*. The paper that marked out at 25 on the day became, for a holder able to wait six years and pay lawyers, a litigation asset worth far more than the dealer's horizon could see.

"So who was right? Me at 36, or the buyer who paid more and held on?"

"Both, at different horizons, and neither of us knew the second horizon existed. A markout tells you whether a bid was good over the horizon you chose to hold. It cannot tell you about a court that hasn't ruled yet. That's what §3.1.3 meant about the desk not holding to resolution." She shuts the laptop. "The question you asked me after Brambleworth was how you'd know afterwards whether a bid was good. Part of the answer is that you have to say *afterwards when*."

The short leg has its own problem, and Ines saves it for last because it is the one a banker never meets: in a pitch book, "short" is a word. Nobody lends a term loan. There is no securities-lending desk for syndicated loans; Serta was not among the short list of US names whose single-name CDS trades regularly; and a loan index or a loan ETF would short the asset class while leaving Serta's own default whole in the book. > **View From Another Seat.** *The CLO manager.* Read the captions and a pattern appears. The New York case that went the other way from Serta on the same day was brought by lenders whose caption leads with a CLO: *Ocean Trails CLO VII v. MLN Topco* (App. Div. 1st Dept., December 31, 2024). The seat explains why that is not a coincidence. A CLO is a box of leveraged loans financed by its own tranches of notes, and the box has rules. It generally cannot fund new money on demand, because its cash belongs to a waterfall and its reinvestment rights are bounded by its indenture; an uptier that asks participants to put in \$200mm of first-out money is asking for the one thing many CLO vehicles find hardest to give. It has a CCC bucket, typically capped around 7.5% of the portfolio, beyond which excess CCC loans are carried at market value rather than par in its overcollateralization tests, so a downgrade wave can push it to sell exactly when the bid is worst. And it has those overcollateralization tests, whose failure diverts cash away from the equity and junior notes to pay down the senior notes. Put together, the rules make the CLO the natural excluded lender: often unable to join the new money, sometimes forced to sell into the announcement, and structurally slow to organise. From the dealer's chair, CLO flow is the clearest example of the low-$\pi$ seller of §3.2, whose sales are about its tests and not about the borrower. It is also the population whose silence in a name says least, because it was rarely in the room. The CLO seat has its own information problem, too: the SEC's 2024 order against Sound Point (§3.6.3) concerned a manager that traded loans in its CLOs while it took part in lender groups and creditors' committees. A manager on a committee is restricted like anyone else, and its CLOs' flow goes quiet with it.

## 3.8 The Engine: RFQ Adverse Selection, Markouts, 8-K Updates and the Pre-LMT Detector

Every scene in this chapter needed something computed, and the engine is the list of those computations. The Serta Friday needed `gm_half_spread`, the exact asymmetric charge for a lopsided two-point prior: the 2.9 points between a 39 mid and a 36 bid. Every RFQ, Brambleworth's included, needs `gm_bid`, which stacks that charge, the winner's-curse shading for $n$ dealers and the inventory term of (3.2) into one price, in price points and never in spread units. The flinch Theo felt on the Brambleworth Friday needs `fit_pi`, which turns the features of §3.2.2 into a calibrated $\pi(x)$ and learns from the RFQs the desk lost as well as the ones it won. The Tuesday-morning vignette needed `markouts_bp`, the raw and IDO hand history, computed against reference mids that should be stored as received, with the time they were received, and flagged when stale. The one-fill client of Exercise 6 needs `shrunk_cpty_scores`, which borrows strength from the crowd before it lets a lucky counterparty earn a tighter quote. The SVB Monday needed `update_pd_from_8k` and its companion `spread_move`: an odds update keyed to a filing's acceptance time, an optional base-rate correction, and an explicit $\kappa$ on the way from $\mathbb{P}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$. And the Saks and Serta springs needed `cusum_lmt_detector` with `prs_threshold`, a running tally of unrestricted, LME-prone net buying whose alarm threshold is set by what the documents say. The specification below makes each of them precise enough that ROBO cannot misread it.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**What ROBO built from the vague version.** Theo's first attempt at a spec was one line: "Build a quoting engine that minimizes markouts." ROBO built exactly that. It quoted every bid forty points below the mid. Markout on fills: zero, because there were no fills. Hit rate: zero. Franchise: zero. ROBO did not complain, and it did not guess; it minimized the objective it was given. The precise version maximizes expected PnL *subject to* a hit-ratio target, which is how Barzykin and Ciceri (HSBC) formulate bond market making with a hit-ratio constraint (arXiv 2604.20406, 2026): dualize the target into a Lagrange multiplier and the quote decomposes into a riskless spread, an inventory correction and a hit-ratio correction. Ines calls the multiplier "paying to stay at the table." This chapter's engine implements the riskless-spread and inventory pieces, (3.1)–(3.2); Chapter 18 adds the hit-ratio term.

**Module.** `ch03_rfq_adverse_selection.py`; numpy, scipy and pandas only; one seeded generator `rng = np.random.default_rng(3)` shared by all blocks; blocks run in order in one interpreter.

**Edge cases and guards.** Reject `pi` outside $[0,1]$, `q` outside $(0,1)$ and negative gaps; never let a spread-unit `sigma_v` reach a price-unit formula (unit-tag the column). Compute the logistic function through `tanh` to avoid overflow. Fit likelihoods on means, not sums, so optimizer tolerances are scale-free. Counterparty scores must be shrunk, and the shrinkage intensity floored at zero, never negative. CUSUM thresholds are floored so that no single observation can trigger an alarm. Every feature must be point-in-time: an 8-K's `file_date` and acceptance time, not its period-of-report date.

**Block 1: the quote.** Equation (3.1) and its asymmetric form, the winner's-curse shading and equation (3.2).

At 2:58 on the Brambleworth Friday the bid had to come out of something faster than a whiteboard. The working version is a handful of small functions, small on purpose because each fails in its own way: the half-spread on impossible probabilities, the shading on bad calibration, the inventory term on a wrong sign.

Listing 3.1 builds the quote from four functions. `sigmoid` is the logistic function written through `tanh`, which returns the same values as $1/(1+e^{-z})$ but cannot overflow on a large negative input; every later block leans on it. `gm_half_spread(pi, q, delta)` is the exact asymmetric half-spread of §3.2.1 for a sell RFQ, the gap times $q(1-q)\pi$ over $\tfrac{1-\pi}{2} + \pi(1-q)$. It raises an error for $\pi$ outside $[0,1]$, $q$ outside $(0,1)$ or a negative gap, rather than returning a plausible-looking number, and the ask side is the same call with $1-q$. `winners_curse_w` returns $w(n) = $ `w_per_log` $\times \ln n$: zero for a lone dealer, rising slowly with the crowd. The default of 0.15 is a placeholder, not a finding; the chapter's instruction is to calibrate it from cover prices. `gm_bid` then assembles equation (3.2): the mid, less the adverse-selection term (the half-spread at $q = \tfrac12$ and gap $2\sigma_V$, which collapses to $\pi\sigma_V$), less the curse shading in units of $\sigma_V$, less the inventory term $\tfrac12\gamma\sigma_{\text{IDO}}^2(2q + Q)$. Everything is vectorized, so a day's RFQs go in as arrays and a column of bids comes out.

The assertions at the bottom tie the code to the text. The symmetric case reproduces $\pi\sigma_V$; the stylized Serta inputs ($\pi = 0.3$, $q = 0.7$, gap 20 points) give about 2.86 points; the small-$\pi$ approximation holds; the half-spread rises with $\pi$; and a bid with no information and no risk equals the mid.

The live engine and the backtest both call this one listing for the quote, and it is less than the whole quoting problem: it returns a bid only (a positive `size` means the dealer buys), it fixes $q = \tfrac12$ inside `gm_bid`, so a skewed binary prior needs a direct call to `gm_half_spread`, and its hit-ratio term waits for Chapter 18. The guards check ranges, not meaning, so the units bug of §3.2.1 still gets through: a `sigma_v` in spread terms passes every one of them and returns a bid wrong by orders of magnitude, with no error and a very confident decimal.

**Block 2: a simulated RFQ tape, the zero-profit property and the winner's curse.** Informed senders know the two-point value; uninformed senders pick a side at random; the true $\pi(x)$ is logistic in three features. Competitors share a weak signal about the value, which is what creates the winner's curse.

Before a new $\pi(x)$ gets anywhere near a live bid, it plays a toy market where the answer is known, because live RFQ data is censored (you only see the hands you won), slow, and silent about whether the model or the tape is wrong. Ines's version: nobody learns a new game on the first hand played for real money.

Listing 3.2 is that toy market. `BETA_TRUE` fixes the truth, an intercept plus loadings on a size z-score, an event-window flag and a distressed-counterparty flag, with `SIGMA_V` at 1.5 points. `simulate_rfq_flow` draws 200,000 RFQs. Each gets a true $\pi$ from the logistic of its features and is informed with that probability. The bond's value is the mid plus or minus $\sigma_V$; informed clients sell only when the value is the low one, uninformed clients flip a coin, and $\eta = +1$ means the dealer buys. The horizon mid is the value plus a little noise. Between two and eight dealers compete, so there are one to seven rivals, each posting a price concession, and the rivals share a weak signal ($\rho = 0.3$) about the value: their concessions widen when the trade is bad for the dealer and narrow when it is good. The function returns the tape and the design matrix, with the best and second-best rival concession on every row.

Two tests follow. In Test A one dealer fills everything: the Glosten–Milgrom quote at the true $\pi$ earns a mean markout within three standard errors of zero, while quoting the mid loses about $\sigma_V$ times the average $\pi$, many standard errors below zero. In Test B, `rfq_outcomes` labels each RFQ Done, Covered or Traded Away by comparing our half-spread with the rivals', and the assertions say the unpleasant part out loud: markouts on fills are negative, and the RFQs we lost have better counterfactual markouts. On the seeded run the dealer fills 54% of the tape at about $-1.3$ points, and the hands it lost would have made about $+1.5$. The last lines re-quote with the `winners_curse_w` shading, which cuts fills to 36% and shrinks the loss without erasing it, because the shading is only as good as its calibration.

It is a bench, not a market model: the rivals are one stylized parameter, and the curse exists only because $\rho > 0$; at zero, fills become a fair sample of the flow (Exercise 2). The two-dealer auction is its blind spot. With one rival, the second-best rival price is infinite, so every lost RFQ there is labeled Covered by construction, and the Covered share says nothing about cover distance.

**Block 3: fitting $\pi(x)$ from markout signs.** Under the symmetric model, a filled RFQ is followed by an adverse move with probability $(1 + \pi(x))/2$, so we fit that link directly by maximum likelihood rather than forcing a plain logistic regression onto the adverse-sign label.

The toy market knows who was informed; the real one only shows what the price did afterward, and the adverse probability that §3.2.2 ties to $\pi$ lives between one half and one. A plain logistic regression forced onto that label gives coefficients that are wrong in a way no residual plot will show. The fit runs nightly, because the flow mix changes and a human reading standard errors at 6 a.m. is a control that will eventually be on holiday.

Listing 3.3 is `fit_pi`. It takes a feature matrix `X` with an intercept column and a 0/1 vector marking RFQs followed by an adverse move. Inside, `nll_and_grad` computes $s = \text{sigmoid}(Xb)$, maps it to $p = (1+s)/2$ clipped away from 0 and 1, and returns the negative mean log-likelihood together with its analytic gradient, using $dp/d(\text{index}) = s(1-s)/2$. The mean, not the sum, keeps optimizer tolerances independent of sample size. `scipy`'s L-BFGS-B starts at zero and uses that gradient. Standard errors come from the expected Fisher information, $X^\top \text{diag}(w) X$ with $w = (dp)^2 / (p(1-p))$, inverted, square-rooted on the diagonal. The function returns $\hat\beta$ and its standard errors.

The driver then closes the loop with the toy market. It labels adverse as $\eta\,(m_h - m) < 0$ on the whole tape, recovers `BETA_TRUE` to within four standard errors on every coefficient, and checks that the fitted $\pi$ correlates above 0.95 with the true one. Note that the labels use every RFQ, not just the fills, which only a simulation can do; Block 2 explained why a fit on fills alone is biased. Finally it prices Theo's Friday RFQ: large, inside the lender-call window, from a distressed-fund account, eight dealers. The fitted $\hat\pi$ is about 0.64, and with a weekend $\sigma_V$ of 6 points, `gm_bid` returns about 81.07. The mid on Thursday was 81. That agreement should make you suspicious rather than proud, since it is one hand.

In production the label is the sign of the IDO markout of Block 4; a raw-markout label teaches the model to call the whole market toxic. The link has a floor, and the floor can bite: adverse probability cannot fall below one half, so a segment whose true $\pi$ is near zero, or whose sample happens to show fewer than half adverse moves, has no interior optimum. Its coefficients drift toward large negative values, $\hat\pi$ goes to zero, and the standard errors blow up or the information matrix refuses to invert.

**Block 4: IDO markouts and shrunk counterparty scores.** The IDO reference implements §3.3.3; the hand from §3.3 is the regression test.

Theo's first markout report accused a pension fund of insider trading for owning beta, and Ines caught it in ten seconds. Nobody reads every report in ten seconds, so the correction goes into code and runs before any markout reaches a counterparty score. Once the markouts are clean, the average over a counterparty's three fills is still mostly luck, and a desk that tiers its clients on raw averages will eventually fire the wrong one.

Listing 3.4 has three pieces. `ido_reference_move` is the bracket of §3.3.3 in points: the reference price times minus spread duration times beta times the index-spread change, minus modified duration times the Treasury-yield change, with the changes in decimals so that 20bp is 0.0020. `markouts_bp` takes a fills table with `p0`, `eta` and a `mid_{h}d` column for each horizon, plus optional `sys_{h}d` columns holding the systematic move over the same horizon. For each horizon it subtracts the systematic move from the later mid, then returns $\eta\,(\text{ref} - p_0)/p_0 \times 10^4$, the markout in basis points of price. The regression test is the Friday hand: bought at 85.75, mid 81 on Thursday, systematic move $-0.4785$, markout about $-498$bp, asserted to lie between $-510$ and $-490$.

`shrunk_cpty_scores` implements §3.3.4. For each counterparty it takes the fill count and mean markout, pools the within-counterparty variance across all of them (weighted by fills minus one, so one-fill accounts add no variance estimate but stay in the table), estimates $\tau^2$ as the variance of the counterparty means less the average sampling variance, floors it at zero, and shrinks each mean by $n_c/(n_c + \sigma^2/\tau^2)$. It returns the table of counts, raw means, shrinkage factors and scores, with $\tau^2$ and $\sigma^2$. If $\tau^2$ comes out zero, every factor is zero and every score is zero, which is the honest answer when the data cannot separate the accounts. The test simulates 400 counterparties with a true spread of 20bp and per-fill noise of 150bp, and asserts that the shrunk scores beat the raw means on mean squared error; on the seeded run they do so by about a factor of eight, even though the estimated $\hat\tau$ lands near 42bp, so shrinkage is gentler than ideal.

The scores feed $\pi(x)$; they are not verdicts on clients. Two defaults are traps. `shrunk_cpty_scores` reads `mo_bp` unless it is told otherwise, while `markouts_bp` emits `mo_5d_bp`. And a missing, misspelled or never-merged `sys_{h}d` column is quietly treated as zero, so the function returns raw markouts and the pension fund of §3.3.3 is toxic again, unless something checks that the columns exist before the call.

**Block 5: the Bayesian 8-K and the base-rate correction.** Equations (3.3) and (3.4), with the worked example of §3.4.2 as a regression test.

The parser fires at 4:31 p.m., the filing contains the word "forbearance", and the fastest readers in the market are already repricing. §3.4.2 did the algebra on a napkin; what the desk automates is the discipline around it: the prior is re-based to the live default rate before the likelihood ratio touches it, inputs that are not probabilities are refused, and the answer comes out in spread, because nobody has ever hedged a posterior odds ratio.

Listing 3.5 is short, and each function is one equation. `elkan(p, S, T)` is (3.4): it takes a PD calibrated to a training sample with default rate `S` and re-bases it to the live rate `T`. `update_pd_from_8k(p_prior, lr, S, T)` is (3.3): it rejects a prior outside $[0,1]$ or a negative likelihood ratio, applies the Elkan correction first when both `S` and `T` are supplied, and returns $\text{LR}\cdot p / (1 - p + \text{LR}\cdot p)$. Both accept arrays, so a book of names and a vector of likelihood ratios go through in one call. `hazard_from_pd` inverts the survival relationship with `log1p`, which keeps the small-PD cases accurate, and divides by the horizon.

The assertions are the regression suite. An LR of one leaves every prior unchanged, and the posterior rises monotonically in the LR. The worked example, a 6% prior and an LR of 2.5, returns 13.76%. Converting the hazard move with $L = 0.6$ and $\kappa = 2$ gives about 1,034bp, asserted between 1,000 and 1,070. The base-rate correction stays inside $[0,1]$, does nothing when `S` equals `T`, and is checked to be exactly an odds update. The naive $T/S$ scaling is shown to break the bound for any output above $S/T$. The spread conversion is inline test code with $L$ and $\kappa$ as bare numbers, so a spec should ask ROBO for a reusable function that takes $\kappa$ as an explicit input, never a hidden constant.

It takes the LR as given, so it is only as good as the likelihood-ratio table behind it, conditioned on rating and PRS (§3.4.2). The optional pair is the trap: supply `T` without `S`, or the reverse, and the function skips the re-basing without a warning, so a PD calibrated to a 2% training sample is updated as if it already sat on the live base rate. When the live rate is higher, as it is for the book this chapter cares about, the stressed names come out understated.

**Block 6: the pre-LMT detector.** Page's CUSUM (3.5), vectorized over paths, the Lorden false-alarm bound as a test, and the PRS-dependent threshold.

Ines's player who stops betting is easy to spot at a poker table and impossible to spot by hand across three thousand tranches. A daily z-score misses the small, persistent pattern that is the whole signature of accumulation and cannot say in advance how often it will cry wolf; the desk wants an alarm that adds up weak evidence, forgets stale evidence, and states its false-alarm rate before it is switched on.

Listing 3.6 is Page's CUSUM from (3.5), wrapped with the PRS-dependent threshold. `cusum_lmt_detector(y, mu1, sigma_y, h)` takes ADV-scaled net buying by the LME-prone set, either a single series or a matrix with one column per path or tranche. It converts each day to the log-likelihood ratio $\ell_t = (\mu_1/\sigma_y^2)(y_t - \mu_1/2)$ and runs the recursion $S_t = \max(0, S_{t-1} + \ell_t)$ in a loop over time that is vectorized across columns. It returns the statistic in the shape of the input and, for each column, the index of the first day the statistic exceeds the threshold, or $-1$ if it never does. The threshold `h` can be a scalar or one value per column. `prs_threshold` maps a Priming Risk Score to $h = h_0 - \operatorname{logit}(\text{PRS})$, clips the score away from 0 and 1, and clips the result between a floor of 2.5 and a cap of 10, so a tranche with no blockers gets a short fuse and one with a Serta blocker gets a long one.

The tests check the two promises of the text. On 500 null paths of 6,000 days with $h = 4$, the average run length to a false alarm must be at least $e^4 \approx 55$, with paths that never alarm counted at the full 6,000 days; the seeded run gives about 304. Under a shifted mean with $\mu_1 = \sigma_y$, every path alarms, and the average delay is about 8.7 days against the $h/K = 8$ of the text, inside a deliberately loose assertion. The threshold is checked to fall as PRS rises and to equal $h_0$ at a PRS of one half.

An alarm triggers review and a pause on building long inventory; it is not a trade signal on its own. It is calibrated on independent Gaussian noise, and real ADV-scaled flow is fat-tailed and autocorrelated, so the $e^h$ bound is a design target, not a guarantee. The big print is its blind spot. The log-likelihood ratio is linear in `y`, so a single outsized day, an index rebalance or one block, counts in full; with $\mu_1 = \sigma_y$, any day above about 3 sigma clears the 2.5 floor on its own. The floor protects against ordinary noise, not against an outsized print, which is why `y` is winsorized before it enters.

Theo puts the USB stick on the desk next to the printout. It still holds the 13-week model, and it now also holds a column called `liq_runway_wks_pit_v1`. It is no longer emotional support. It has a refresh cadence and a decay horizon, which is more than most emotional support can say.

- **A survival question is a distribution with the variance left out.** The price is the mean. The dealer is paid for the conditional variance over the *holding horizon*, $\sigma_V^2(h)$, not over the life of the case, and a calendar event inside the horizon (a lender call, an 8-K deadline, a grace-period expiry) moves that variance more than any covenant does. Moyer's 62.5% was always a two-point distribution.
- **A bid is a posterior, not a markup.** $b = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[V \mid \text{client sells}]$, which for a symmetric two-point value is $m - \pi\,\sigma_V$ (3.1). The uninformed seller pays $\pi\sigma_V$ for the privilege of trading in a market that contains informed sellers. Charging it is what keeps you at zero; skipping it makes you the client's free data vendor.
- **Winning is information.** With $n$ dealers in competition, condition on winning: shade by $w(n)\,\sigma_V$ and carry the inventory term (3.2). The hands you lose look better than the hands you win. That is the winner's curse, and a backtest run on fills alone cannot see it.
- **Markouts are the hand history; IDO markouts are the honest hand history.** Measure at 1, 5 and 20 business days, strip index-spread and Treasury beta first, shrink counterparty scores toward zero, and never compute a markout against a mid you have not checked for staleness. A raw markout will accuse a pension fund of insider trading for owning beta.
- **An 8-K is a likelihood ratio with a filing date.** Update in odds (3.3), re-base with Elkan (3.4) when the training sample's default rate is not the live one, use the acceptance timestamp as the event time, and say out loud what $\kappa$ is when you cross from $\mathbb{P}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$. The lazy version sets $\kappa = 1$ and understates the spread move by a factor of $\kappa$ (by half, at the worked example's $\kappa = 2$) without telling anyone.
- **The Edge Ledger prices what you know.** Every RSSG skill becomes a feature with a data source, a refresh cadence, a half-life and a list of who else has it. Put features on a common clock before you add them, and remember that the edge in a document-derived feature is the cost of extracting it, which decays far more slowly than a statistic anyone can compute.
- **Pre-LMT flow is the purest adverse selection in credit, and it is sequentially detectable.** Run a CUSUM (3.5) on ADV-scaled net buying by LME-prone, *unrestricted* counterparties, with the threshold lowered by the Priming Risk Score. A restricted counterparty's silence is a feature, not calm.
- **The document matters more on the desk than it did at the bank.** Serta and Mitel were decided on the same day on different words, and two of them, "open market," were worth \$261.13mm before interest. The likelihood ratio of a flow pattern is conditional on the pro rata sharing section, which is the one input the ex-RSSG reader parses faster than any model in the building.

The precise version, ready to paste:

> Build a Python module `ch03_rfq_adverse_selection` (numpy, scipy, pandas only; one generator `np.random.default_rng(3)`; pure, vectorized functions; no network or file I/O in the core). All expectations are under $\mathbb{P}$, the desk's physical beliefs, except the spread translation in (6). Prices are in points per 100 par, sizes in \$mm face, horizons in business days.
>
> 1. `gm_half_spread(pi, q, delta)`: the exact asymmetric Glosten–Milgrom half-spread for a sell RFQ (§3.2.1); the buy side is `gm_half_spread(pi, 1 - q, delta)`. Raise on `pi` outside $[0,1]$, `q` outside $(0,1)$, or `delta < 0`.
> 2. `gm_bid(mid, sigma_v, pi_hat, n_dealers, q_inv, size, gamma, sigma_ido, w_per_log)`: equation (3.2). `sigma_v` is the physical value volatility over the holding horizon in points; the column is unit-tagged and a spread-unit input raises. `size > 0` means the dealer buys; `gamma` is in $1/(\text{point}\cdot\$\text{mm})$. Return the bid *and* its three components (adverse selection, winner's curse, inventory) for logging.
> 3. `fit_pi(X, adverse)`: maximum likelihood for $\mathbb{P}(\text{adverse} \mid x) = \tfrac12(1 + \sigma(x^\top\beta))$, fitted on Done RFQs **and** on counterfactual markouts of Covered and Traded Away RFQs; mean log-likelihood; return $\hat\beta$ and Fisher standard errors. Every feature is point-in-time: computable from data stamped strictly before the RFQ's `ts`.
> 4. `markouts_bp(fills, horizons=(1, 5, 20))` with an IDO variant per §3.3.3. A mid older than `max_stale_days` (default 5) is flagged and excluded, never used.
> 5. `shrunk_cpty_scores(fills)`: empirical-Bayes shrinkage of per-counterparty mean IDO markouts; between-counterparty variance floored at zero; output `n`, `raw`, `shrink`, `theta_hat`. Counterparty names never leave the desk; logs carry internal ids.
> 6. `update_pd_from_8k(p_prior, lr, S=None, T=None)`: equation (3.3), with optional Elkan re-basing (3.4). The event time is the filing's EDGAR acceptance timestamp, never the period-of-report date. A companion `spread_move(p_prior, p_post, L, kappa)` returns $L\,\kappa\,(\lambda' - \lambda)$ in bp; `L` and `kappa` are required arguments with no defaults, because $\kappa = 1$ must be a decision, not an accident.
> 7. `cusum_lmt_detector(y, mu1, sigma_y, h)` and `prs_threshold(prs, h0, h_min, h_max)`: equation (3.5). `y` is ADV-scaled net *client* buying of one tranche by LME-prone counterparties that are not on the restricted list. Exclude the desk's own principal flow and all inter-dealer prints. Restricted counterparties enter as a separate "silence" feature (the drop in their usual axe and inquiry frequency), never as zeros in `y`. The floor `h_min` guarantees that no single observation can fire an alarm.
>
> **Tests that must pass.** On a simulated tape, a single dealer quoting GM with the true $\pi(x)$ earns zero within three standard errors, and quoting mid loses about $\bar\pi\,\sigma_V$; under competition, mean fill markouts are negative and lost-RFQ counterfactuals are better; $\hat\beta$ is within four standard errors of the truth; shrunk scores beat raw scores in mean squared error; a likelihood ratio of one is the identity; the worked 8-K example gives $p' = 0.1376$ and about 1,030bp at $L = 0.6$, $\kappa = 2$; on null paths the CUSUM's average run length is at least $e^{h}$, and on alternative paths the mean delay is close to $h/K$.
>
> **Per-RFQ log.** $\hat\pi$; each bid component; outcome (Done, Covered, Traded Away, Not Traded); cover price where reported; raw and IDO markouts at 1, 5 and 20 days; detector state and threshold for every tranche of the issuer.
>
> **Do not.** Fit $\pi$ on fills only. Remove the adverse-selection term because a fills-only backtest says the bid is too wide. Use period-of-report dates. Let one observation fire an alarm. Read a cooperation agreement's restricted population as calm. Add a hit-ratio target; that is Chapter 18's job. Until then the engine quotes the zero-profit bid and reports its hit ratio, and nobody asks it to minimize markouts, which is how ROBO ended up bidding forty points below the mid.

### Exercises

1. **(Moyer's footnote, with a clock.)** The near-dated bond trades at 85. Model A is Moyer's two-point distribution: 100 with probability 0.625, 60 with probability 0.375. Model B has three outcomes: refinancing at 100 (probability 0.50), a distressed exchange worth 80 (0.25) and Chapter 11 worth 60 (0.25). (a) Show that both models price the bond at 85 and compute their standard deviations. (b) For Model A, compute the exact asymmetric Glosten–Milgrom half-spreads at $\pi = 0.1$ for a sell RFQ and for a buy RFQ, and compare them with the symmetric approximation $\pi \times \text{sd}$. Explain in one sentence why the sell side is wider. (c) Suppose only 20% of the between-scenario variance resolves within a five-day hold and the diffusive volatility is 1.5 points per square-root day. Use §3.1.3 to compute $\sigma_V(5)$ for each model and the approximate zero-profit bid at $\pi = 0.1$. Which moves the quote more, the choice between Models A and B or the horizon?
   *Answer check:* (a) 19.36 and 16.58 points. (b) 1.92 points for a sell RFQ and 1.83 for a buy, against 1.94: with $q = 0.625$ the bad state is the less likely one, so a sell, which points toward it, moves the posterior further than a buy, which points where the prior already leans. (c) $\sigma_V(5) \approx 9.29$ and 8.14 points, bids of about 84.07 and 84.19. The model choice moves the bid by about a tenth of a point and the horizon moves it by about a point. The banker's instinct to argue about scenario probabilities is right about the case and wrong about the quote.

2. **(Switch off the winner's curse.)** In Block 2 of §3.8, set `rho=0`, so that competitors' quotes carry no information about the value. (a) Which assertion in Test B fails, and why is that the correct failure? (b) Using Milgrom and Weber's (1982) distinction between private and common values, explain why a winner's curse needs a common-value component, and argue that the post-trade value of a bond is almost purely a common value. (c) A platform reports the cover price to the winner of each Done RFQ. Explain how covers let you evaluate a *larger* shading $w(n)$ on historical data without re-running any auction, and why they cannot tell you what a *smaller* $w(n)$ would have won.
   *Answer check:* (a) `mo_fill.mean() < -3 * se(mo_fill)` fails. With `rho = 0`, whether you win depends only on $x$ and on noise independent of the value, so $\mathbb{E}[\text{MO} \mid \text{fill}, x] = \mathbb{E}[\text{MO} \mid x] = 0$. A test that still passed with its effect switched off would be testing nothing. (c) A won RFQ stays won under extra shading $\Delta$ exactly when the shaded price still beats the cover, so you can recompute the Done set and its markouts for any larger $w$. Whether a tighter quote would have won an RFQ you lost depends on the winning level, which many protocols never show the losers, so the curve is identified in one direction only. Ines: "The cover is the hand you didn't see. It only gets shown to the player who won the pot."

3. **(Kaz's bag of words, de-biased.)** Kaz trains a classifier on a *balanced* sample of 8-Ks containing the phrase "liability management": half from filers that defaulted within a year and half from filers that did not, so $S = 0.5$. In the live corpus the one-year default rate among filers using the phrase is $T = 0.03$ *(illustrative)*, because most of them are investment-grade issuers running tender offers (see the Trend Watch in §3.4). (a) A filing scores $p = 0.9$. Compute the Elkan-corrected probability (3.4). (b) Compute Kaz's shortcut $p\,T/S$, say in which direction it errs, and explain why the error grows as $p \to 1$. (c) Theo conditions the live base rate on "sponsor-backed and rated B3 or below," for which $T = 0.15$ *(illustrative)*. Recompute both numbers. (d) Using one-year hazards, $L = 0.6$ and $\kappa = 2$, how many basis points of spread separate the corrected answer in (a) from the shortcut? (e) Explain to Kaz, in one sentence and without the word "prior," why a balanced training set is not a balanced world.
   *Answer check:* (a) 0.218. (b) 0.054, too low: the correction acts on odds, and the shortcut rescales probabilities; as $p \to 1$ the corrected value tends to one while the shortcut tends to $T/S$. (c) 0.614 against 0.27. (d) About 2,280bp, roughly the difference between a single-B quoted on spread and a bond that trades on price. (e) One answer: "The model learned how much more often defaulters use the phrase than survivors do, not how often users of the phrase default, and only the second number trades."

4. **(Put the Ledger on a clock.)** Two Edge Ledger features describe the same second-lien: a flow signal with $\text{IC}_0 = 0.10$ and a half-life of 3 business days, and a fulcrum view with $\text{IC}_0 = 0.04$ and a half-life of 120 business days *(illustrative)*. (a) Compute each feature's IC at horizons of 1 and 5 days. (b) Find the crossover horizon $h^\star$ at which the two ICs are equal, in closed form and numerically. (c) The RFQ engine's markout horizons are 1, 5 and 20 days. Which feature belongs in $\pi(x)$, which in the portfolio model, and which in both? (d) Mags asked for a column for who lied. Define "lied" as a quantity you can compute from your own RFQ and axe logs, and say what its decay horizon should be.
   *Answer check:* (a) Flow 0.079 and 0.031; fulcrum 0.040 and 0.039. (b) $h^\star = \ln(\text{IC}^{\text{flow}}_0/\text{IC}^{\text{fulcrum}}_0) \,/\, \big[\ln 2\,(1/h^{\text{flow}}_{1/2} - 1/h^{\text{fulcrum}}_{1/2})\big] \approx 4.1$ days. (c) From about day four onward the fulcrum view is the stronger signal, even at the RFQ engine's own five-day horizon, so it belongs in both; the flow signal belongs in $\pi(x)$ only. Your ninety-hour fulcrum analysis beats the tape after four days. Frame that result; it is the only time this chapter flatters you. (d) One answer: the shrunk rate at which a counterparty's shown axes are followed, within $k$ days, by its trades in the opposite direction. Its half-life should be long, because, as Mags would tell you, so is a grudge.

5. **(Detector economics, or how to get restricted by your own compliance department.)** You run the CUSUM (3.5) with $\mu_1 = \sigma_y$ on each of 400 sponsor-backed tranches. (a) Using Lorden's bound, give an upper bound on false alarms per year (252 trading days) across the universe at $h = 4$ and at $h = 8$. (b) With $K = \mu_1^2/(2\sigma_y^2)$, compute the approximate detection delay at each threshold. If a group needs about 30 trading days from first accumulation to signing *(illustrative)*, how much warning does each leave? (c) With `prs_threshold` and $h_0 = 6$, compute the thresholds and delays for $\text{PRS} = 0.9$, $0.5$ and $0.1$. (d) Compliance will investigate every alarm and has the staff for one a week. Find the smallest uniform $h$ that keeps the null bound at or below 52 alarms a year, and explain why the realized false-alarm rate will be lower than the bound.
   *Answer check:* (a) At most $400 \times 252 / e^{4} \approx 1{,}846$ a year at $h = 4$, and about 34 at $h = 8$. Compliance, handed 1,846 alarms a year, will do what compliance does with 1,846 alarms a year, which is restrict you from the sector. (b) $K = 1/2$, so about 8 and 16 days, leaving about 22 and 14 days of warning. (c) $h = 3.80$, $6.00$ and $8.20$, with delays of about 7.6, 12 and 16.4 days: the document work that sets the PRS buys about nine days of warning at the top of the range. (d) $e^{h} \ge 400 \times 252 / 52 \approx 1{,}938$, so $h \ge 7.57$. Lorden's $e^{h}$ is a lower bound on the average run length, so the realized false-alarm rate sits below the bound, and Block 6's null simulation measures by how much.

6. **(ROBO and the lucky client.)** Theo tells ROBO: "Quote tighter for good clients." (a) Describe what ROBO builds, and what happens to the client whose only fill so far marked out at $+300$bp. (b) With a per-fill markout standard deviation of 150bp and a between-client standard deviation of 20bp (Block 4's values), compute the shrinkage factor $k = n/(n + \sigma^2/\tau^2)$ for $n = 1$, 10 and 100, and the shrunk score of the one-fill client. (c) Write the two-sentence spec that fixes it, with units and the IDO requirement. (d) Explain why the client who wins your tightest quote is the one whose score you should trust least.
   *Answer check:* (a) ROBO ranks clients by raw mean markout, so the one-fill client gets the tightest quote on the platform and keeps getting it until its second trade, which arrives promptly, because it is now being shown the best price on the platform. (b) $\sigma^2/\tau^2 = 56.25$, so $k \approx 0.017$, $0.151$ and $0.640$; the one-fill client's shrunk score is about $+5$bp. (c) For example: "Score each counterparty by the empirical-Bayes shrunk mean of its 5-day IDO markouts in bp of price, re-estimating the between-counterparty variance monthly and flooring it at zero. Tighten quotes by at most `c` bp per bp of shrunk score, never on a raw mean, and log every override for a counterparty with fewer than `n_min` fills." (d) Picking the maximum of many noisy estimates selects for noise. It is the winner's curse again, with you as the auctioneer and your own scores as the bidders.
