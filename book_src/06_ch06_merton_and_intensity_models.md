## 6.1 Itô's Lemma: The Volatility Tax

### 6.1.1 Desk framing: every claim is a function of something random

Theo's waterfalls have always been functions. The equity recovery in his plan-of-reorganization model was $\max(\text{EV}-\text{claims ahead},0)$; the second-lien recovery was a ramp that started at the first-lien's par and stopped at its own. What he never had to do was ask how fast those functions *move* when enterprise value wiggles every day. On a systematic desk that is the whole job, because the hedge ratio, the expected return, and the volatility of every security are derivatives of a payoff function with respect to a random state variable.

Ordinary calculus says $df = f'(V)\,dV$. Kaz's position is that ordinary calculus is a fine tool for things that do not wiggle, like bridges and Mags's opinion of adjusted EBITDA. For things that do, there is a correction term, and it is not optional.

Theo, to his credit, does not ask for the correction term on Thursday night. He asks something better: "Before you write anything Greek, tell me what shape the thing is. If the firm is worth \$100 today, what does next year look like?" Kaz puts the marker down. It is the right question, and Kaz answers it in three small pieces, one at a time, starting with the shape.

> **Math, Gently.** *Distributions, and why asset values are lognormal.* A distribution is a list of what could happen and how often. The **normal** distribution is the bell curve: outcomes cluster around an average and spread out symmetrically, so a fall of \$10 is exactly as likely as a rise of \$10. It is the right shape for things that *add up* from many small independent pieces, like the daily change in a spread measured in basis points or the error in a forecast. It is the wrong shape for things that *compound*, because a firm worth \$100 cannot fall by \$150, and a bell curve does not know that. When changes compound, you get a **lognormal**: the *logarithm* of the value is normal, so the value itself can never go below zero and has a long right tail (a few paths double or triple; none go negative).
>
> Here is the smallest possible example. Start with \$100 and let it move up or down 10%, twice, with equal odds. The four equally likely endings are $100\times1.1\times1.1=121$, $100\times1.1\times0.9=99$, $100\times0.9\times1.1=99$, and $100\times0.9\times0.9=81$. The **mean** is $(121+99+99+81)/4=100$: on average you broke even. The **median**, the middle outcome, is 99: the typical path lost money. The 121 at the top is pulling the average up, which is what a long right tail does. Mean above median is the fingerprint of a lognormal, and it matters enormously in credit, because default happens to the typical path, not the average one.
>
> You will meet the rest of the family as the book goes on, each on the job it is good at: the **exponential** for *how long until* a default arrives (§6.1.4), the **Poisson** for *how many* defaults arrive in a window (§6.6), the **Beta** for a recovery rate trapped between 0 and 100 cents (Chapter 5, §5.5), and **fat-tailed** distributions for losses that are far worse than any bell curve admits (Chapter 5, §5.7). The full version of the lognormal, with the exact mean and median, is in §6.1.2 (The Full Derivation).

Theo looks at the four endings for a while. "So the typical firm loses money even when the average one doesn't?"

"The typical *path* does," Kaz says. "Hold that thought; it comes back with a tax bill. First you need to know how far a path wanders."

> **Math, Gently.** *A random walk, and why everything scales with $\sqrt{t}$.* Flip a coin a hundred times; win \$1 on heads, lose \$1 on tails. How far from where you started do you typically end up? Not \$100, because the wins and losses mostly cancel. Not \$0 either. The answer is about \$10, which is $\sqrt{100}$. Each flip adds 1 to the *variance* (the average squared distance), so after $n$ flips the variance is $n$ and the typical distance, the standard deviation, is $\sqrt{n}$. That is a **random walk**: a running total of independent shocks. Brownian motion, the $W_t$ that appears in every model in this chapter, is a random walk with the flips made infinitely small and infinitely frequent.
>
> The desk consequence is immediate. Variance grows in proportion to time; volatility grows with the square root of time. A stock that moves 6% on a typical day does not move $6\%\times252$ in a year; it moves about $6\%\times\sqrt{252}\approx95\%$. That one conversion, from daily to annual by $\sqrt{252}$, is the most common silent error in structural credit (§6.1.3). The full version, where squared Brownian wiggles add up to exactly the elapsed time, is in §6.1.2 (The Full Derivation).

"A 6% day is a 95% year," Theo says, writing it down, because he can already see the spreadsheet where somebody forgot. Then he goes back to the coin flips. "But you still owe me a dollar. The average ended at 100 and the middle path at 99. Where did the dollar go?"

Kaz picks the marker up again. "To the tax man."

> **Math, Gently.** *Itô's $\tfrac12\sigma^2$: the volatility tax.* Go back to the two-step example and look at it in *log* returns, which is how compounding is measured. Up 10% is a log return of $\ln 1.1=+0.0953$; down 10% is $\ln 0.9=-0.1054$. The down move costs more than the up move earns. Average them and you get $-0.0050$ per step, almost exactly $-\tfrac12(0.10)^2=-0.005$. Two steps cost about $0.01$ of log value, and $100\times e^{-0.01}\approx99.0$: that is the median from the first box, recovered. **The 99 is the volatility tax.** It exists because a wiggle's square is always positive, whichever way the wiggle went: $(+0.1)^2$ and $(-0.1)^2$ are both $0.01$. Ordinary calculus throws squared changes away as too small to matter; with randomness they never cancel, they accumulate, and they leak out of the compounded return at a rate of $\tfrac12\sigma^2$ per unit time.
>
> In annual terms: a firm whose assets have volatility $\sigma_V=25\%$ pays a tax of $\tfrac12(0.25)^2=\tfrac12(0.0625)\approx3.1\%$ a year of log drift. If the assets are expected to grow 8% a year on average, the median path grows at about 4.9%. For a bondholder, whose problem is the bad paths, the median is the number that matters, and it is lower than the headline drift by exactly the tax. The full version, which is Itô's lemma, is in §6.1.2 (The Full Derivation).

Kaz's own summary, delivered at the whiteboard with the marker capped for emphasis, is the version the desk repeats. Itô's lemma is the tax code of stochastic calculus: the $\tfrac12\sigma_V^2$ is withheld at source from every log return, and the only refund is convexity. If you own a payoff with $f_{VV}>0$, the drift of your claim picks up $+\tfrac12\sigma_V^2V^2f_{VV}\,dt$. In a levered firm the equity holders own the convexity and the bondholders sold it to them, which is the entire Merton model in one sentence, previewed. ### 6.1.2 The Full Derivation: Itô's lemma

Let the firm's asset value follow geometric Brownian motion under the physical measure $\mathbb{P}$:

$$
dV_t=\mu V_t\,dt+\sigma_V V_t\,dW_t,
$$

where $\mu$ is the expected asset return (annualized), $\sigma_V$ is the asset volatility (annualized, decimal), and $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion with respect to the filtration $\mathcal{F}_t$. Take any smooth function $f(t,V)$ and expand it to second order:

$$
df=f_t\,dt+f_V\,dV+\tfrac12 f_{VV}\,(dV)^2+f_{tV}\,dt\,dV+\tfrac12 f_{tt}\,(dt)^2+\cdots
$$

The rules that make stochastic calculus different are the multiplication table $(dt)^2=0$, $dt\,dW_t=0$, and $(dW_t)^2=dt$. The last one is the surprise. Over $[0,T]$ split into $n$ steps of length $\Delta t=T/n$, the sum $\sum_i(\Delta W_i)^2$ has mean $\sum_i\Delta t=T$ and variance $\sum_i 2\Delta t^2=2T^2/n\to0$. So the sum of squared increments converges to the deterministic number $T$: Brownian motion's squared wiggles do not vanish, they add up to time itself. Hence

$$
(dV_t)^2=\sigma_V^2V_t^2\,(dW_t)^2=\sigma_V^2V_t^2\,dt,
$$

and the second-order term that ordinary calculus discards is now first order. Collecting terms gives **Itô's lemma**:

$$
df=\Big(f_t+\mu V f_V+\tfrac12\sigma_V^2V^2 f_{VV}\Big)dt+\sigma_V V f_V\,dW_t.
$$

Apply it to $f=\ln V$, with $f_V=1/V$, $f_{VV}=-1/V^2$, $f_t=0$:

$$
d\ln V_t=\Big(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2\Big)dt+\sigma_V\,dW_t,\qquad V_T=V_0\exp\!\Big[\big(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2\big)T+\sigma_V W_T\Big].
$$

So $\ln V_T$ is normal with mean $\ln V_0+(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2)T$ and variance $\sigma_V^2T$. The mean of $V_T$ is $V_0e^{\mu T}$, but the median is $V_0e^{(\mu-\frac12\sigma_V^2)T}$, lower by the factor $e^{-\frac12\sigma_V^2T}$.

That factor is the volatility tax, the same leak the two-step example in §6.1.1 showed in miniature: the mean grows at $\mu$, the median at $\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2$.

### 6.1.3 Annualize or die

The $\sigma_V$ in every formula in this chapter is annualized. Daily equity returns give a daily standard deviation; multiply by $\sqrt{252}$ (trading days) or, for weekly data, $\sqrt{52}$. The $\sqrt{\cdot}$ comes straight from $(dW)^2=dt$: variance scales with time, so volatility scales with its square root.

The trader who owns the name has already replied-all to say that if the bonds are now risk-free he would like to be paid like it. It takes the desk quant until lunch to find it. The equity-volatility file the engine reads every night comes from a vendor, and over the weekend the vendor "harmonized" its analytics feed so that every volatility field is reported at the frequency of the underlying data. ### 6.1.4 Column N, renamed

> **Math, Gently.** *A hazard rate, the exponential clock, and Poisson counts.* (Chapter 5, §5.1.5, first turned the thirteen-week model into a hazard; this is the two-minute refresher with the light switched on.) Picture a light bulb with a 2%-a-year failure rate. That 2% is a **hazard rate**: the chance of failing in the next small slice of time, *given that it has not failed yet*. It is not the chance of failing over its whole life. Survival is a product of many small "did not fail this slice" probabilities, and with a constant hazard that product is an exponential: survival to year 5 is $e^{-0.02\times5}=e^{-0.10}\approx90.5\%$, a little better than the $1-5\times2\%=90\%$ you would get by adding, because a bulb that fails in year 2 cannot fail again in year 3. The time until failure then has an **exponential** distribution, and the exponential has a strange and useful property: it is *memoryless*. A bulb that has burned for five years faces the same 2% next year as a new one. That is also exactly why ROBO, asked for "the default probability" without a horizon, returns 100%: a clock that never runs down rings eventually, and $1-e^{-0.02t}\to1$ as $t$ grows. Turn the question around and count instead of wait: if you own a warehouse of many such bulbs, the *number* that fail in a year follows a **Poisson** distribution, the shape of counts of rare arrivals at a known rate. Waiting times are exponential, counts are Poisson, and they are two views of one process (§6.6.2 builds default on exactly that object). The full version, the limit of Theo's weekly product, is the derivation that follows.

Return to Theo's spreadsheet. Column O holds the conditional probability $h_k$ of hitting the liquidity floor in week $k$ given survival through week $k-1$. The probability of surviving $k$ weeks is the product of the per-week survival probabilities:

$$
S(t_k)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(1-h_j),\qquad h_j=\lambda_j\Delta t,\quad \Delta t=\tfrac{1}{52}.
$$

Take logs and use $\ln(1-x)=-x+O(x^2)$:

$$
\ln S(t_k)=\sum_{j=1}^k\ln(1-\lambda_j\Delta t)=-\sum_{j=1}^k\lambda_j\Delta t+O(\Delta t)\;\longrightarrow\;-\int_0^{t}\lambda_s\,ds,
$$

so in the continuous limit $S(t)=\exp\!\big(-\int_0^t\lambda_s\,ds\big)$. The weekly hazard $\lambda_j$ is constant within each week: a piecewise-constant hazard curve, which is exactly the object a CDS bootstrap produces in §6.8. Theo renames column N from "Runway" to $S(t)$. Kaz updates the fonts. The thirteen-week model has been reborn as a survival curve; it was always one, it just did not know the Greek.

## 6.2 Merton 1974: Equity Is a Call, Debt Is a Short Put

### 6.2.1 Desk framing: every waterfall is a payoff diagram

Merton's firm has one asset worth $V_t$ and one zero-coupon debt claim with face value $F$ due at $T$. At maturity, if $V_T\ge F$ the debt is paid in full and equity keeps the rest; if $V_T<F$ the bondholders take the firm. So

$$
E_T=\max(V_T-F,0),\qquad B_T=\min(V_T,F)=F-\max(F-V_T,0).
$$

We write the market value of the debt claim as $B_t$ (B for bond). The style bible reserves $D$ for duration and $P(t,T)$ for the riskless discount factor, so neither is available.

> **Math, Gently.** *Payoff diagrams: the call, the put, and the hockey stick.* A payoff diagram is a picture of what you get paid (vertical axis) for each possible value of the thing underneath (horizontal axis). A **call** struck at $F$ is the right to buy at $F$: below $F$ it is worthless, so its picture lies flat along the floor; above $F$ it rises one-for-one, a straight line heading up and to the right. Flat, then a kink, then a ramp: a hockey stick lying on the ice with the blade pointing up. A **put** struck at $F$ is the right to sell at $F$: it is the mirror image, worth $F-V$ when $V$ is below $F$ and nothing above, a hockey stick with the blade pointing up to the left. Merton's observation is that a firm's balance sheet is drawn from these two shapes. Equity is the call. Debt is a flat line at $F$ (a riskless bond) with a put carved out of it below $F$, which is the same as saying the debt is $\min(V,F)$: a ramp that rises with the firm until it hits par, and then goes flat because bondholders never get more than they are owed.
>
> A tiny number makes it real. Let $F=80$. If the firm ends worth $V_T=90$, equity gets $90-80=10$ and the debt gets its full 80. If it ends worth $V_T=70$, equity gets nothing and the debt gets the whole 70. Compared with a riskless bond paying 80 either way, the bondholder lost 10 in the bad state, and 10 is exactly what a put struck at 80 pays when $V_T=70$. The bondholder **wrote a put** to the shareholders, collected a premium for it in the form of a credit spread, and paid out on it when the firm came in below par. Every structural model in this chapter is a way of pricing that put. The full version, with the pricing equation and closed forms, is in §6.2.2–§6.2.3.

Theo has drawn this picture a hundred times, labeled "Recovery by Class." Equity is a call struck at the debt. The debt is a riskless bond minus a put that the bondholders wrote to the shareholders. The fulcrum security, the class that converts into the new equity, is the tranche whose strike sits where the asset distribution has its mass: the class that is neither deep in the money (paid in full) nor so far out of the money that the plan gives it a tip. RSSG trained Theo to find the fulcrum by reading the waterfall. Merton finds it by reading the option.

Mags does not accept this on Thursday night, and the argument that follows is the most useful twenty minutes of the evening. Theo sketches an illustrative capital structure on the back of a dumpling menu: a \$400mm first-lien term loan, \$300mm of second-lien notes, \$250mm of unsecured notes, and a sell-side valuation of \$600mm *(illustrative)*. "Easy," he says. "First lien is covered, unsecureds are out of the money, second lien gets \$200mm of its \$300mm. Second lien is the fulcrum." Mags shakes her head without looking up. "You found the fulcrum at *one* valuation. Nobody in a restructuring has ever agreed on one valuation. The second lien's banker will tell the judge the company is worth \$750mm so his class gets paid and the unsecureds get a \$50mm tip. The first lien's banker will say \$350mm, and then the first lien is the fulcrum and the second lien gets warrants and a handshake. The fulcrum is not a point on your waterfall. It is wherever the fight is."

Kaz, who has been listening while pretending to tidy the whiteboard, writes three lines under Theo's sketch. The first-lien claim is $\min(V,400)$. The second-lien claim is $\max(V-400,0)-\max(V-700,0)$, a call spread struck at 400 and 700. The unsecured claim is a call spread struck at 700 and 950, and the equity is a call struck at 950. "So every class is a call spread on the same firm," Kaz says. "Mags's 'wherever the fight is' means: the tranche whose two strikes bracket most of the probability. If the valuation range runs from 350 to 750, most of the distribution of $V$ sits between 400 and 700, and the second-lien call spread is the one that moves dollar-for-dollar with the firm across that range. That's the fulcrum. It's not where the point estimate is. It's where the density is."

"Which is a fancier way of saying what I said," Mags says. Theo, who has spent six years watching ad hoc groups argue about exactly this, finds that he agrees with both of them, and writes in his notebook a sentence he will reuse at the risk committee: *the fulcrum is the strike under the mass.* Merton's contribution is to make "the mass" a number, by putting a distribution on $V$ and computing it. The waterfall says who gets paid at each valuation; the option says how much each valuation is worth, weighted by how likely it is. ### 6.2.2 Replication and the pricing PDE

The payoff diagram tells you what each claim is worth on the last day. The harder question, the one the market answers every second, is what it is worth *today*, when nobody knows where $V$ will end. The surprising answer, which earned Black, Scholes and Merton their reputations, is that you do not need to know how fast the firm is expected to grow. You need only its volatility and the cash rate. The idea that makes this possible fits in a box.

> **Math, Gently.** *No-arbitrage and "risk-neutral," in plain words.* Suppose you could hold a mix of the firm's equity and the firm's assets, rebalanced continuously, such that whatever the firm does over the next instant the mix neither gains nor loses. A combination that cannot lose is a savings account. A savings account must earn the cash rate, no more (or everyone would borrow cash to buy it) and no less (or everyone would sell it and lend the cash). That one sentence is **no-arbitrage**, and it does something remarkable: once the hedged mix is pinned to the cash rate, the firm's expected growth rate $\mu$ drops out of the price entirely. Whether you think the firm will grow 3% or 15% a year, the hedge costs the same to run, so the claim costs the same to buy.
>
> The price of a claim then *looks like* an average of its payoffs computed in an imaginary world where every asset grows at the cash rate. That imaginary world is the **risk-neutral measure** $\mathbb{Q}$, and its probabilities are not forecasts. They are prices in disguise. An analogy: an insurer that expects a 1% chance of a fire in a given year does not charge 1% of the house value. It charges, say, 1.5%, because fires cluster in bad years (dry summers, recessions, when its own capital is scarce) and the insurer needs to be paid for bearing a loss that arrives when it hurts most. Quote that 1.5% back as "the probability of a fire" and you have a $\mathbb{Q}$ probability: the true chance ($\mathbb{P}$, 1%) plus a premium for *when* the loss arrives. Credit spreads are priced the same way. The real-world default probability lives in $\mathbb{P}$; the price, and every probability backed out of a price, lives in $\mathbb{Q}$; the gap between them is the credit risk premium of §6.3. Chapter 6-B (§6-B.2) builds the same argument from a one-period, two-state example, if you want to watch it work with three numbers. The full version for Merton's equity follows.

#### The Full Derivation: the pricing PDE

Assume for now that $V_t$ is traded (Kaz: "assume it's traded"; Mags: "you cannot short a factory"; both are right, and §6.4 deals with the consequences). Form a portfolio long one unit of equity and short $\Delta$ units of the asset: $\Pi=E-\Delta V$. By Itô's lemma applied to $E(t,V)$,

$$
d\Pi=\Big(E_t+\mu VE_V+\tfrac12\sigma_V^2V^2E_{VV}-\Delta\mu V\Big)dt+\big(E_V-\Delta\big)\sigma_VV\,dW_t.
$$

Choose $\Delta=E_V$. The $dW_t$ term vanishes, so the portfolio is riskless and must earn the riskless rate, $d\Pi=r\Pi\,dt=r(E-VE_V)\,dt$. Equating drifts, the $\mu$ terms cancel and

$$
E_t+rVE_V+\tfrac12\sigma_V^2V^2E_{VV}-rE=0,
$$

with terminal condition $E(T,V)=\max(V-F,0)$ and boundary condition $E(t,0)=0$. This is the Black–Scholes PDE (Bohn and Stein give the same replication argument in their Appendix 3A, Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.206). The physical drift $\mu$ has disappeared: the price is the same as if assets grew at $r$, which is the statement that prices are expectations under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$. Default probabilities, which are about what will actually happen, still need $\mu$. Keeping those two facts in separate drawers is §6.3.

### 6.2.3 Closed forms

The solution is the Black–Scholes call on $V$:

$$
E_0=V_0N(d_1)-Fe^{-rT}N(d_2),\qquad d_1=\frac{\ln(V_0/F)+(r+\tfrac12\sigma_V^2)T}{\sigma_V\sqrt T},\qquad d_2=d_1-\sigma_V\sqrt T,
$$

where $N(\cdot)$ is the standard normal CDF.

> **Math, Gently.** *$N(d)$, $d_2$, and distance to default.* $N(x)$ is the share of a standard bell curve that lies to the left of the point $x$: $N(0)=50\%$, $N(1)\approx84\%$, $N(2)\approx97.7\%$. The quantity $d_2$ is a ruler reading. It measures, in standard deviations of the log asset value, how far the centre of the (risk-neutral) distribution of $\ln V_T$ sits above $\ln F$, the log of the strike. So $N(d_2)$ is the share of the bell to the right of the strike: the fraction of outcomes in which the firm ends above its debt and the bondholders are paid par. In the worked example of §6.2.4, $d_2=0.928$ and $N(0.928)\approx0.823$: about 82% of the outcomes pay par, and the remaining 18% are defaults. $N(d_1)$ is the same bell read one $\sigma_V\sqrt T$ further along, and it doubles as the hedge ratio, the number of units of the firm's assets that one unit of equity behaves like. **Distance to default** ($DD$, below) is the same ruler with the real-world drift $\mu$ in place of $r$: a $DD$ of 2 says the default point sits two standard deviations below where the assets are actually expected to end, and on a bell only about 2.3% of outcomes fall that far. Credit desks quote $DD$ the way doctors quote blood pressure: one number, imperfect, and alarming when it is low. The full version is the algebra in this section and the measure change in §6.3.

The debt is whatever is left:

$$
B_0=V_0-E_0=Fe^{-rT}N(d_2)+V_0N(-d_1)=Fe^{-rT}-\underbrace{\Big[Fe^{-rT}N(-d_2)-V_0N(-d_1)\Big]}_{\text{put on }V\text{ struck at }F}.
$$

Read the middle expression as a waterfall: with risk-neutral probability $N(d_2)$ the bondholders receive $F$, discounted; otherwise they receive the assets, whose risk-neutral value conditional on default contributes $V_0N(-d_1)$. The risk-neutral default probability is $\mathbb{Q}(V_T<F)=N(-d_2)$.

Two more results fall out of Itô. Applying the lemma to $E(t,V)$, the diffusion coefficient of equity is $\sigma_VVE_V=\sigma_VVN(d_1)$, so

$$
\sigma_EE_0=N(d_1)\,\sigma_VV_0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \sigma_E=\sigma_V\cdot\frac{V_0}{E_0}N(d_1).
$$

Equity volatility is asset volatility times a leverage multiplier. And under $\mathbb{P}$ the log asset value at $T$ is normal with mean $\ln V_0+(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2)T$, so the physical default probability is $N(-DD)$ with the **distance to default**

$$
DD=\frac{\ln(V_0/F)+(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2)T}{\sigma_V\sqrt T},
$$

the number of standard deviations of $\ln V_T$ separating the expected log asset value from the default point. Note that $d_2$ is $DD$ with $\mu$ replaced by $r$. Mixing the two inside one formula is the most common bug in Merton code that compiles.

### 6.2.4 The spread, a worked example, and the short-end embarrassment

The continuously compounded credit spread over the riskless rate is defined by $B_0=Fe^{-(r+s)T}$:

$$
s(T)=-\frac1T\ln\frac{B_0}{Fe^{-rT}}=-\frac1T\ln\!\Big[N(d_2)+\frac{N(-d_1)}{\ell}\Big],\qquad \ell=\frac{Fe^{-rT}}{V_0},
$$

where $\ell$ is quasi-leverage. Spread depends only on $\ell$, $\sigma_V\sqrt T$, and $T$: leverage and volatility are the two inputs that matter, as Bohn and Stein put it (p.137), "asset volatility is much more important to default prediction than is asset drift."

*Worked example (illustrative).* Take $V_0=100$, $F=80$, $r=4\%$, $\sigma_V=25\%$, $T=1$. Then $\ln(1.25)=0.2231$, $d_1=(0.2231+0.0713)/0.25=1.178$, $d_2=0.928$, $N(d_1)=0.881$, $N(d_2)=0.823$, and $Fe^{-rT}=76.86$. Equity is $E_0\approx 88.05-63.27=24.78$; debt is $B_0\approx75.22$; the spread is $s\approx-\ln(75.22/76.86)\approx 2.16\%$, or about 216 bp; and $\mathbb{Q}(\tau\le1)=N(-0.928)\approx17.7\%$. The implied equity volatility is $\sigma_E\approx0.25\times(100/24.78)\times0.881\approx89\%$, a leverage multiplier of about 3.6. With $\mu=8\%$, $DD\approx1.09$ and the physical PD is about 13.8%.

Look at what the model says about recovery. A 17.7% risk-neutral default probability and a 216 bp spread imply a risk-neutral loss given default of roughly $0.0216/0.177\approx12\%$: Merton's defaulted firm recovers about 88 cents. That is because default in a diffusion model happens to firms that missed par *by a little*, with $V_T$ just under $F$. Mags's defaulted firms miss by a lot, then pay advisers for eighteen months. Endogenous recovery that is too high is the first reason pure Merton spreads come out too tight.

The second reason is the short end. For $V_0>F$, as $T\downarrow0$, $d_2\approx\ln(V_0/F)/(\sigma_V\sqrt T)\to+\infty$. The Mills-ratio bound $N(-x)\le\phi(x)/x$ means $N(-d_2)$ decays like $\exp(-c/T)$, faster than any power of $T$, and $N(-d_1)$ does the same. So the bracket in $s(T)$ tends to 1 and

$$
\lim_{T\downarrow0}s(T)=0.
$$

A Merton firm cannot default tomorrow unless it is already dead today. Mags has a list of firms that disagree, alphabetized. In the worked example the three-month Merton spread is about 70 bp and the two-and-a-half-week spread is under 1 bp, while CCC paper of any tenor trades hundreds of basis points wide. Duffie and Lando (2001, p.634) state the problem precisely: "With perfect information, yield spreads for surviving firms are zero at zero maturity, and are relatively small for small maturities, regardless of the riskiness of the firm." Their fix, noisy accounting information, is the bridge to the reduced-form models of §6.6: if investors cannot see exactly how close $V_t$ is to the barrier, default arrives as a surprise with an intensity, and short spreads become strictly positive. ## 6.3 $\mathbb{P}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$: The Credit Risk Premium

### 6.3.1 Desk framing: what happens versus what it costs to find out

RSSG trained Theo in $\mathbb{P}$. A recovery analysis asks what will actually happen: how much EBITDA, what multiple, which classes get paid. A CDS quote lives in $\mathbb{Q}$: it is the price at which someone will take the other side of that question, and that someone wants paying for the risk that the answer arrives in a recession, when everything else in their book is also losing money.

> **Poker Table.** Ines draws two columns on a napkin: "$\mathbb{P}$: what happens. $\mathbb{Q}$: what the table charges you to find out." Physical PDs are the true odds of the hand. Risk-neutral PDs are the odds implied by the price of the bet, and they are always worse for the buyer of protection, because default risk is concentrated in states of the world where everyone's chips are worth more. "The difference is the rake," she says. "And on this desk, we are the house." Then the hand history. Theo's first week: a vendor model gave a name a 2% one-year $\mathbb{P}$-PD, the CDS curve implied a 3.4% $\mathbb{Q}$ hazard, and Theo wanted to sell protection because "the market is too scared." The twenty-day markout was flat. Nothing was mispriced. He had rediscovered the risk premium, which is not alpha. It is carry with a jump in it, and the jump is what you are paid to own. *(illustrative)*

### 6.3.2 The measure change

Ines's napkin has the right two columns, but it does not say how to get from one to the other. Theo's instinct, from his banking years, is that $\mathbb{Q}$ must be a different *story* about the firm: gloomier forecasts, worse EBITDA, a lower multiple. It is not. It is the same story told with loaded dice.

> **Math, Gently.** *Girsanov, or loading the dice without changing how hard they are thrown.* Under $\mathbb{P}$ the firm's log asset value drifts upward at its real expected rate and wiggles with volatility $\sigma_V$. To price claims, §6.2.2 showed you can pretend the assets grow at the cash rate $r$ instead. Girsanov's theorem says that this pretend world is a legitimate probability world with the *same* wiggle: the dice are thrown exactly as hard, the volatility $\sigma_V$ is untouched, and only the tilt changes, from $\mu$ down to $r$. Because the centre of the bell for $\ln V_T$ slides toward the default point while its width stays put, more of the bell ends up below the strike, and every default probability computed under $\mathbb{Q}$ comes out higher than under $\mathbb{P}$. How far does the centre slide? By $(\mu-r)/\sigma_V$ standard deviations per unit of $\sqrt{T}$, a number called the *market price of risk*. In the §6.2.4 firm, $\mu=8\%$, $r=4\%$ and $\sigma_V=25\%$, so the slide is $0.04/0.25=0.16$. Distance to default falls from about 1.09 to about 0.93, and the one-year default probability rises from about 13.8% to about 17.7%. Nothing about the firm changed. What changed is that the second number includes the price of bearing the first one. The full version is the derivation that follows.

#### The Full Derivation: the measure change

Girsanov's theorem says that if we define

$$
dW^{\mathbb{Q}}_t=dW_t+\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma_V}\,dt,
$$

then $W^{\mathbb{Q}}$ is a Brownian motion under a measure $\mathbb{Q}$ equivalent to $\mathbb{P}$, and substituting into the asset dynamics,

$$
dV_t=\mu V_t\,dt+\sigma_VV_t\Big(dW^{\mathbb{Q}}_t-\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma_V}dt\Big)=rV_t\,dt+\sigma_VV_t\,dW^{\mathbb{Q}}_t.
$$

The volatility is unchanged; only the drift moves, from $\mu$ to $r$. The quantity $(\mu-r)/\sigma_V$ is the asset's market price of risk. To pin it down, Bohn and Stein (Appendix 3B) use the CAPM: $\mu-r=\beta_A(\mu_M-r)$ with asset beta $\beta_A=\rho\,\sigma_V/\sigma_M$, where $\rho$ is the correlation of asset returns with the market and $\sigma_M$ is market volatility. Write the market Sharpe ratio as $\text{SR}_M=(\mu_M-r)/\sigma_M$ (not $\lambda$, which this book reserves for default intensity). Then $(\mu-r)/\sigma_V=\rho\,\text{SR}_M$ and, comparing $d_2$ with $DD$ from §6.2.3,

$$
DD^{\mathbb{Q}}=DD^{\mathbb{P}}-\rho\,\text{SR}_M\sqrt T,\qquad \text{PD}^{\mathbb{Q}}=N\!\Big(N^{-1}\big(\text{PD}^{\mathbb{P}}\big)+\rho\,\text{SR}_M\sqrt T\Big).
$$

Because $N$ is increasing and the shift is positive whenever $\rho>0$, $\text{PD}^{\mathbb{Q}}>\text{PD}^{\mathbb{P}}$.

#### What the shift does to a risky firm and a safe one

*Worked example (illustrative).* In the §6.2.4 firm, $\mu-r=4\%$ and $\sigma_V=25\%$ give a market price of risk of $0.16$, which is what $\rho=0.4$ and $\text{SR}_M=0.4$ deliver. The one-year shift is $0.16$: $DD^{\mathbb{P}}\approx1.09$ becomes $DD^{\mathbb{Q}}\approx0.93=d_2$, and the PD rises from about 13.8% to about 17.7%, a ratio of 1.3. Now take a safe issuer with $DD^{\mathbb{P}}=3.0$. The same shift moves the PD from $N(-3.0)\approx0.135\%$ to $N(-2.84)\approx0.226\%$, a ratio of 1.7. The same additive shift in DD space is a larger *multiplicative* shift for safer names, because the normal tail is steeper further out. That is the structural-model version of a stylized fact every IG trader knows: investment-grade spreads are many multiples of expected loss, while distressed spreads are closer to it. Note also the $\sqrt T$: the premium grows with horizon, so five-year $\mathbb{Q}$ PDs are further from $\mathbb{P}$ than one-year PDs.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.210:* "The risk-neutral DD is smaller than the physical DD, reflecting the adjustment that results in a higher PD, all else equal."
>
> **Annotation.** The adjustment is one line of algebra; getting it wrong is a career. Two errors recur on desks. First, double counting: applying the $\mathbb{P}\to\mathbb{Q}$ shift to a PD that was *already* backed out of spreads, so the premium is charged twice and every bond looks rich. Tag every PD field with its measure in the schema, not in a comment. Second, sign confusion in the reverse direction: going from a CDS-implied hazard to a $\mathbb{P}$-PD for a loss forecast requires *subtracting* the premium, and the premium is not observable, so the honest output is a range. Where the 2026 desk departs from the textbook shift: the CAPM diffusion premium is too small to explain investment-grade spreads, as the next paragraph explains, so a desk that uses only this adjustment will think IG credit is persistently cheap and will be persistently wrong about why.

### 6.3.3 The credit spread puzzle, and why the premium has a jump in it

Kaz brings the puzzle to the desk the following Tuesday, holding two printouts like a referee holding two red cards. "This one," Kaz says, tapping the first, "says there is no puzzle. Calibrate the structural model properly to a long enough default history and it fits investment-grade spreads. If anything it underprices high yield, and the leftover is illiquidity." Theo asks which one is right. "This one, obviously," Kaz says, and starts reading him the second paper's abstract to show why it is wrong, and then reads its calibration section, and then goes quiet for a while. "Although," Kaz says, "if you measure leverage with the market value of debt instead of book, firms are more levered in exactly the bad states, and then the asset dynamics don't look lognormal, and once you let the assets jump you need a big premium on the jumps to match the spreads, and the gap is mostly in investment grade, which is where the first paper said there wasn't one." By the time Ines wanders over, Kaz is defending the second paper with the same conviction they had brought to the first. "You've changed sides," Ines says. "I've updated," Kaz says. Mags, from across the aisle: "In my day we called that being wrong twice and charging for both." Theo, who has watched two expert witnesses do exactly this to a valuation in a contested confirmation hearing, notes that the papers are not really disagreeing about the world. They are disagreeing about which number to put in for leverage and which shape to allow for the shocks, which is the kind of disagreement that never ends in a winner, only in a desk that knows which assumption it is making.

Calibrate a diffusion structural model to historical default rates and ask what spread it implies for a BBB bond. For two decades the answer was "much less than the market spread," which became known as the credit spread puzzle. Feldhütter and Schaefer (2018), *The Myth of the Credit Spread Puzzle* (Review of Financial Studies 31(8)), argued that once you calibrate Black–Cox carefully to a long default history, it fits investment-grade spreads and actually *under*-prices high yield, partly because of illiquidity. Bai, Goldstein, and Yang (2020), *Is the Credit Spread Puzzle a Myth?* (Journal of Financial Economics 137(2)), reversed the conclusion when calibrating to market rather than book debt values, rejected geometric Brownian motion for asset value, and found that a large jump-risk premium is needed: the puzzle is real, and it lives in investment grade. The desk takeaway does not depend on who wins. The $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{P}$ gap has a diffusive part, which the formula above captures, and a jump part, which it does not. The jump part is the premium for owning the Tuesday on which a BBB name announces an accounting restatement. It is paid most generously in the names where it looks least necessary.

## 6.4 KMV and Distance to Default: The Inverse Problem

### 6.4.1 Desk framing: two equations, two unknowns, one deadline

Merton needs $V_0$ and $\sigma_V$. Neither is on a screen. What is on a screen is the stock price, from which the desk computes market capitalization $E_0$ and realized or implied equity volatility $\sigma_E$. The KMV insight (Vasicek and Kealhofer, commercialized by KMV and later Moody's) is to treat the Merton equity formula and the Itô volatility link as two equations in the two unknowns, solve them for each firm every day, and then throw away the Gaussian part of the model at the last step.

Theo asked ROBO to "compute distance to default." ROBO returned 1,420 miles: approximately the great-circle distance from the desk in Manhattan to Houston, seat of the Southern District of Texas bankruptcy court. It was not a bad guess about where the firm was headed. The precise spec gives units: *distance to default is measured in standard deviations of the log asset value at a one-year horizon.*

### 6.4.2 The solver

The system is

$$
g_1(V_0,\sigma_V)=V_0N(d_1)-Fe^{-rT}N(d_2)-E_0=0,\qquad g_2(V_0,\sigma_V)=N(d_1)\,\sigma_VV_0-\sigma_EE_0=0.
$$

In words: find the asset value and asset volatility that, plugged into Merton, reproduce today's market cap *and* today's equity volatility. There is no formula that hands you the answer. You have to search for it, and the search method every desk uses is three hundred years old.

> **Math, Gently.** *Newton's method and the Jacobian.* Newton's method is "guess, see how wrong you are, and slide downhill along the slope." Suppose you want $\sqrt2$, the number $x$ with $x^2-2=0$. Guess $x=1$. The error is $1^2-2=-1$, and the slope of $x^2-2$ at $x=1$ is $2$, so move by $-(-1)/2=+0.5$ to $x=1.5$. Now the error is $0.25$ and the slope is $3$, so move by $-0.25/3$ to $x\approx1.4167$. One more step gives $1.41422$, correct to five digits. Each step roughly doubles the number of correct digits, which is why Newton is the default for any smooth equation you need solved fast and often. With two unknowns and two equations, the "slope" becomes a small table, the **Jacobian**: a grid saying how much each output moves per unit change in each input. For KMV the outputs are "error in market cap" and "error in equity volatility," and the inputs are asset value and asset volatility, so the Jacobian is a $2\times2$ table and each Newton step solves a pair of simultaneous linear equations instead of dividing by one number. The method has one well-known weakness: where the slope is nearly flat, "slide downhill by the slope" means "jump to somewhere absurd," and that is exactly what happens to KMV when equity is almost worthless (the Ways This Blows Up box in §6.4.3). The full version, with the table written out, follows.

#### The Full Derivation: the Jacobian and the starting point

Solve on log-parameters $z=(\ln V_0,\ln\sigma_V)$ so that positivity is automatic. The Jacobian uses three Black–Scholes partials: $\partial E/\partial V=N(d_1)$, vega $\partial E/\partial\sigma_V=V\phi(d_1)\sqrt T$, and $\partial d_1/\partial\sigma_V=-d_2/\sigma_V$, where $\phi$ is the standard normal density. With $\partial/\partial z_1=V\,\partial/\partial V$ and $\partial/\partial z_2=\sigma_V\,\partial/\partial\sigma_V$,

$$
J=\begin{pmatrix} VN(d_1) & \sigma_VV\phi(d_1)\sqrt T\\[4pt] \sigma_VVN(d_1)+V\phi(d_1)/\sqrt T & \sigma_VV\big[N(d_1)-\phi(d_1)\,d_2\big]\end{pmatrix}.
$$

A good starting point is $V_0^{(0)}=E_0+Fe^{-rT}$ and $\sigma_V^{(0)}=\sigma_EE_0/V_0^{(0)}$, the "equity plus book debt" guess. Newton converges in a handful of steps for ordinary firms.

#### The Vasicek–Kealhofer iteration

The **Vasicek–Kealhofer (VK) iteration** avoids trusting a single $\sigma_E$, which is nonstationary whenever leverage changes. Take about three years (156 weeks) of equity values $E_t$. Guess $\sigma_V$. For each week, invert the call formula $E_t=C(V_t;\sigma_V)$ for $V_t$. Compute the weekly log asset returns, annualize their standard deviation to get a new $\sigma_V$, and repeat until it stops moving. Bohn and Stein describe the key step:

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.169:* "We then run the equity value equation backwards to determine an implied asset value."
>
> **Annotation.** "Run the equation backwards" is the whole trick, and it is cheap because the call price is strictly increasing and convex in $V$, so Newton's method from the right of the root converges monotonically and can be vectorized across all 156 weeks at once (§6.11). The VK loop then extracts $\sigma_V$ from a *time series* of implied asset values rather than from today's equity volatility, which matters because, as Bohn and Stein note on p.136, "a firm can have stationary asset volatility and still have nonstationary equity volatility as the firm changes its leverage over time." A leveraged recapitalization doubles equity vol overnight without changing the business. Where the 2026 desk departs: the 156-week window is too slow for a name in an active liability-management process, where the capital structure itself changes month to month. Desks recompute $F$ at every 8-K and use implied equity vol from listed options, when they exist, as a faster input (Chapter 23).

**Quant-shop life (fictional composite): Monday, why is this number different from yesterday?** The setting is the credit team of a systematic asset manager, the kind of shop where an overnight distance-to-default job feeds a long-only corporate bond portfolio that rebalances at noon. Theo is on a rotation there for the quarter, and this month the job is his. At 6:45 a.m. the exception report is waiting: 47 of 3,100 issuers flagged, each one a row whose DD moved by more than a threshold, or whose solver exited badly, or whose inputs failed a check. The portfolio manager will not touch the rebalance until every row has an explanation, and the risk committee, which meets on Wednesdays, asks the same question about any number that moved: *why is this different from yesterday?* Nobody on the team calls this modelling. They call it "the explain," and it is most of the job.

Theo starts sorting. Thirty-one of the 47 are stale equity: names that did not trade on Friday, a halted stock awaiting a merger vote, two post-reorg equities that print by appointment, a Canadian listing whose vendor feed carried Thursday's close forward. Their DDs did not move because the firms moved; they moved because the volatility estimator, fed a run of unchanged prices and then one real trade, briefly concluded the stock had become either a bond or a lottery ticket. The rule for those is mechanical: carry forward the last good DD with a staleness flag and a counter. Twelve are solver guards. In each, equity is small relative to debt, $N(d_1)$ is near zero, Newton has walked to the $\sigma_V=3$ bound, and the engine, correctly, has refused to report the answer. For those, the fallback is Bharath and Shumway's naive DD (§6.4.3), which never fails because it never solves anything, and the flag says so. The last four are the interesting ones. Each issuer's default point jumped by 15% to 30% overnight, and all four had filed a 10-K in the past week. The vendor's "total debt" field now includes operating lease liabilities that were previously excluded. The firms did not borrow anything. The measurement of $F$ changed under them.

Those four take the rest of the morning, because they are not errors. Bohn and Stein say off-balance-sheet liabilities "should be included in the DD calculation" (p.159), so the new numbers are arguably *more* right than the old ones, and the honest explain is "a step change in measurement, not a change in credit; not news." Theo writes that sentence four times, with the lease amounts from each 10-K beside it, and then writes the paragraph the committee will actually read: what changed, why, which way, and whether to blend the new default points in over a month or take them at once. The firm is not a bank, so the Federal Reserve's model-risk guidance does not bind it by statute. But its largest pension client's due-diligence questionnaire is lifted almost word for word from that guidance, including the line that vendor products are in scope and that where a vendor's internals are closed a firm "may have to rely more on sensitivity analysis and benchmarking" (SR 11-7 attachment, PDF pp.15–16). So the explain also includes a benchmark: the overnight DD ranks against the vendor's own default-probability ranks, which move together with a rank correlation the team checks every day, that nobody has asked about in years, and that everyone will ask about the day it falls.

Kaz, passing with coffee, reads over his shoulder and tells him about the most famous spreadsheet in credit. When the U.S. Senate investigated JPMorgan's 2012 synthetic credit losses, it found that the author of a new value-at-risk model "had to manually enter data into multiple spreadsheets each trading day, which often took hours," and that an error in those spreadsheets "likely had the effect of muting volatility by a factor of two" (U.S. Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, *JPMorgan Chase Whale Trades: A Case History of Derivatives Risks and Abuses*, 15 March 2013, pp.185–86, quoting the bank's own task-force report). "Nobody was doing anything exotic," Kaz says. "They were doing what you're doing, by hand, and nobody was asking what you're answering." At 11:20 Theo sends the explain. At 11:35 the PM replies "ok." The rebalance runs at noon. The morning ended before lunch for one reason, and it was not the solver: it was a naive formula sitting underneath the solver, waiting to catch the twelve names where the clever version fell over. A solver that fails silently on 3% of the universe would have cost the team the day. A solver that fails *loudly*, with a fallback and a flag, costs a coffee.

### 6.4.3 The default point and the empirical mapping

KMV does not set the default point to total face value. The convention (Crosbie and Bohn 2003, *Modeling Default Risk*, Moody's KMV) is

$$
X=\text{STD}+\tfrac12\,\text{LTD},
$$

short-term debt plus half of long-term debt, after removing items such as deferred taxes and minority interest and adding material off-balance-sheet liabilities. Bohn and Stein are explicit that "large levels of off-balance-sheet liabilities can make a difference and should be included in the DD calculation" (p.159), a sentence that will reappear, with feeling, in the First Brands case of §6.9. The reported distance to default is the simplified one-year version

$$
DD^{\text{KMV}}=\frac{V_0-X}{V_0\,\sigma_V},
$$

which for $V_0$ near $X$ is close to $\ln(V_0/X)/\sigma_V$, the Merton DD with the drift dropped (drift matters little at one year; volatility matters a lot).

Then comes the step that makes KMV a commercial product rather than a homework exercise: the DD is *not* pushed through $N(-\cdot)$. It is mapped to an Expected Default Frequency (EDF) through an empirical, monotone table estimated from historical defaults, sorted into cohorts by DD.

> **Math, Gently.** *An empirical, monotone mapping: the staircase that never goes up.* Forget the bell curve for a moment and do what an actuary would. Take every firm-year in a long history, say 25,000 firms over many years. Sort them into buckets by their DD at the start of the year: 0 to 0.5, 0.5 to 1, and so on. In each bucket, count who defaulted within the year and divide by the bucket size. You might find 40 defaults among 200 firm-years with DD below 0.5 (20%), 50 among 500 in the next bucket (10%), and, far out, 3 among 15,000 firm-years with DD above 4 (0.02%, or 2 bp) *(illustrative)*. That last number is about six times what the normal distribution says a DD of 4 deserves ($N(-4)\approx0.003\%$), and it is the empirical fact that made KMV a business. Now draw the counts as a staircase from left to right. Because more distance from default cannot sensibly mean *more* default, the staircase is allowed only to step down as DD rises. With noisy counts it sometimes will (a bucket at DD 3.0 to 3.5 shows 1.1% while its neighbour at 2.5 to 3.0 shows 0.9%), and the fix, called **isotonic regression**, is to merge any adjacent buckets that break the rule into one step with their pooled rate. The result is a table that respects the ordering the model is good at and lets the data, not the Gaussian, set the probabilities. The full version is the paragraph and the source extraction that follow.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.160:* "Empirical research, however, found that nearly 60 percent of its sample of over 25,000 firms have DDs that are 4 or greater. Recall that under the normal distribution, a DD of 4 is nearly equal to zero."
>
> **Annotation.** Under the normal, $N(-4)\approx3\times10^{-5}$, so a Gaussian mapping would assign effectively zero default probability to most of the corporate universe, which then defaults at a few basis points a year anyway. The fix is an empirical mapping, and Bohn and Stein are blunt about the data required: "At a bare minimum, a sample (ideally unbiased) of 400 defaults in a range of credit qualities is needed," with "at least 1,000 defaults" recommended (p.161). The desk's own DD-to-PD table is an isotonic fit on half-sigma DD buckets with at least that many events, and its spec stops ROBO at the last bucket instead of letting it extrapolate the Gaussian tail. The deeper point, which Bharath and Shumway made empirically, is that DD is mostly a *ranking* variable. The structure of the formula (leverage scaled by volatility) carries the information; the probability scale comes from data.

Bharath and Shumway (2008), *Forecasting Default with the Merton Distance to Default Model* (Review of Financial Studies 21(3)), showed that a "naive" DD that skips the solver entirely forecasts default about as well as the iterated one. Their naive asset volatility is a weighted average of equity volatility and a debt volatility proxy:

$$
\sigma_V^{\text{naive}}=\frac{E_0}{E_0+F}\,\sigma_E+\frac{F}{E_0+F}\big(0.05+0.25\,\sigma_E\big),
$$

with $V_0\approx E_0+F$ and the past equity return standing in for $\mu$. Most of the signal is in the *functional form*, which is why this formula, and not the solver, rescued Theo's Monday morning: it caught the twelve names where Newton walked to the bound.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** *The KMV solver in distress.* When $E_0\ll F$, $N(d_1)\to0$, the second row of the Jacobian collapses, and Newton wanders off to $\sigma_V=4$ or $V_0<E_0$. Guards: bound $\sigma_V\in[0.01,3]$, require $V_0\ge E_0$, check that the residuals are actually small rather than trusting the solver's exit code, and fall back to the naive DD with a flag when any guard trips (Monday's twelve rows, §6.4.2). Stale equity, the other thirty-one rows, fails more quietly: a stock that trades by appointment has a realized $\sigma_E$ that is mostly bid-ask bounce. Financials fail outright, because their "debt" is deposits and their assets are not lognormal at all; that is item 10 on the consolidated list (§6.12), and the next case.

On the Wednesday after the Monday explain, the risk committee asks Theo the question the explain had been written to survive: what is the default probability on the four lease names now? He answers with a rank and a bucket instead, and says why. As a *rank*, a DD has earned its trust: across operating companies with traded stocks, sorting by leverage over volatility is one of the most robust signals in credit, it survived Bharath and Shumway's attempt to replace the solver with a back-of-the-envelope formula, and it degrades gracefully when the inputs are a little stale. As a *probability*, it is only as good as the table behind it, and the table needs the 400-default floor quoted above. The team's ten years of a few hundred high-yield issuers is not that table, so a PD printed to two decimals would be a claim the data cannot back. The chair asks what would change his mind. Theo points at Monday's rows: equity that actually trades (the 31 stale names), a default point measured the way the table measured it (the four lease names), and a firm of the kind the table was fitted on. The last is why the most widely used empirical tables leave banks out, and it is the one the next case is about.

### 6.4.4 Case Study: Silicon Valley Bank, March 2023, and why banks break Merton

The cold open left Theo on a Thursday night with one monitor showing a company that sells things and another showing a bank whose stock had just lost sixty percent in a session. This is the story of that bank's last two weeks as a going concern, told the way the desk lived it: in five dated beats, with the cast arguing, the tools giving answers, and the answers turning out to be about the wrong question. The facts come from the Federal Reserve's own post-mortem (Board of Governors, *Review of the Federal Reserve's Supervision and Regulation of Silicon Valley Bank*, 28 April 2023, hereafter "the Fed review"), SVB Financial Group's FY2022 10-K and its March 2023 8-Ks; market colour from secondary sources is marked as such. The dialogue is invented and belongs to the fictional cast.

#### Scene A: The week the strike came due

*Beat 1. Late February 2023: the DD that said 4.* SVB Financial Group files its annual report on Friday, 24 February. Kaz's overnight structural run does not, strictly, include banks; the empirical mapping table excludes them, for reasons §6.4.3 has just finished explaining. But the run computes a raw DD for every listed issuer with bonds outstanding "for completeness," and on the Monday after the 10-K, the desk's fictional overnight run puts SIVB's raw DD at about 4 *(illustrative)*. It is a comfortable number. The inputs are not mysterious. The market capitalization is in the mid-teens of billions of dollars, the default point is the bank's liabilities, and the equity volatility fed to the solver is the stock's 30-day implied volatility, which for most of the previous year had been sitting near the bottom of its range; a vendor's pre-market options report published on 10 March would later put the floor of SIVB's 52-week implied-volatility range at 39 (Market Rebellion, pre-market IV report, 10 March 2023). Divide that equity volatility by a leverage multiplier of more than ten and you get an asset volatility of a few percent, and a few percent of asset volatility makes almost any cushion look wide. Nobody else is worried either. On 1 March, the sell-side analysts covering the stock rate it 12 Buy, 11 Hold and 1 Sell, and the rating agencies have held their ratings steady since 2015; Moody's last changed its rating of SVB Financial Group in 2007 (Fed review, pp.26–27).

Mags, who reads 10-Ks the way other people read detective novels, reads this one on the Saturday. On Monday she drops a printout on Theo's desk with four numbers circled in red. The held-to-maturity securities portfolio is carried at an amortized cost of \$91.3 billion; its fair value, disclosed a few pages later, is \$76.2 billion. That is a \$15.2 billion unrealized loss against total stockholders' equity of \$16.0 billion (computed from the SVBFG FY2022 10-K, filed 24 February 2023). "Mark the HTM book," Mags says, "and the equity is a rounding error. They just haven't been asked to mark it." The Fed's review would later put the same picture into a peer table: securities were 55% of SVB's assets against 25% for large-bank peers; held-to-maturity securities were 78% of its securities against 42%; and uninsured deposits were 94% of its deposits against 41% (Fed review, Table 1, 2022:Q4). The weighted-average duration of the HTM portfolio at year-end was 6.2 years (10-K, p.66). In bank language: a long-duration bond fund, funded by money that could leave on a phone call, run by a firm whose accountants were allowed not to notice the first problem until the second one arrived.

Theo reads the printout twice. "So the equity is a call on assets that are mostly fixed-rate bonds, struck at liabilities that are mostly demand deposits."

"So the asset is not lognormal," Kaz says, "and the strike does not have a maturity. It has a door."

Nobody acts. There is nothing to act on: the desk does not trade bank bonds as a systematic sleeve, there is no single-name CDS on SVB Financial Group on any screen anyone can find, and a 10-K that discloses a hole is not the same as a market that has priced one. Mags puts the printout in a folder labelled *BANKS – NOT MY PROBLEM YET*. The folder is three inches thick.

*Beat 2. Wednesday 8 March: the bonds they could sell.* After the close, SVB Financial Group announces a balance-sheet restructuring. It has sold about \$21 billion of available-for-sale securities, booking an after-tax loss of about \$1.8 billion; it plans to raise \$2.25 billion of capital in common stock and mandatory convertible preferred, including \$500 million from General Atlantic; and it discloses that Moody's and S&P are considering negative rating actions (Fed review, pp.4–5; SVBFG 8-K, 8 March 2023). A later 8-K names the buyer of the securities as Goldman Sachs and the book value sold as about \$23.97 billion, for net proceeds of about \$21.45 billion (SVBFG 8-K, 14 March 2023): a loss of roughly 10.5% of book on paper that had been bought as risk-free. The same evening, Silvergate announces that it is winding down (Fed review, p.24).

Ines reads the release standing up, which is how she reads anything that might move a market. "They sold the bonds they were allowed to sell, at a loss, to tell you about the bonds they weren't allowed to sell." She does the arithmetic out loud. If a sale of the *available-for-sale* book costs 10.5 points, the much larger held-to-maturity book, which has longer duration, is under water by more, and every depositor with a Bloomberg terminal, or a friend with one, can do the same subtraction. Kaz reruns the raw DD with the new equity price indicated after hours. It falls, but not by much. "The solver doesn't know what a depositor is," Kaz says. "It thinks the problem is that the stock went down."

*Beat 3. Thursday 9 March: not a diffusion.* This is the day of the cold open. The stock closes at \$106.04, down from \$267.83 the day before, a fall of about 60% *(approx.; secondary sources)*. What nobody on the floor knows at 11:52 p.m. is what is happening inside the bank. Depositors have withdrawn more than \$40 billion in a single day, and that evening and the next morning management tells its supervisors to expect more than \$100 billion more on Friday; together, the Fed review says, that was "roughly 85 percent of the bank's deposit base" (Fed review, p.5). For scale, the Fed review sets this beside two famous runs of the 2008 crisis: Wachovia lost about \$10 billion over eight days, and Washington Mutual \$19 billion over sixteen. SVB lost twice WaMu's total in one business day and was lining up to lose more than five times WaMu's total on the next.

The options market, the one place where a probability of disaster is quoted continuously, wakes up only now. The same pre-market report that recorded the 39 floor would record SIVB's 30-day implied volatility at 167 on the morning of 10 March, inside a 52-week range whose top was 280 (same report). Kaz feeds 167 into the solver and gets a DD below 1. Then Kaz sits back, looks at the two monitors, and says the most useful sentence of the week.

"That is not a diffusion. That is $F$'s maturity jumping to zero."

Theo asks Kaz to say it again, slower, for the notebook. Merton's debt is due at $T$, and the model's whole apparatus, the call option, the volatility, the distance to default, measures the chance that assets wander below the strike *by then*. A bank's deposits are due *whenever the depositor asks*. On a normal day they behave like long-dated funding, because most depositors never ask. On 9 March the effective maturity of \$40 billion of liabilities went from "years, in practice" to "today," and the strike went from being a number far below the assets to being a number the bank had to pay in cash it did not have. A diffusion calibrated to 39% equity volatility had assigned that day a probability indistinguishable from zero, not because the volatility estimate was a little low, but because the event was not a large move in $V$ at all. It was a change in the contract.

*Beat 4. Friday 10 March: the holdco at 40.* Before the open, trading in the stock is halted. By midday the California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation has closed Silicon Valley Bank, which had about \$209 billion of total assets at year-end, and appointed the FDIC as receiver (FDIC press release, 10 March 2023). SVB Financial Group's 8-K that day says it plainly: "The Company is no longer the parent company of the Bank" (SVBFG 8-K, 10 March 2023). The capital raise is terminated. What is left at the holding company is a pile of claims and a few businesses: SVB Capital, SVB Securities, some cash and securities, and \$3.3 billion of senior unsecured notes in seven tranches, from a \$350 million 3.50% note due January 2025 to a \$450 million 4.570% fixed-to-floating note due 2033 (SVBFG FY2022 10-K, Note 15; SVBFG 8-K, 22 March 2023). #### Looking back: what the tools say

Three answers, from three angles, and each is right about something.

**What the structural model saw.** An equity-driven, Merton-type vendor PD flagged the bank well before the run: S&P Global Market Intelligence reported that its Market Signal PD for SVB first jumped in September 2022, from about 1.1% to 2.5% in ten days, and peaked at about 6% on December 6, 2022, roughly five times the US regional-bank median of 1.27% (as reported by the vendor in a blog post; figures not independently verified this edition). Equity fell, equity volatility rose, DD fell. The model fired.

**What the structural model could not see.** Nagel and Purnanandam (2020), *Banks' Risk Dynamics and Distance to Default* (Review of Financial Studies 33(6); NBER working paper w25807), explain why a bank is not a Merton firm. A bank's assets are themselves loans and bonds, which are short puts on *borrowers'* assets. So bank asset value is a concave function of the economy, its volatility rises as its value falls, and bank equity is a mezzanine claim on a pool of short puts, not a call on a lognormal asset. A constant-$\sigma_V$ Merton model calibrated in good times understates bank default risk precisely when it is building. SVB adds two features the model has no slot for: an HTM book carried at amortized cost (the accounting $V$ was a mark-to-model asset in a mark-to-market model), and a deposit base that could leave in a day. The 9 March run was the jump Kaz named in Beat 3, in $F$'s *maturity* rather than in $V$.

**How the desk would have flagged and traded it.** Three signals were available from public data. (1) DD momentum: a falling DD over September to December 2022, at a time when the bank's senior unsecured bonds were not repricing by a comparable amount (we lack verified bond marks, so treat this as the qualitative pattern the signal looks for). (2) A financials-specific adjustment: replace lognormal $V$ with a concave, state-dependent volatility, as Nagel and Purnanandam suggest, which lowers DD further for a bank with a long-duration securities book in a rising-rate year. (3) A jump overlay: uninsured deposit share as a run-intensity covariate, treated as a jump to default, not a diffusion. The trade was underweight the bank's senior unsecured debt, or long protection where it traded, sized as a jump-to-default position rather than a spread-duration one (Chapter 18). Chapter 23 revisits SVB through the equity-volatility surface.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** *DD is not a rating.* A DD of 4 for a bank and a DD of 4 for an industrial are not the same distance. The industrial's asset volatility is measured on a convex claim's denominator; the bank's is measured on a pool of short puts whose volatility is lowest right before it explodes. Desks that pool financials and non-financials in one DD-to-PD table are averaging a ruler and a rubber band (items 5 and 10, §6.12).

> **View From Another Seat.** *The opportunistic distressed fund and the dealer desk, looking at the same holdco note.* To an opportunistic distressed fund, the SVB Financial Group notes at 30 to 40 cents were never a structural-model problem. They were an underwriting problem with three moving parts: how much of the parent's cash on deposit at the failed bank the FDIC would honour, what the non-bank subsidiaries would fetch, and how many other claims would land in the same class. Tax was one of those claims, and it moved a lot: in July 2024 the IRS agreed to cut one tax claim against the estate from about \$649 million to \$43 million, and the 1.8% notes due 2026 rose as much as 2.625 points, to about 63.6 cents, on the news (Bloomberg, 2 July 2024, as republished by Accounting Today). A fund in that seat buys at a price that works if two of the three fights go its way, holds through a confirmation hearing, does not mark to the screen in between, and treats a twelve-point gap between two notes in the same class as noise around its recovery estimate. A dealer desk sees the same twelve points as the entire opportunity. It has no interest in owning the claim through 2024; it wants to buy the cheap 2031s from a seller who needs out, sell the dear 2025s to a buyer who believes in reinstatement, and be flat by Thursday, because its capital is charged daily and its risk limits do not have a line for "pending litigation with a federal agency." Same claim, two seats: one lives on the recovery, the other on the dispersion around it. Howard Marks's memo written in the aftermath, "Lessons from Silicon Valley Bank" (Oaktree Capital, 17 April 2023), is the long-horizon investor's reading of the same weeks and is worth putting beside the desk's.

## 6.5 Black–Cox: First Passage and Covenant Barriers

### 6.5.1 Desk framing: the covenant you spent ninety hours modeling

Merton lets the firm default only at $T$. Bohn and Stein are polite about it: "This case is not realistic as a firm will typically default at whatever time its market asset value falls through its default barrier" (p.151). Black and Cox (1976) fixed it by adding a barrier that can be hit at any time, and they gave it a name every RSSG banker will recognize.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Schmid_Credit_Risk_Pricing_Models p.121:* "Black & Cox (1976) introduce so called safety covenants which provide the bondholders the right to force the firm into bankruptcy as soon as the firm value hits some deterministic time-dependent threshold."
>
> **Annotation.** A safety covenant is a maintenance covenant, and the 1976 paper is the first formal model of the thing Theo spent his analyst years computing headroom against. The insight is that a covenant converts the bondholders' European short put into a *barrier* option: lenders get control as soon as value crosses the line, not when the debt matures. What the 2026 desk must add: Black and Cox assumed the covenant is exercised mechanically. In practice a breach starts a negotiation (waiver fee, margin step-up, amend-and-extend), so a barrier crossing is a *repricing option* held by the lenders, not a default. And since the cov-lite wave, most institutional term loans carry no maintenance covenant at all. A maintenance covenant is a Black–Cox barrier. A cov-lite loan is a Black–Cox barrier that was removed years ago, and nobody told the model.

Kaz, reading a revolver term sheet for the first time, asks whether a "springing lien" is seasonal. Theo explains that it springs into existence when a trigger is hit, a downgrade for the lien or, for the covenant cousin, revolver utilization above some threshold such as 35%. Kaz thinks about it. "So it's a barrier multiplied by a Heaviside function of utilization." That is exactly right, and it is the section's point: a springing covenant is a barrier that switches on only when the firm is already drawing on its liquidity, which is to say only when it is already in trouble.

### 6.5.2 The first-passage probability

Theo's instinct, once he sees a barrier, is that it must make default more likely than Merton says, and his instinct is right. The question is by how much, and the answer comes from one of the prettiest arguments in probability.

> **Math, Gently.** *First passage and the reflection principle.* Merton asks one question: is the firm below the line *on the last day*? A covenant asks a different one: did the firm touch the line *on any day*? The second question always has a bigger answer, because it also catches every path that dipped below the line and then recovered. How many of those are there? Picture the asset path as a line on a chart with a floor drawn across it. Take any path that touches the floor at some moment and ends the year *above* it. From the moment it first touched, flip the rest of the path upside down, reflecting it in the floor like an image in a puddle. The flipped path ends *below* the floor. With no drift, up and down moves are equally likely, so the original and its mirror twin are equally likely too. That pairing means: the number of paths that touched the floor and came back equals the number that ended below it. So the probability of *ever* touching the floor is exactly twice the probability of *ending* below it. Worked: the §6.2.4 firm has a risk-neutral Merton PD of about 17.7%, and its risk-neutral log drift is tiny ($r-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2=0.04-0.031\approx0.009$). Put a flat barrier at $F$ and treat the drift as zero, and the probability of touching it within a year is roughly $2\times17.7\%\approx35\%$. One covenant, same firm, same volatility, twice the default probability. With drift the mirror twins are no longer equally likely, and the correction is one extra exponential factor; the full version, with that factor and three checks, follows.

#### The Full Derivation: first passage with a drift and a moving barrier

Let the barrier grow at a deterministic rate $g$ toward $K$ at $T$: $K_t=Ke^{-g(T-t)}$. (We use $g$ for the barrier growth rate because $\gamma$ is reserved for risk aversion.) Default is the first time assets touch the barrier, $\tau=\inf\{t\ge0:V_t\le K_t\}$. Work in log distance to the barrier, $X_t=\ln(V_t/K_t)$, which by §6.1 is a Brownian motion with drift:

$$
dX_t=m\,dt+\sigma_V\,dW_t,\qquad m=\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2-g,\qquad x_0=\ln\frac{V_0}{K_0}>0.
$$

Under $\mathbb{Q}$, replace $\mu$ by $r-q$, where $q$ is the payout rate. For driftless Brownian motion the reflection principle says every path that touches 0 and ends above it has a mirror-image path that ends below it, so $\mathbb{P}(\min_{s\le T}X_s\le0)=2\,\mathbb{P}(X_T\le0)$. With drift, the mirror path is reweighted by the Girsanov density, and the first-passage probability is

$$
\mathbb{P}(\tau\le T)=N\!\Big(\frac{-x_0-mT}{\sigma_V\sqrt T}\Big)+e^{-2mx_0/\sigma_V^2}\,N\!\Big(\frac{-x_0+mT}{\sigma_V\sqrt T}\Big).
$$

Three checks make this formula trustworthy. First, the first term alone is $\mathbb{P}(X_T\le0)$, the probability of being below the barrier at $T$; with $g=0$ and $K=F$ it is the Merton PD $N(-DD)$, and the second term counts the paths that crossed and came back. Second, with $m=0$ both terms equal $N(-x_0/\sigma_V\sqrt T)$ and the PD is exactly twice the Merton number (the "$pd_a=2pd$" line in Bohn and Stein, p.151). Third, as $T\to\infty$ with $m>0$ the first term vanishes and the second tends to $e^{-2mx_0/\sigma_V^2}<1$: a firm with positive drift has a positive probability of never defaulting, while for $m\le0$ default is certain eventually, which is what ROBO meant.

Valuation follows the same machinery. Equity is a down-and-out call on $V$; debt is the riskless bond minus a put plus a rebate paid when the barrier is hit (Bohn and Stein give the boundary conditions on p.152). The formulas are longer than Merton's and add nothing conceptually beyond the passage probability, so the reference implementation in §6.11 prices the PD and leaves the claim values to the exercises.

#### When to reach for a barrier, and when it breaks

Black–Cox is the right tool whenever somebody is actually *watching the line*. A maintenance-covenant loan tested every quarter, a bank revolver with a minimum-liquidity test, an asset-based facility with a borrowing base recomputed monthly, a structured vehicle with an overcollateralization trigger: each has a monitored threshold and a party with the right to act when it is crossed, and in each of them Merton's "only at maturity" understates the probability that the lenders get control by something close to a factor of two. It is also the right tool for a firm that will default on a liquidity floor long before its debt matures, which is Theo's column N in one sentence. The model breaks in two directions, and both have names. It breaks when the barrier is *negotiable*: the threshold exists on paper, but the parties can move it, waive it, or, as at Serta in 2020, rearrange who stands behind whom so that the line the minority lenders thought protected them was moved by a majority vote (§6.5.3, Docket Diary below). And it breaks when the barrier is *absent*: a cov-lite term loan has no maintenance line at all, so a Black–Cox model with a barrier at the old covenant level overstates default timing and badly overstates recovery. The seasoned practitioner's first check is to open the credit agreement, not the model: is there a maintenance test, who can waive it, by what vote, and has anybody recently hired a liability-management adviser?

### 6.5.3 Covenants, cov-lite, and uptiers as barrier mechanics

The cleanest way to see how the barrier behaves in practice is to follow one loan through its life, from the year it had a covenant to the year somebody moved the line. The loan is illustrative; every stage of its life is not.

It begins, as most sponsor-backed term loans did before the mid-2010s, with a maintenance covenant, and **a maintenance covenant is a barrier in EBITDA space.** A total leverage test $\text{Debt}/\text{EBITDA}\le c$ becomes a barrier on value once you write $V\approx m_{\text{EV}}\cdot\text{EBITDA}$ for an enterprise-value multiple $m_{\text{EV}}$:

$$
\frac{\text{Debt}}{\text{EBITDA}}\le c\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad V\ge K\equiv\frac{m_{\text{EV}}}{c}\,\text{Debt}.
$$

*Example (illustrative).* \$600mm of debt, \$110mm of EBITDA (5.45x), a 6.0x covenant, and an 8x multiple give $V=880$, $K=800$, and $x_0=\ln1.1\approx0.095$. With $\sigma_V=25\%$ and roughly zero drift, the one-year passage probability is about $2N(-0.38)\approx70\%$. A tight maintenance covenant is hit most years, and most hits end in an amendment fee rather than a default, which is why lenders fought to keep them: the barrier is an option to reprice. In our loan's early years that is exactly what happens. EBITDA softens one winter, the leverage test trips, and instead of a bankruptcy there is a lender call, a 25-basis-point amendment fee, a margin step-up, and a slightly looser covenant for four quarters. Theo spent his analyst years on the borrower's side of those calls. Seen from the model, each one is the lenders exercising a barrier option and being paid for it.

Then the loan is refinanced in a hot market, and the new document has no maintenance test at all. **Cov-lite deletes the barrier.** With no maintenance test, the only barriers left are payment dates and maturity: the model reverts to a discretely monitored, Geske-style compound option in which default happens only when a coupon or a maturity cannot be paid or refinanced. Passage probabilities fall, recoveries fall further (by the time a cov-lite borrower misses a coupon, value is far below the old barrier), and the lender's repricing option is gone. Nobody on the lender side wanted that trade, but in a market where a dozen funds are bidding for every allocation, the lender who insists on a covenant does not get an allocation. The model's barrier did not move; it was simply deleted from the contract, and the next downturn finds the borrower drifting well below where the old line used to be, with nobody entitled to call a meeting.

So when the downturn arrives, it is the borrower and its sponsor who call the meeting, and they call it with some of the lenders rather than all of them. **An uptier moves the barrier for someone else.** In a non-pro-rata uptier, a majority group exchanges into new super-priority debt $F^{+}$ that ranks ahead of the left-behind lenders. The asset process $V_t$ does not change. But the left-behind lenders' payoff becomes $\min\big(\max(V_T-F^{+},0),F\big)$: their effective strike rises from $F$ to $F+F^{+}$ and their recovery in default falls. The participating lenders bought a lower strike with the left-behind lenders' collateral. That is the barrier math of the LMT era, and Chapter 13 turns it into a game. Our illustrative loan has lived the whole arc: a barrier that the lenders could exercise, then no barrier, then a barrier that a subset of the lenders moved underneath the rest. For the general-audience version of how cov-lite paper led to lenders fighting one another, the *Odd Lots* episode "Inside the Blood Sport of Creditor-on-Creditor Violence," with the Financial Times's Sujeet Indap (25 November 2024), is the place to start.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The CLO manager who was left behind.* For a collateralized loan obligation, an uptier is not an abstract change of strike. A CLO holds a diversified pool of broadly syndicated loans, is constrained by tests on the par value and rating mix of that pool, and has a limited bucket for CCC-rated assets; a loan that is downgraded and stripped of its collateral can push the vehicle toward those limits and divert cash away from its junior notes. Depending on their documents, CLOs could also find it hard to provide the new money that uptiers asked of participants, which is one reason they so often ended up on the wrong side of the line. At Serta Simmons, the lenders left behind by the June 2020 transaction recovered between 0.6% and 2.4% in the bankruptcy, while participants recovered 100% on one layer of the new debt and about 75% on another (Skadden, as compiled in this book's case file). The left-behind group that litigated included the "LCM Lenders" (as Mayer Brown's 31 July 2026 note labels them; a collateralized loan obligation issuer, LCM XXII Ltd., appears in a case caption, though the vehicles' CLO status is background knowledge rather than something the opinion states). After the Fifth Circuit's reversal, the bankruptcy court on remand issued a memorandum opinion on 7 July 2026 holding the participating lenders liable for breaching the credit agreement's pro rata sharing provision and setting damages at \$261.13 million as of the June 2020 transaction date, before prejudgment interest (Mayer Brown, 31 July 2026). From the CLO manager's seat, the barrier moved for someone else, and that someone was a vehicle with a CCC bucket, a set of coverage tests, and six years to wait for the court to say so.

### 6.5.4 Monte Carlo and the Brownian bridge

The closed form of §6.5.2 is beautiful and fragile: change the barrier into a step function, add a coupon date, let volatility depend on leverage, and it is gone. What survives every one of those changes is the brute-force method, which is to make up a great many futures and count. It has one trap, and the trap has an exact fix.

> **Math, Gently.** *Monte Carlo, and the dip you did not see.* **Monte Carlo** means: simulate 10,000 possible futures for the firm's assets, each built from random shocks with the right volatility, and count the fraction in which the asset path crossed the barrier. If 3,512 of 10,000 paths cross, the estimated probability is 35.1%, with a sampling error of about half a percentage point (the error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{\text{number of paths}}$, the random walk of §6.1.1 again). The trap is that a computer cannot simulate a continuous path. It simulates snapshots, one a month, say, and checks the barrier only at the snapshots. A path that is above the barrier at the end of January, dips below in mid-February, and is back above by the end of February is recorded as a survivor. The **Brownian bridge** formula answers the question "given where the path was at two snapshots, what is the chance it dipped below the line in between?" Worked: suppose the log asset value is 0.05 above the log barrier at both month-ends, with $\sigma_V=25\%$ and $\Delta t=1/12$. Then $\sigma_V^2\Delta t=0.0625/12\approx0.0052$, and the dip probability is $\exp(-2\times0.05\times0.05/0.0052)=\exp(-0.96)\approx38\%$. A monthly grid records that month as a certain survival; the truth is a better-than-one-in-three chance of a covenant breach. Multiply that miss across every month and every path, and a naive simulation understates default badly. The full version, with the formula and how to use it, follows.

Simulating first passage on a grid of $n$ steps misses every crossing that happens between grid points and comes back. The bias is downward and large: on a monthly grid, a material fraction of barrier hits never show up. The fix is exact. Conditional on two consecutive log values $x_i$ and $x_{i+1}$, both above a log barrier $b$, the probability that the path touched $b$ in between is

$$
p_i=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{2(x_i-b)(x_{i+1}-b)}{\sigma_V^2\,\Delta t}\Big),
$$

and this holds for Brownian motion with *any* constant drift, because conditioning on both endpoints removes the drift. Draw $U_i\sim\text{Unif}(0,1)$ and record a hit if $U_i<p_i$ (Glasserman 2004, *Monte Carlo Methods in Financial Engineering*). The bridge-corrected simulation is unbiased on a coarse grid, which §6.11 checks against the closed form.

Empirically, Feldhütter and Schaefer (2018) found that Black–Cox calibrated to long-run default rates prices investment-grade spreads well, while Bai, Goldstein, and Yang (2020) dispute it (§6.3.3). The desk does not need to adjudicate. It needs the barrier in the right place, which in 2026 is a harder question than the math.

> **Trend Watch.** *Default is now a negotiation (2020–2026).* The barrier in a structural model is supposed to be a contractual fact. In the liability-management era it became a negotiated one, and three kinds of evidence say so.
>
> Start with the share of defaults. S&P Global Ratings reported in February 2025 that distressed exchanges made up about two-thirds of US speculative-grade defaults in Q4 2024 and that the distressed-exchange default rate ran at about 3.2% through end-2024 against about 2% for all other default types combined (approx.; S&P Global Ratings, February 20, 2025, as summarized; not independently verified this edition). A law-firm survey citing Oaktree's Q4 2024 commentary put the share of corporate defaults occurring through liability management exercises at "over 50%" (Quinn Emanuel, *Creditor-on-Creditor Violence*, January 2026).
>
> Then the courts, which turned the barrier into a question of contract interpretation. On December 31, 2024, the Fifth Circuit held in *In re Serta Simmons Bedding* (125 F.4th 555) that Serta's 2020 uptier was not an "open market purchase" under its credit agreement; the same day, the New York Appellate Division, First Department, upheld the Mitel uptier in *Ocean Trails CLO VII v. MLN Topco* (Quinn Emanuel, January 2026). The location of the barrier now depends on a sentence in Section 2 of a credit agreement and on which court reads it.
>
> Finally the level of defaults itself. Moody's global speculative-grade trailing-twelve-month default rate was 4.3% in May 2026, up from 4.1% in December 2025, against a historical average of 4.2%; the US rate was 5.0%, with Moody's baseline forecasting declines to 3.1% globally and 3.5% in the US by December 2026 (First Trust, *Factoid*, June 22, 2026, citing Moody's). A year earlier UBS Asset Management had projected a 4.8% default rate for USD high yield in 2025, or 3.1% excluding two large distressed names (UBS AM, *2025 Fixed Income Default Study*, January 17, 2025).
>

A structural model with a fixed barrier is calibrated to a world that ended in 2020.

## 6.6 Reduced Form I: Cox Processes, Jarrow–Turnbull, Duffie–Singleton, and Three Recoveries

### 6.6.1 Desk framing: default as a surprise with a rate

Structural models explain *why* a firm defaults and then struggle to make it happen on time. Reduced-form models give up on why. Default is the first jump of a counting process whose arrival rate $\lambda_t$ you read off market prices, and the short end works by construction because a jump can arrive tomorrow. Mags approves, grudgingly. "Back in my day we priced bonds by phone and by grudge. The grudge was the intensity. You just didn't write it down."

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.328:* "The key assumption of reduced-form models is that the intensity process implies a distribution for the time to default. Said another way, all firms are assumed to default eventually and the riskier ones default earlier."
>
> **Annotation.** "All firms default eventually" is ROBO's 100% from the cold open, stated as a modeling axiom. It is harmless for pricing, because a 30-year survival probability of 20% and one of 25% differ by little in present value, and it is the reason reduced-form models are the language of CDS desks: they price the *timing* of default directly. What they give up, as Schmid puts it below, is any link to the balance sheet. The 2026 desk runs both: intensities for pricing and hedging, structural signals for ranking and for telling it when the intensity is about to move (§6.9).

### 6.6.2 The Cox construction and the zero-recovery bond

> **Math, Gently.** *Poisson processes and Cox processes: rain, and weather.* A **Poisson process** is raindrops falling on a paving stone at a known average rate, say two an hour. You cannot say when the next drop lands, but you know the rate, and from the rate everything else follows: the number of drops in an hour has a Poisson distribution (§6.1.4), the wait for the next drop is exponential, and the chance of at least one drop in the next minute is about $2/60\approx3.3\%$. Reduced-form credit models treat default as *the first raindrop*: the firm survives until the first arrival of a Poisson clock whose rate is the hazard $\lambda$. That alone gives you a positive short spread, because a drop can land in the next minute. But a constant rate is too simple, because in credit the rate itself moves: it rises in recessions, falls when the firm refinances, jumps when the sponsor hires restructuring counsel. A **Cox process** is raindrops whose rate is itself weather. First the weather is drawn (drizzle on Monday, a storm on Thursday), and then the drops fall at whatever rate the weather sets. In credit the "weather" is the intensity path $\lambda_t$, driven by spreads, equity, the economy; given the weather, default is a simple Poisson arrival; averaged over all possible weather, it is something richer. The trick that makes the whole family tractable is that you can price a bond by first conditioning on the weather and then averaging, which is exactly what the derivation below does. The full version follows.

#### The Full Derivation: the Cox construction and the zero-recovery bond

Let $\lambda_t\ge0$ be adapted to a filtration generated by economic factors, and let $\Theta\sim\text{Exp}(1)$ be independent of those factors. Define default as the moment the accumulated hazard exhausts the exponential clock:

$$
\tau=\inf\Big\{t:\int_0^t\lambda_s\,ds\ge\Theta\Big\}.
$$

This is a Cox, or doubly stochastic Poisson, process (Schmid p.71 gives the construction). Conditional on the factor path, $\mathbb{Q}(\tau>T\mid\text{factors})=\mathbb{Q}(\Theta>\int_0^T\lambda_s ds)=e^{-\int_0^T\lambda_sds}$, so by the tower property

$$
S(t,T)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[e^{-\int_t^T\lambda_u\,du}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_t\Big],
$$

on $\{\tau>t\}$. The same conditioning prices a zero-recovery defaultable bond (Lando 1998):

$$
P^{d,0}(t,T)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[e^{-\int_t^Tr_u\,du}\,\mathbf 1_{\{\tau>T\}}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_t\Big]=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[e^{-\int_t^T(r_u+\lambda_u)\,du}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_t\Big].
$$

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Schmid_Credit_Risk_Pricing_Models p.129:* "From a technical point of view, pricing of defaultable discount bonds is the same as pricing of non-defaultable discount bonds."
>
> **Annotation.** Replace $r$ by $r+\lambda$ and every term-structure tool you own (affine models, trees, the Veronesi toolkit) prices credit. That is the engineering win of reduced form, and it is why the CDS bootstrap in §6.8 is a rates bootstrap with one extra curve. The catch Schmid flags a page earlier is the price of that convenience: reduced-form models capture short spreads "more realistically," but "their main draw-back is the missing link between firm value and corporate default" (p.128). On a 2026 desk that drawback is a feature request: the hybrid of §6.9 puts the link back by making $\lambda$ a function of distance to default.

### 6.6.3 Three recovery conventions, which are three numeraires

What the bondholder receives at $\tau$ decides the pricing formula. Write $L=1-R$.

> **Math, Gently.** *Three recoveries, or "forty percent of what?"* When somebody says "assume 40% recovery," the right reply is Theo's banker reply: forty percent of *what*? There are three standard answers, and each one changes the arithmetic. **Recovery of face value** (RFV) means 40 cents per dollar of par; it is how bankruptcy claims and CDS auctions work, and it is what Mags means. **Recovery of market value** (RMV) means 40% of whatever the bond was worth the instant before default; if the bond was trading at 60, that is 24 cents. **Recovery of Treasury** (RT) means 40% of a riskless zero-coupon bond with the same maturity; if that zero is worth 82 cents on the default date, the holder gets about 33 cents. Now take a bond spread of 500 bp and the same "40%." Under market-value recovery the answer is clean: the spread is exactly hazard times loss, so the hazard is $0.05/0.60\approx8.3\%$ a year. Under face-value recovery with the bond trading near par, the answer is almost the same. But if the bond is trading at 60, losing from 60 down to 40 of par is a loss of only a third of its current value, and the hazard needed to justify 500 bp is far higher. Same spread, same "40%," very different default probability, because the 40% was a fraction of a different thing. Mathematically, each convention measures the recovered value in a different unit of account, a different **numeraire**: par, the bond itself, or a Treasury. The full version, one formula per convention, follows.

#### The Full Derivation: three recoveries, three formulas

**Recovery of market value (RMV; Duffie and Singleton 1999).** At default the holder receives a fraction $R$ of the bond's pre-default market value. Then the defaultable bond is a riskless bond discounted at an adjusted short rate,

$$
P^{d}(t,T)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[e^{-\int_t^T(r_u+\lambda_uL_u)\,du}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{F}_t\Big],\qquad s_t=\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}_tL_t,
$$

so the instantaneous spread is intensity times loss. Bohn and Stein describe this version of the model as one that "models the instantaneous, risk-neutral expected rate of loss of market value, which is a function of default intensity and instantaneous LGD at time t resulting from default" (Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.348).

**Recovery of face value (RFV, the CDS and bankruptcy convention).** The holder receives $R$ times par at $\tau$. With $r$ independent of $\lambda$ and $P(t,u)$ the riskless discount factor,

$$
P^{d}(t,T)=P^{d,0}(t,T)+R\int_t^TP(t,u)\big(-\partial_uS(t,u)\big)\,du.
$$

**Recovery of Treasury (RT; Jarrow and Turnbull 1995).** The holder receives $R$ riskless zeros of the same maturity, so under independence $P^{d}(t,T)=P(t,T)\big[R+(1-R)S(t,T)\big]$. Jarrow and Turnbull's 1995 model is the constant-$\lambda$ special case.

> **Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality.** *Myth:* "40% recovery is 40% recovery." *Reality:* it depends on the numeraire. A bond trading at 60 the day before default that recovers 40 of par has an RFV recovery of 40% and an RMV recovery of $40/60\approx67\%$. Plug an RFV quote into an RMV formula and you understate loss by a third. Schmid makes the general point: "Different recovery models can be transformed into each other. Basically, the value of the defaultable security at default is expressed using different numeraires" (Schmid_Credit_Risk_Pricing_Models p.95). Store the convention as an enum field next to every recovery number.

The **Duffie–Lando bridge** (2001) ties the two families together. If investors observe asset value only through noisy accounting reports, a first-passage structural model *acquires* an intensity: default remains a barrier crossing in the firm's true value, but from the investor's filtration it arrives as a surprise. In their words, "with imperfect information, however, yield spreads are strictly positive at zero maturity because investors' are uncertain about the nearness of current assets to the trigger level" (p.634). This is the cleanest argument for using intensities at the short end and structural signals for the long end: they are the same model seen through different information sets.

#### When to reach for intensities, and when they break

Intensity models are the right tool whenever the job is *pricing and hedging what trades*. A CDS desk marks its book, computes its jump-to-default exposure and quotes upfronts off a hazard curve, because the hazard curve is built from the prices it is hedging with and reproduces them exactly. Anything with a short end, a three-month note, a revolver draw, a bond two quarters from maturity, belongs here too, since only a model in which default can arrive tomorrow gives a sensible short spread. And intensities are the natural language for portfolios of defaults, because a vector of hazards and a correlation structure is something a simulation can actually consume. They break in two ways a practitioner should expect. The first is mechanical: when the market curve is *inverted*, as it is for a name the market thinks will default soon or not at all, a naive bootstrap can produce a negative hazard in a later bucket, which is a probability of un-defaulting and a sign that the recovery or the interpolation assumption is wrong (§6.8.3). The second is conceptual: an intensity tells you *how likely* default is and nothing about *why*. When the risk committee asks whether the 300 bp move in a name's five-year CDS reflects the business, the balance sheet, a lender group forming or a technical squeeze in the protection market, the hazard curve has no answer. That is the job of the structural side, and it is why §6.9 marries the two. The first check a seasoned practitioner makes on any hazard curve is whether every bucket is positive and every bucket's survival is below the last; the second is which recovery convention was used to build it, because two desks with different conventions will disagree about the hazard while agreeing on every price.

## 6.7 Reduced Form II: CIR Intensities in Closed Form

A bootstrapped hazard curve, the subject of §6.8, is a photograph: it tells you what the market charges today for default risk at each horizon. It says nothing about how that curve will *move*. For most of the desk's daily work that is fine. For anything that depends on the path, a stress test, an option on a spread, the exposure a dealer carries to a counterparty over the life of a swap, the desk needs a film rather than a photograph, and the simplest film that behaves like real credit is the one in this section.

What does real credit do? Mags can tell you without a model. Hazard rates for a high-yield name spike when the economy turns, when a quarter disappoints, when a sponsor goes quiet, and then, unless the firm actually defaults, they drift back toward wherever that kind of company usually lives. They do not wander off to infinity, and they do not go negative. That is a thermostat: a level the system is pulled toward, a speed at which it is pulled, and noise that keeps knocking it off. The Cox–Ingersoll–Ross model adds one more touch, which is that the noise shrinks as the hazard gets small, so a safe name's hazard jitters gently near zero while a stressed name's swings wildly. That single square root is what keeps the hazard from crossing zero in the continuous model, though, as the Ways This Blows Up box at the end of this section warns, not in a careless simulation of it. The reward for accepting this particular film is a closed-form survival curve, which is why academics love it. The price is that one day's CDS curve barely pins down its parameters, which is why, as Bohn and Stein note below, practitioners are more cautious. If you are reading for the desk rather than for an exam, the next box and the annotation are what you need; the full derivation is there for the day you implement it.

> **Math, Gently.** *Mean reversion, or the thermostat on the hazard.* A mean-reverting quantity is pulled back toward a long-run level, and the pull is stronger the further away it is. Write the long-run level as $\theta$ and the speed as $\kappa$. Each year, roughly a fraction $\kappa$ of the gap between today's hazard and $\theta$ closes, on average. The cleanest way to feel $\kappa$ is through its **half-life**, $\ln2/\kappa$: the time it takes for half the gap to close. With $\kappa=0.5$, the half-life is about 1.4 years. So a name whose hazard has spiked to 6% against a long-run level of 2% should, absent default and absent new shocks, be expected back to about 4% in 1.4 years and about 3% in 2.8 years. Volatility $\sigma_\lambda$ is the size of the shocks that keep knocking it around, and in CIR the shocks are scaled by $\sqrt{\lambda}$, like a ball that bounces more gently the closer it gets to the floor. Two names with the same five-year CDS can have very different films: one a fast thermostat around a high level, the other a slow one around a low level, which is exactly the identification problem the annotation below describes. The full version follows.

#### The Full Derivation: CIR survival in closed form

Let the intensity follow a Cox–Ingersoll–Ross square-root diffusion under $\mathbb{Q}$:

$$
d\lambda_t=\kappa(\theta-\lambda_t)\,dt+\sigma_\lambda\sqrt{\lambda_t}\,dW_t,
$$

with mean-reversion speed $\kappa$, long-run level $\theta$, and intensity volatility $\sigma_\lambda$ (subscripted to keep it apart from $\sigma_V$). By Feynman–Kac, $S(t,\lambda)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{-\int_t^T\lambda_udu}]$ solves

$$
S_t+\kappa(\theta-\lambda)S_\lambda+\tfrac12\sigma_\lambda^2\lambda S_{\lambda\lambda}-\lambda S=0,\qquad S(T,\lambda)=1.
$$

Try the affine ansatz $S=A(\tau)e^{-B(\tau)\lambda}$ with time to maturity $\tau=T-t$. Then $S_t=(-A'/A+B'\lambda)S$, $S_\lambda=-BS$, and $S_{\lambda\lambda}=B^2S$. Substituting and collecting the terms in $\lambda$ and the constant terms separately gives two Riccati ODEs:

$$
B'(\tau)=1-\kappa B-\tfrac12\sigma_\lambda^2B^2,\qquad \frac{A'(\tau)}{A(\tau)}=-\kappa\theta B(\tau),\qquad A(0)=1,\;B(0)=0.
$$

With $\eta=\sqrt{\kappa^2+2\sigma_\lambda^2}$ the solution is

$$
B(\tau)=\frac{2(e^{\eta\tau}-1)}{(\eta+\kappa)(e^{\eta\tau}-1)+2\eta},\qquad A(\tau)=\left[\frac{2\eta\,e^{(\kappa+\eta)\tau/2}}{(\eta+\kappa)(e^{\eta\tau}-1)+2\eta}\right]^{2\kappa\theta/\sigma_\lambda^2}.
$$

The **Feller condition** $2\kappa\theta\ge\sigma_\lambda^2$ keeps $\lambda_t$ strictly positive. Violate it and the hazard visits zero, at which point ROBO declares the issuer immortal. The transition law is a scaled noncentral chi-square (Veronesi p.597): given $\lambda_s$, $\lambda_{s+\Delta}=c\,Y$ with $c=\sigma_\lambda^2(1-e^{-\kappa\Delta})/(4\kappa)$ and $Y$ noncentral $\chi^2$ with $4\kappa\theta/\sigma_\lambda^2$ degrees of freedom and noncentrality $\lambda_se^{-\kappa\Delta}/c$, so paths can be simulated exactly with no discretization error.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.341:* "The Cox, Ingersoll, and Ross (CIR) specification is quite popular among academics but is less widely used in practice."
>
> **Annotation.** Why practitioners hesitate: one CDS curve barely identifies $\kappa$ and $\theta$ separately (a fast reversion to a high level and a slow reversion to a low one can price the same five tenors), the square-root diffusion cannot produce the sudden jumps that distressed curves show, and the closed form invites overfitting. Where CIR earns its keep on a 2026 desk: anywhere you need *dynamics* rather than today's curve, such as scenario generation for a CVA or jump-to-default overlay, options on spreads, and stress tests. Fix $\kappa$ from a cross-sectional prior, fit $\theta$ and $\lambda_0$ to the curve, and report the sensitivity to $\kappa$. Bohn and Stein also warn that sign conventions for $A$ and $B$ differ across texts (Lando writes $\alpha,\beta$); test against a Monte Carlo before trusting any formula you copied, including this one.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** *Euler goes negative.* The naive Euler step $\lambda_{t+\Delta}=\lambda_t+\kappa(\theta-\lambda_t)\Delta+\sigma_\lambda\sqrt{\lambda_t\Delta}\,Z$ can push $\lambda$ below zero, and then $\sqrt{\lambda}$ is NaN and the simulation silently produces garbage. Use exact noncentral-$\chi^2$ sampling, or full truncation ($\sqrt{\max(\lambda,0)}$ in the diffusion and $\max(\lambda,0)$ in the drift and the integral), and count how many paths hit zero as a diagnostic (item 9, §6.12).

## 6.8 Hazard Bootstrapping from CDS and the Credit Triangle

### 6.8.1 Desk framing: the 13-week model, industrialized

A CDS contract pays $(1-R)$ times notional at default in exchange for a running coupon $c$ until default or maturity. Since the 2009 standardization, North American single-name contracts trade with fixed coupons of 100 or 500 bp plus an upfront payment, and the ISDA CDS Standard Model converts between upfront and par spread. Mags remembers it differently: "Back in my day we quoted running spreads, confirmed by fax, and settled arguments over the recovery by lunch." She concedes the standard model is an improvement. "Now everyone is wrong in the same way, which is what a market is."

Theo, who was in college in 2009, asks what "by fax" means in practice, and Mags tells him, because nobody ever asks and she has been waiting fifteen years. In the winter of 2008 she was running a distressed book that bought protection on auto suppliers, and every single-name contract was its own small negotiation. A dealer would quote five-year protection on a supplier at some enormous running spread, two thousand five hundred basis points, say, a number that meant the buyer was paying a quarter of the notional every year for insurance on a company that might not last one. The trade was agreed on the phone. The confirmation came by fax, sometimes the next day, sometimes the next week, and for years the industry's unconfirmed-trade backlog was the kind of thing regulators summoned dealers to meetings about. When a name defaulted, protection was settled physically: the buyer delivered a bond and received par. "Which worked fine," Mags says, "until there was more protection outstanding than bonds in existence." That happened at Delphi in 2005, when buyers of protection scrambled to find deliverable bonds and bid them up around the settlement, which is not what a defaulted bond is supposed to do. The industry patched it with ad hoc cash-settlement auctions, one name at a time.

The fix, when it came in the spring of 2009, was called the Big Bang, and Mags speaks of it with the grudging respect she reserves for things that put people like her younger self out of business. North American contracts moved to fixed coupons of 100 or 500 bp, with the difference made up by an upfront payment; auction settlement was hardwired into the contracts; committees of dealers and investors were set up to rule on whether a credit event had happened; and a standard model, the one this section rebuilds, was published so that everyone converted upfront to spread with the same arithmetic. "Before, every trade was a conversation. After, every trade was a number." She pauses. "I liked the conversations. I made money on the conversations, because the other side didn't know the documents." She looks at Theo. "You'd have liked them too."

Theo would have. But he notices something about the standard model that Mags, who learned the market before it existed, takes for granted. Fixing the coupon and standardizing the conversion turned every CDS quote into a statement about one object, a survival curve, and made it possible to bootstrap that curve the same way at every desk in the world. His thirteen weekly columns were a private survival curve. The Big Bang made a public one.

### 6.8.2 The two legs

Let $t_1<\dots<t_n=T$ be the quarterly premium dates with accrual fractions $\Delta_i$ (ACT/360 in production; we use year fractions in the reference code), $P(0,t)$ the riskless discount curve, and $S(t)$ the $\mathbb{Q}$ survival curve. The premium leg includes accrued premium on default, approximated by half a period:

$$
\text{PV}_{\text{prem}}=c\sum_{i=1}^n\Delta_iP(0,t_i)\Big[S(t_i)+\tfrac12\big(S(t_{i-1})-S(t_i)\big)\Big]\equiv c\cdot\text{RPV01}.
$$

The protection leg pays $1-R$ at default, computed on a fine grid $u_0<u_1<\dots$ with midpoint discounting:

$$
\text{PV}_{\text{prot}}=(1-R)\sum_jP\big(0,\tfrac{u_{j-1}+u_j}{2}\big)\big(S(u_{j-1})-S(u_j)\big).
$$

The par spread equates the legs, $s=\text{PV}_{\text{prot}}/\text{RPV01}$, and for a standard-coupon contract the upfront paid by the protection buyer is $(s-c)\cdot\text{RPV01}$ per unit notional.

### 6.8.3 The bootstrap and the credit triangle

> **Math, Gently.** *Bootstrapping, and the credit triangle.* A **bootstrap** solves a curve one tenor at a time, left to right, like Theo filling in his weekly columns: you cannot know week 7's survival until you know week 6's. The one-year CDS quote involves only the hazard over the first year, so solve for that hazard first. The two-year quote involves the first year's hazard (now known) and the second year's (unknown), so solve for the second. Keep going until the last quote is used up. Each step is a one-unknown problem, which is why the method is fast and robust, and why a bad quote at one tenor contaminates every tenor after it. The **credit triangle** is the back-of-envelope version, and it is the most useful three-symbol formula in the chapter: spread $\approx$ hazard $\times$ loss, $s\approx\lambda(1-R)$. The intuition is insurance arithmetic. If a name defaults at a rate $\lambda$ a year and each default costs the protection seller $1-R$ per dollar of notional, then a fair annual premium is the expected annual loss. Worked: a 500 bp spread with $R=40\%$ gives $\lambda\approx0.05/0.60\approx8.3\%$ a year, so one-year risk-neutral survival is about $e^{-0.083}\approx92\%$ and five-year survival about $e^{-0.417}\approx66\%$. A triangle with three corners, spread, hazard and recovery, and any two give you the third. The full version, which shows why the triangle is exact for a flat curve and only approximate for quarterly coupons, follows.

#### The Full Derivation: the piecewise-constant bootstrap and the triangle

Make $\lambda$ piecewise constant on the quote buckets: $\lambda(t)=\lambda_k$ for $t\in(T_{k-1},T_k]$, so $S(t)=\exp\!\big(-\sum_k\lambda_k\cdot|(T_{k-1},T_k]\cap(0,t]|\big)$. Solve for $\lambda_1$ so the $T_1$ quote reprices, then hold it fixed and solve for $\lambda_2$ from the $T_2$ quote, and so on, each step a one-dimensional root search (Brent's method). This is the procedure the ISDA model and MathWorks' `cdsbootstrap` implement, the latter documenting that "the survival probability is defined in terms of a piecewise constant hazard rate."

The credit triangle drops out when $\lambda$ is flat and the premium is paid continuously. Then $\text{PV}_{\text{prot}}=(1-R)\int_0^TP(0,t)\lambda S(t)\,dt$ and $\text{PV}_{\text{prem}}=s\int_0^TP(0,t)S(t)\,dt$. The integrands differ by the constant factor $\lambda$, so

$$
s=\lambda(1-R)\qquad\text{exactly, for any deterministic discount curve,}
$$

and approximately when coupons are paid quarterly in arrears (the discrete premium leg is worth slightly less, so the bootstrapped $\lambda$ comes out slightly below $s/(1-R)$; §6.11 measures the gap). A flat 300 bp curve with $R=40\%$ implies $\lambda\approx5\%$ a year. Run the same arithmetic on the ICE BofA CCC & Lower index OAS of 1,278 bp in mid-October 2026 (Chapter 5, Trend Watch, from FRED) and the risk-neutral hazard is about 21% a year.

Mags, on the triangle: "Forty percent recovery is a convention. In thirty years I have never been handed exactly forty." She is right, and it does not matter for the bootstrap: a CDS spread identifies only the product $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}(1-R)$, so the conventional $R$ is a quoting device for turning spreads into upfronts. It is not a forecast. If you change $R$ you change $\lambda$ in the opposite direction and reprice every quote identically. The hazard curve you hand the risk system inherits the convention, so ship $R$ with it.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** *Bootstrapping a distressed curve.* (1) *Negative hazards.* Distressed curves invert: 1-year protection costs more than 5-year because the market expects default soon or not at all. Steep enough inversion makes the forward hazard on a later bucket negative, which is an arbitrage in the model and a data error or a recovery-assumption error in practice; MathWorks warns about "non-monotone default probabilities (that is, negative hazard rates)." Flag the bucket, floor it only in a separate output, and never silently floor the curve you use to reprice. (2) *Points upfront treated as running spread.* A distressed name quoted at 35 points upfront plus 500 bp running is not a 500 bp credit. (3) *Day counts.* ACT/360 premium accrual against ACT/365 discounting is a few basis points of error that compounds across a book. (4) *Forgetting accrual on default,* which overstates the premium leg's value to the protection seller. (5) *The wrong recovery by seniority:* subordinated contracts conventionally use a lower recovery than senior ones; check the current standard assumptions for your region and tier rather than hard-coding them. The first of these is item 7 on the consolidated list (§6.12).

> **Trend Watch.** *Single-name CDS: episodic liquidity, central clearing, and a curve migration (2018–2026).* ISDA's May 2023 *Single-name CDS Market Update* reported quarterly single-name traded notional ranging between about \$405 billion and \$1.1 trillion over 2018 to Q1 2023, with a trough in Q4 2020 and a five-year peak of \$1.1 trillion in Q1 2023 as the bank failures drove hedging demand. Breadth shrank: the number of reference entities trading in a quarter fell from 854 in Q1 2018 to 719 in Q1 2023, and has since held in a narrow range of about 710 to 760 names (751 in Q4 2025; ISDA, July 2026); of 1,169 entities that traded at least once in five years, about 600 traded less than once a quarter, while 496 traded every quarter and accounted for more than 80% of activity. In Q1 2023, 51.2% of single-name notional and 87.4% of index notional reported in the US was centrally cleared; by 2025 the shares were 63.1% and 78.1% (ISDA, July 2026). BIS statistics put total CDS notional at \$8.5 trillion at end-December 2023, down from \$9.9 trillion six months earlier, with 65% centrally cleared (BIS, May 16, 2024), and credit derivatives at \$9.2 trillion at end-June 2024, down 9% year on year (BIS, November 21, 2024). Plumbing changed too: the ISDA CDS Standard Model, open-source C code administered by S&P Global Market Intelligence, announced in June 2026 that its standard rate curves were moving to a new S&P Global site, with the old site shutting after August 15, 2026 (cdsmodel.com). What it means for the desk: for most issuers, a full single-name CDS curve is a stale or synthetic object. Bootstrap where the curve trades, and elsewhere build $\lambda$ from bond spreads, index-implied intensities, or structural signals. Store the date of the last real trade next to every hazard curve, and check the curve source URL in your config before the next migration breaks it at 6 a.m.

## 6.9 Hybrids: Structural Signals Inside Intensity Models

### 6.9.1 The two-stage fit

The desk does not have to choose. Bohn and Stein (p.354–355) sketch a two-stage hybrid. **Stage 1** fits a physical intensity $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_t$ to equity-based default probabilities through $1-\text{cpd}(t)=\exp(-\int_0^t\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_s\,ds)$, where cpd is the cumulative PD term structure from the structural model. **Stage 2** fits a market price of default risk $\Pi_t$ so that $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}_t=\Pi_t\,\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_t$ reprices the CDS or bond curve.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.355:* "Berndt, Douglas, Duffie, Ferguson, and Schranz (2004) use EDF and CDS data to do exactly this sort of estimation."
>
> **Annotation.** The Berndt et al. template turns the ratio $\Pi_t=\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}_t/\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_t$ into a time series per issuer, and that series is the cleanest version of the systematic "value" signal: credit is cheap when the market charges a large multiple of the structural default rate *relative to that issuer's own history and its peers*. The 2026 desk adds two things the 2004 paper could not: daily structural PDs for thousands of issuers from vendors or in-house engines (Trend Watch, §6.2), and the discipline that the ratio is a signal only after factor exposures are stripped. A high $\Pi_t$ in a CCC name during a risk-off week is beta wearing a disguise. IDO or it didn't happen.

### 6.9.2 Equity leads credit

The second hybrid use is speed. Equity prices incorporate news faster than bonds, so changes in DD lead changes in spread.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.31:* "the only spill-over to achieve meaningful statistical significance is equity Momentum, which generates a higher alpha than corporate bond Momentum itself."
>
> **Annotation.** A structural model is a principled way to turn equity momentum into a *credit* signal: the Merton hedge ratio $\partial B/\partial E$ tells you how much a given equity move should have moved the bond, so "DD momentum" (the change in DD over one to three months) measures the information the bond market has not yet priced. Chapter 10 builds it as a trend feature. The limit, as Dynkin and coauthors note, is that the Merton hedge ratio "ignores other important aspects of a firm's operations, such as cash flows and profitability" (Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.88), which is the opening for Theo's thirteen-week liquidity runway as a covariate in the intensity: $\lambda_t=\exp(\beta_0+\beta_1DD_t+\beta_2\,\text{Runway}_t+\cdots)$.

> **Math, Gently.** *A log-linear intensity: why the exponential, and why each input is a multiplier.* A hazard rate has to be positive; a negative chance of default is not conservative, it is nonsense. The easiest way to guarantee positivity is to model the *logarithm* of the hazard as a weighted sum of inputs and then exponentiate: whatever the inputs do, $e^{\text{anything}}$ is positive. The exponential also changes what the weights mean. In $\lambda=\exp(\beta_0+\beta_1DD+\beta_2\,\text{Runway})$, adding one unit to $DD$ does not *add* $\beta_1$ to the hazard; it *multiplies* the hazard by $e^{\beta_1}$. Worked *(illustrative)*: suppose $\beta_1=-0.5$, so each extra standard deviation of distance to default multiplies the hazard by $e^{-0.5}\approx0.61$. A name with a 3% hazard at $DD=2$ has about $3\%/0.61\approx4.9\%$ at $DD=1$ and about $1.8\%$ at $DD=3$. Give Theo's runway a weight of $\beta_2=-0.05$ per week, and four more weeks of liquidity multiplies the hazard by $e^{-0.2}\approx0.82$, whatever the DD is. Multipliers are the natural language of risk ("this covenant breach doubles the hazard"), and they are what the Cox proportional-hazards models of Chapter 14 estimate. The full version is the hybrid fit of §6.9.1 with these covariates in the first stage.

#### The Pair Book, PB-04: General Motors, a replay cut off at the close of 3 May 2005

*Pair Book entry PB-04, a replay cut off at the close of Tuesday 3 May 2005: the players see the tape up to that close and nothing after it. Owen Achterberg's capital-structure pod at Vantress (Chapter 4-C) and the desk are fictional composites, and the sizes, the desk's model outputs and all P&L are illustrative, from the desk's fictional run. General Motors, its five-year CDS and its stock, the published backtest and the dates are real and sourced.*

Ines's Friday replay has a guest. Owen Achterberg, who runs the capital-structure pod on the Vantress floor and whom Theo knew before the desk, has come across after his own close, because tonight's hand is the oldest trade in his book and Ines never lets a guest watch without chips. The usual rule gets one clause for him: anyone may use a study whose data end before the cut-off, whenever it was published. Mags reads the ticker on the first slide, says "I was there," and rolls her chair back to the wall, which is the replay rule for people with memories.

The slide is short: General Motors' five-year protection and its common stock, daily, to the close of Tuesday 3 May 2005. GM is on the last rung of investment grade at S&P, one notch above high yield. GM and Ford between them are about 3% of the investment-grade bond market and could be as much as 15% of global high yield if they fall (BIS *Quarterly Review*, June 2005, p.6). In the weeks to the cut-off the protection has been getting, in the words of the trade's later historians, "ever more expensive" (Duarte, Longstaff and Yu 2007, p.789).

Kaz has already put the name through the solver of §6.4: equity value and equity volatility in, asset value and asset volatility out, then the Merton five-year spread. The model spread on the printout is a fraction of the screen's *(the desk's fictional run)*. "Merton says GM's protection is rich," Kaz says. "Merton says that about nearly everybody." A diffusion firm recovers too much when it defaults and cannot default soon at all (§6.2.4), and the gap left over is the credit spread puzzle of §6.3.3. A level that is wrong for everyone is not a signal about anyone. The signal is the gap measured against the name's own history, and GM has one.

Owen has brought it. The published version of this trade ran on 261 North American names and 135,759 daily five-year CDS spreads from January 2001 to December 2004, four months short of tonight's cut-off, with CreditGrades, a first-passage cousin of Merton that Chapter 23 builds, standing in as the model (Duarte, Longstaff and Yu 2007, p.788). When the market spread exceeds the model's by a trigger, sell \$100 of protection and short the stock by the model's delta; leave at convergence or after 180 days; pay a bid–ask of 5% of the spread (p.790). The equally weighted book earned 0.705% a month, a Sharpe ratio of 1.2 over 48 months (Table 5, p.791); at a trigger of 0.5 the mean was zero or negative (p.792); and "convergence occurs for only a small fraction of the individual trades" (p.790). GM is the paper's illustration. In October 2002 its market spread went above 440 bp while the model stayed under 300; the gap was gone by February 2003; and the same trade put on in August 2002 lost money first, because the gap kept widening (p.789). Kaz reruns October 2002 through the desk's engine and lays it beside tonight. On the desk's run, tonight's gap is the wider.

Ines deals the card.

*Card. Close of Tuesday 3 May 2005. Write down the protection you sell, the stock you short and the model that gave you the number, the capital the pair needs, and the kind of day on which both legs lose.*

Owen goes first. Sell \$10mm of five-year protection, which makes the pod long GM's credit without owning a bond, and short the stock against it. Kaz writes the ratio, which falls straight out of §6.2.3. Selling protection behaves like owning the debt; the debt and the stock are both functions of the same asset value; and the chain rule turns their two asset deltas into one equity delta:

$$
\frac{\partial B}{\partial E}=\frac{\partial B/\partial V}{\partial E/\partial V}=\frac{N(-d_1)}{N(d_1)} .
$$

Per \$100 of protection sold, the short is $100\cdot\frac{N(-d_1)}{N(d_1)}\cdot\frac{E_0}{F}$ dollars of stock: \$100 of protection stands in for \$100 of the firm's face $F$, and the hedge shorts the matching slice of its equity $E_0$. On GM the inversion gives $N(d_1)\approx0.75$ and $E_0/F\approx0.12$ *(the desk's fictional run: high leverage and low asset volatility, a carmaker with a finance arm as the solver sees it)*. The ratio is $0.25/0.75=1/3$, the product is $100\times\tfrac13\times0.12=4$, and so the pod shorts \$4 of stock per \$100 of protection: \$0.4mm against the \$10mm.

"That's the part of the model that works," Owen says, and it is. The hedge removes the direction of the firm, the move in $V$ that hurts the sold protection and pays the short stock in the same proportion, and on that slope structural models have a good record. Schaefer and Strebulaev (2008) find that they give "quite accurate predictions of the sensitivity of corporate bond returns to changes in the value of equity," and the Barclays test in §6.2's Trend Watch puts Merton's ratios about 5% below the empirical ones. Accurate, though, is not the same as large. Over the backtest the correlation between changes in GM's stock price and its CDS spread was $-0.32$ (p.789). A hedge, however well sized, can remove at most the square of that, about 10% of the CDS leg's variance. Ninety per cent of the position is left. "That's the idio," Owen says. "The gap. It's what the platform pays me for, and it's nearly the whole trade." It is also why the paper needed \$66 of capital per \$100 of notional to run its book at 10% volatility, and \$45 to \$52 in the investment-grade rows where GM sat through the backtest, against about \$8 for a swap-spread trade (Tables 1 and 5). Owen books the investment-grade figure: \$4.7mm of capital for \$10mm of protection. "The stock is liquid," he says. "The correlation isn't."

Kaz's printout has a third line, a rate delta. In Merton a higher riskless rate lifts the assets' risk-neutral drift and lowers the default probability, so the model says sold protection gains when rates rise and wants a Treasury position against it, about \$1,000 per basis point at this size *(the desk's fictional run; on the §6.2.4 firm stretched to five years, a 1 bp rise in $r$ tightens the Merton spread by about a quarter of a basis point, and a quarter of a basis point on a risky annuity near four is \$1,000 on \$10mm)*. Owen crosses it out. The same paper that blessed the equity slope found that the Merton model "does not capture the interest rate sensitivity of corporate debt" (Schaefer and Strebulaev 2008), so the rate line is the least trustworthy number on the page, and hedging it would cancel a sensitivity that may not be there. He writes it on the card as a leak: open, small and named.

Kaz is not finished. "It's delta-neutral and short volatility twice." The debt is a riskless bond minus a put on the assets and the stock is a call on them, so selling protection is short the put and shorting the stock is short the call, and both lose when asset volatility rises with the asset value unchanged. The §6.2.4 firm shows the size of it. Move $\sigma_V$ from 25% to 30% with $V_0$ held at 100 and the equity rises from 24.78 to about 25.87, or 4.4%, while the spread widens from 216 bp to about 362 bp. The debt loses exactly the 1.09 the equity gains, and a pair hedged at that firm's ratio of 0.135 loses on both legs: 1.09 on the protection and another 0.15 on the short stock.

"What else makes the stock go up and the spread go wider on the same day?" Theo asks.

"In this model? Nothing. Only $\sigma_V$."

Theo counts on his fingers the things he used to be paid to see coming: a buyback paid for with debt, a special dividend, a leveraged buyout, a buyer who wants the shares more than the creditors want him to have them. Each is good for the stock and bad for the bonds, and none of them is a move in $V$. "The delta assumes every move in the stock is news about the assets," he says. "Half my old job was moves that weren't." Then he names one that has nothing to do with the equity. One notch from high yield, a downgrade forces some holders of the credit to sell and the stock barely notices, so the protection carries a forced-seller factor that the short leg does not own, and the pair holds it unhedged. Kaz writes both under the vega line and draws a box round the word *delta*. It is a slope measured at today's price, and a jump in the stock, for any reason the model has no word for, walks straight off it.

Then the carry, which decides how long Owen can afford to wait for a gap that, on the paper's own count, usually does not close. Selling protection earns the running premium: at an illustrative 500 bp, \$500,000 a year on \$10mm. The paper's 5% bid–ask at that level is 25 bp wide, about half a point each way on a risky annuity near four, so \$50,000 in and \$50,000 out. The short leg is a borrowed stock, not a borrowed bond, which is the quiet virtue of the construction: the pod takes its credit risk in the CDS and borrows only a large company's shares, from the prime broker's stock-loan desk, at an illustrative general-collateral fee of 25 bp, about \$1,000 a year and roughly paid for by the rebate on the short-sale proceeds. It owes the lender every dividend GM pays while the short is open, about \$28,000 a year at an illustrative 7% yield. Over the paper's 180-day limit, with nothing moving, that is \$250,000 of premium less \$14,000 of dividends and \$100,000 of round-trip spread: about \$136,000, or 2.9% on \$4.7mm of capital for half a year *(all illustrative)*. The thesis is worth about \$4,000 for every basis point the gap closes. "The carry isn't the thesis," Ines says. "It's what the table pays you for sitting in front of the vol line and the default." At an illustrative recovery of 40, a default tomorrow costs the protection \$6mm, and the \$0.4mm short can make back at most \$0.4mm.

Owen's card says \$10mm sold, \$0.4mm short by the Merton delta and resized daily, \$4.7mm of capital, rates left open; the bad day is volatility up, with both legs down. Theo's has the same sizes and a different bad day: anything good for the shareholders and bad for the creditors, or a downgrade on a day when somebody wants the stock. Kaz's adds a re-estimate of the delta after any equity move larger than a tenth. Mags, from the wall, without turning round, says, "Write down who else is in it." Owen writes it down. Ines puts the cards in an envelope, writes *4 May* on the front, and locks it in her drawer.

PB-04 goes into the Pair Book that night as Owen's, open, a replay as of 3 May 2005, with its thesis in one sentence: GM's five-year protection is rich to its own stock by more than its October 2002 gap, and the gap closes within 180 days. The envelope is opened at the next replay, and the two trading days after the cut-off are Chapter 23's to deal (§23.6).

### 6.9.3 Case Study: First Brands, 2025, a firm with no equity ticker

In the cold open Mags said, to her disclosure statement, "And assume there is a stock. Half the names I have lost money on never had one." Two and a half years later the desk got the purest example it will ever see of what she meant.

The news arrives in pieces over September 2025, the way private-company bad news always arrives: not as a price but as a rumour about a price. A large auto-parts supplier that nobody on the systematic desk has ever modelled, because it has no listed equity and its debt is syndicated loans that trade by appointment, is, as the financial press reports it, pulling a refinancing; then it is hiring advisers; then its loans, which normally trade by appointment, are suddenly trading a great deal and a great deal lower; and then the chat windows fill with a phrase the desk has not seen since the Greensill collapse: *supply-chain finance*. Kaz runs the overnight structural engine on the name out of sheer curiosity and gets an error, which is the correct answer. There is no $E_t$. There is no $\sigma_E$. There is no DD. The engine has no input, and for once nobody can blame the vendor.

"What does the credit agreement say?" Theo asks, by reflex, and for once the question has a disappointing answer. The credit agreement says a great deal about the term loans. It says much less about the part of the company's financing that turned out to matter, because that part was not debt in the agreement's sense at all. It was receivables sold to third parties, invoices financed through supply-chain programmes, inventory financed off the balance sheet. Each of those arrangements had its own counterparty who saw only its own slice. Ines puts it in her language: "Every buyer at this table thought they were the only one holding that card. Nobody could see anyone else's hand, and the dealer was the company." Kaz, trying to be helpful, asks whether "factoring" is the same as "factor investing," and Mags explains, at some length and with visible pleasure, that it is not.

The filings, when they come, are worse than the rumours. First Brands files for Chapter 11 in the Southern District of Texas in September; the facts paragraph below has the numbers, each as reported. A supply-chain finance provider says that roughly \$2.3 billion of funds cannot be accounted for. A fund inside a large asset manager, a Jefferies-affiliated fund that had bought the company's receivables, and a Japanese bank joint venture are all reported to hold exposures. Prosecutors later allege that receivables were pledged more than once, so that several financiers believed they owned the same invoice. Mags does not say "I told you so." She says, "This is why I read the footnotes. Not because the footnotes tell you. Because when they don't tell you, that is the footnote."

The best public commentary on the episode came from a seat far from the systematic desk. Howard Marks's memo of 6 November 2025, "Cockroaches in the Coal Mine," was written in the weeks after First Brands and a handful of similar surprises, and two of its sentences belong on the desk's wall beside the twelve items of §6.12. The first is a cycle law: "The worst of loans are made in the best of times." The second is the structural-model lesson in one line: "First, private credit often involves companies that don't file disclosure documents with the SEC." (Both quoted from the memo as published at oaktreecapital.com.) A model that needs a stock price and a 10-K to compute a default point has nothing to say about a company that produces neither, and in a credit market whose fastest-growing segment is private, that is not an edge case. For the court-side view, *The Octus Download* episode 22 (23 December 2025), a conversation with U.S. Bankruptcy Judge Michael B. Kaplan, touches on the First Brands allegations among other things and is worth the hour for anyone who wants to hear how a bankruptcy judge thinks about a case where the estate's first job is to find out what it owns.

#### Decision point: the Monday screen for "First Brands risk"

*Your desk owns none of First Brands' debt. On the Monday after the filing, a client that runs a loan book asks you to screen its other holdings for "First Brands risk." There is no equity to invert and no CDS to read. What do you measure, and what do you tell it the number means?* The answer is in the paragraphs below: you measure opacity itself, the share of a borrower's financing that sits outside its reported debt, and you tell the client that the number is a flag for underwriting rather than a probability, because the thing that went wrong was not a move in the firm's value but the absence of anyone who could see the whole of $F$.

**The facts (secondary sources; confirm against the docket).** First Brands Group, a privately held auto-parts supplier, filed for Chapter 11 in September 2025 in the Southern District of Texas (Case No. 25-90399), listing liabilities in the \$10–50 billion range against assets in the \$1–10 billion range. Its financing relied heavily on off-balance-sheet receivables and supply-chain finance. After the filing, a supply-chain finance provider said roughly \$2.3 billion of funds could not be accounted for; reported exposures included more than \$500 million at a UBS O'Connor fund, about \$715 million of receivables exposure at a Jefferies-affiliated fund, and a \$1.75 billion exposure at a Norinchukin–Mitsui joint venture (as reported). Federal prosecutors alleged that receivables had been pledged more than once; indictments followed on January 29, 2026, and the case converted to Chapter 7 on September 1, 2026.

**The model lesson.** There was no stock price, so there was no $E_t$, no $\sigma_E$, and no DD. Worse, the part of the capital structure that mattered was not on the balance sheet at all, which is precisely Bohn and Stein's warning that "large levels of off-balance-sheet liabilities can make a difference and should be included in the DD calculation" (p.159). A default point built from reported debt would have been too low by an amount nobody outside the company knew. "The model had no input" is not a defense; it is a finding, and it should have been a flag.

**How the desk handles a firm with no equity.** Borrow $\sigma_V$ from listed peers and de-lever it. Fit an intensity to syndicated-loan marks where the loans trade. Treat opacity itself as a covariate: a large, rising share of receivables financing, factoring, and supplier finance relative to reported debt should *raise* $\lambda$, not leave it unchanged. And size such names as jump-to-default positions. Chapter 17 takes up private credit, where this is the normal case rather than the exception.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The direct lender.* A direct lender looks at a company like First Brands from the opposite end of the information spectrum from Theo's desk, and the case is a lesson for both. The direct lender has no screen price and does not want one. It negotiates the loan document itself, covenant by covenant, holds the loan to maturity or to a workout, and carries it at a private mark that moves a few points a quarter. Its edge is access: monthly financial reporting, compliance certificates, a seat at the lender meeting, the right to send in its own accountants. Ares Capital's president put the posture plainly on its Q4 2025 call: the firm will walk away from transactions because of their documentation terms (Ares Capital, Q4 2025 earnings call, 4 February 2026; paraphrased). In a structural model's vocabulary, a direct lender replaces the stock price with the borrower's own reporting as its witness to $V$, and replaces the market's barrier with a negotiated one it can enforce. That is a far better witness than a stock for most private companies, which is why the asset class has grown. It is exactly the witness that failed here. If the receivables have been pledged twice, the borrowing-base certificate is wrong, and every lender reading it, however carefully, is reading the same fiction. The systematic desk had no witness and knew it; the private lenders had a witness and trusted it. The opacity covariate above is the desk's attempt to price the difference.

## 6.10 Case Study: Carvana 2022–23 and Hertz 2020–21, the Call Option That Would Not Expire

### 6.10.1 Carvana: a negotiated barrier move

Chapter 5's cold open put the desk in the chair of a Carvana bondholder on Friday 2 June 2023, the day an exchange offer failed, and asked whether to sell, hold or buy. This scene sits in a different chair at the same table. It follows the trade that the structural model of this chapter most naturally recommends for a name like Carvana, the capital-structure pair, and watches what happened to it when the people short the put decided to restrike it. Equity closes are a vendor's daily bars, restated to as-traded prices and not checked against exchange records, so treat the cents as approximate; the exchange terms come from Carvana's own 8-Ks and 10-Q. The dialogue is invented and belongs to the fictional cast.

#### Scene B: The put that got restruck

*Beat 1. November–December 2022: the pair everyone is in.* By the late autumn of 2022 Carvana is the most argued-about credit in high yield. Its unsecured notes are quoted between 37 and 48 cents on the dollar, with the shortest and the longest paper at similar prices, in what Bloomberg's reporters call a "collapsing bond stack" (Bloomberg, 18 November 2022, as republished by American Banker; secondary). The stock closes at \$3.72 on 27 December 2022, down 99.0% from its peak close of \$370.10 on 10 August 2021 (vendor data). By early November, Institutional Investor reported, the stock had been the best-performing short of 2022 (Institutional Investor, 9 November 2022; secondary).

Kaz runs the name through the structural engine and gets a distance to default near zero. Equity is a deep out-of-the-money call; the notes are mostly a short put; the model is, in its own terms, quite sure what happens next. Ines does not argue with the model. She prices the trade it implies, which is the trade half the street already has on. "Long the bonds, short the stock, hedged at the Merton ratio," she says. "If it defaults, the bonds fall from the forties to a recovery and the stock goes to zero, and the short pays for the bond loss. If it survives, the bonds go to par and the stock goes up a bit, and you've got a small hedge against a big bond gain. Two outcomes, and the pair makes money in the one everybody believes and loses a little in the one nobody believes."

"Which is why everyone's in it," Mags says.

"Which is why everyone's in it," Ines agrees. "That's not a reason to be out of it. It's a reason to ask what the table knows that the model doesn't."

Theo has been reading the indentures, which is what he does when he is nervous. "The model knows two things about the debt," he says. "How much there is and when it's due. The indentures know a lot more. How much secured debt the company can put ahead of these notes. Which subsidiaries guarantee them and which can be designated unrestricted. What a majority of each series can strip from the minority with an exit consent." He taps the five maturities in the 10-Q's debt table. "If the company can change any of those, the strike isn't a number. It's an offer."

Kaz writes on the whiteboard, under the DD: *F is fixed until maturity (assumption).* Then, after a moment, a question mark.

*Beat 2. 22 March 2023: the strike is for sale.* Carvana launches private exchange offers for its unsecured notes: up to \$1.0 billion of new 9.0%/12.0% cash/PIK toggle senior secured second-lien notes due 2028, an acceptance priority that serves the 2025s first, consideration of between \$632.50 and \$808.75 per \$1,000 depending on the series, and a minimum participation condition of \$500 million (Carvana 8-K and press release, 22 March 2023). Before launching, it has designated its ADESA auction business an unrestricted subsidiary, releasing ADESA's guarantees of the existing notes. Chapter 5 walks through the bond-by-bond arithmetic. On the equity desk the arithmetic is simpler. An exchange that swaps a dollar of unsecured face for 63 to 81 cents of new secured face is a lower strike for the call. If it works, the equity's distance to default jumps, not because the firm is worth more but because the firm owes less to the people who stand between it and the shareholders.

Mags reads the release twice and puts it down. "They just told you the strike is for sale."

"At what price?" Theo asks.

"That's the negotiation. The company has named its opening bid. The holders haven't named theirs." The stock closes March at \$9.79 (vendor data), more than double its late-December low. The company's own fair-value disclosure puts the whole note stack at about 53.8% of carrying value at 31 March, up from 44.8% at year-end (Carvana 10-Q for the quarter ended 31 March 2023 and FY2022 10-K, Level 2 disclosures; ratios computed). Both legs of the pair have moved against each other in the expected proportion. So far the pair is working.

Then, for ten weeks, the holders do nothing in public. The offer is extended on 19 April, with the consideration for the 2025s raised; extended again on 3 May; pushed to 1 June on 17 May. On 2 June 2023 Carvana announces that the offers have expired and been terminated because the \$500 million minimum was not met (Carvana 8-Ks, 19 April, 17 May and 1 June 2023). The stock closed May at \$12.92 (vendor data).

#### Decision point: the pair on 2 June 2023

*Friday 2 June 2023. You are running the pair: long Carvana unsecured notes, short Carvana stock, sized at a Merton hedge ratio set when the DD was near zero. The stock has more than tripled from its low; the note stack was last marked by the company at about 54% of carrying value; a \$1 billion secured exchange, offered at what the company called a premium to market, has just failed because the holders, evidently organized, refused it together. Keep the pair, flip it, or stand aside? And what in the documents decides it?*

The desk argues for most of the afternoon. Kaz wants to keep the pair and re-hedge by the model: the DD has risen, so $N(d_1)$ has risen and the ratio $N(-d_1)/N(d_1)$ of §6.9.2 has fallen, so the model wants *less* short stock per bond, a trim rather than a cut, and Kaz is honest enough to say that this is exactly the moment a hedge ratio computed from a fixed-strike model should be trusted least. Ines wants to cut the equity leg. "The table has changed. The holders turned down a secured offer at a premium. That's not people who expect a recovery of thirty. That's people who expect to be paid more, and the only way they get paid more is if the company survives with a smaller stack and a bigger equity cushion. Every scenario where they win is a scenario where the short leg loses."

Theo answers the second question, because it is his question. "What decides it isn't the model. It's three clauses." The first is the secured-debt basket: how much new secured debt the indentures allow ahead of the unsecureds, which caps how far the company can push the holders without their consent. The second is the unrestricted-subsidiary capacity that let ADESA walk out of the guarantee package; the company has already used some of it, and the question is how much is left. The third is the amendment threshold: exit consents need only a majority of each series, so a holder who refuses an exchange that a majority accepts may be left in a note stripped of its covenants. "Put those together and the holders have a credible threat and so does the company. They'll do a deal. The question for the pair is what the deal does to the strike, and a deal that cuts face and extends maturity is a deal that makes the call worth a lot more."

Mags votes to stand aside, on the grounds that she has seen this hand before and the person who wins it is usually the one who did not have to be in it. Ines cuts the equity short in half. Kaz writes the dissent in the risk log.

*Beat 3. 17–19 July 2023: the restrike.* The deal arrives as a Transaction Support Agreement, signed on 17 July and announced on the 19th with Carvana's second-quarter results. Holders of more than 90% of the unsecured notes, about \$5.2 billion, have agreed to exchange into three new series of senior secured notes due 2028, 2030 and 2031, of up to \$4.376 billion in aggregate, alongside a cash tender for some of the 2025s; Carvana commits to raise at least \$350 million of new equity, with its controlling shareholders buying a share (Carvana 8-K, Exhibit 99.1, 19 July 2023; prospectus supplement and Q3 2023 10-Q). The release says the deal eliminates more than 83% of the 2025 and 2027 maturities, cuts total debt by more than \$1.2 billion, and reduces cash interest by more than \$430 million a year for two years. It names the advisers: Moelis and Kirkland & Ellis for Carvana; PJT Partners and White & Case for the noteholder group (Carvana 8-K, Exhibit 99.1, 19 July 2023). And it carries a quotation from John Zito of Apollo, one of the holders: "Apollo is pleased to support this debt exchange agreement ... Working with Carvana, PIMCO, Ares and the ad hoc group of noteholders" (same exhibit).

Theo reads the cleansing materials at his desk and has an odd feeling he takes a minute to place. He recognizes the deck: not the deal, which he was never staffed on, but the house style. The way the pro forma capitalization table is shaded, the footnote numbering, the font on the recovery bridge. Somewhere across the table from every model the desk ran that spring, his former colleagues had been building the barrier move by hand.

The stock closes at \$55.80 on 19 July, up 40.2% in a day (vendor data). S3 Partners estimates that short sellers lost about \$646 million that day and about \$2.18 billion year to date, with short interest at 47% of the float (S3 Partners via Yahoo Finance, 19 July 2023; secondary). The bonds rally too, but by points, not multiples. Ines looks at the pair's P&L and says only, "Half was the right size to cut. All of it would have been righter."

*Beat 4. 1 September 2023: settlement.* The exchange closes with 96.4% participation, about \$5.52 billion of the \$5.725 billion stack (Carvana 10-Q, Q3 2023). Of that, \$402 million of 2025s were tendered for \$341 million in cash, 85 cents on the dollar; the rest was exchanged into \$4.193 billion of new secured notes, whose early coupons could be paid largely in kind. Per dollar of old face, the 2027s got about 76.8 cents of new secured paper, the 2028s about 66.6, the 2029s about 64.9, and the high-coupon 2030s about 89.3 (computed from the 10-Q's tables). Exit consents stripped substantially all of the restrictive covenants from the old indentures, so the \$205 million left behind sits in notes with almost nothing to protect it. Carvana books an \$878 million gain on the extinguishment of debt. In Merton's language, every one of those numbers is a change to the contract the model treats as fixed: the strike fell, the maturity extended, the priority of the old holders moved up, and the minority that stayed out discovered that its "same" claim was now a different, weaker one.

*Beat 5. Afterward: the leg it was sure about.* From the close of 30 December 2022 to the close of 29 December 2023, Carvana's stock rose from \$4.74 to \$52.94, about 1,017%; at 31 December 2024 it closed at \$203.36 (vendor data). Over 2023 the company's own fair-value ratio for its notes went from 44.8% to 84.7% (computed from the 10-K and 10-Qs). Run an illustrative pair through those two numbers: \$10 million face of notes bought at 45, \$4.5 million of market value, hedged with a \$1 million short of stock. Using the fair-value ratio as a proxy for the bond leg, the notes gain about \$4 million over the year before coupons. The short loses about \$10.2 million. The pair, built to profit from the outcome everybody believed, lost on the leg it was sure about *(illustrative; no public source quantifies capital-structure-arbitrage losses on Carvana directly, so the loss is inferred from the equity and bond marks)*. The coda arrives three years later. The 2030 secured notes, born in September 2023 at a 13% PIK coupon for their first year, were refinanced in August 2026 with a term loan priced at SOFR plus 2.25% (Carvana 8-K, 14 August 2026). The put had been restruck, and then the company grew back into the old strike anyway. Figure 6.5 shows the stock's path and the pair's P&L at each quarter-end of 2023.

Here are the facts again, compressed, and then the reading.

**The facts.** Carvana's stock fell roughly 99% from its August 2021 peak (about \$370) to its December 2022 low (about \$3.70) (approx.), while its senior unsecured notes traded at deep discounts. On July 19, 2023, Carvana announced a transaction support agreement with holders of more than 90% (about \$5.2 billion) of its senior unsecured notes (SEC Form 8-K, Exhibit 99.1, July 19, 2023). The exchange, completed September 1, 2023 with 96.4% participation, swapped about \$5.52 billion of unsecured notes for about \$4.2 billion of new senior secured notes, plus a \$341 million cash tender for the 2025 notes, cutting total debt by more than \$1.2 billion and annual cash interest by more than \$430 million for two years, in part because the new notes allowed interest to be paid in kind in the early years (Form 10-Q, Q3 2023). Carvana's FY2023 10-K recorded an \$878 million gain on debt extinguishment. The equity then rallied hard through 2023–25 (approx.).

**The Merton reading.** In December 2022 a Merton DD on Carvana would have been near zero: equity a deep out-of-the-money call, debt mostly a short put. But Merton assumes $F$ and $T$ are fixed until maturity. The exchange changed both. Face value fell (a lower strike for equity), maturities extended (more time value for the call), and the old unsecured holders who participated moved up the priority ladder by taking secured paper at a discount to face. It was a *negotiated barrier move*: the call did not expire worthless because the people short the put agreed to restrike it. For the participating noteholders this was rational: a secured claim at 76% of face beat an unsecured claim on a firm heading toward the barrier.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The opportunistic credit fund and the multi-strategy pod, on the same July day.* To an opportunistic credit manager sitting in the ad hoc group, 19 July 2023 was the day a thesis paid off. That seat had bought or held the unsecured notes at deep discounts, refused the spring exchange, and negotiated its way into secured paper with a higher priority, PIK coupons and a company with more equity beneath it. Its public posture was support, not triumph: Apollo's statement in Carvana's release said it was "pleased to support this debt exchange agreement," working with Carvana, PIMCO, Ares and the ad hoc group (Carvana 8-K, Exhibit 99.1, 19 July 2023). In that seat the stock's 40% jump is good news, because a bigger equity cushion under your new secured notes is a better credit. To the risk manager of a multi-strategy platform, the same day looks like a limit breach. A pod running the long-bond, short-equity pair is judged on its own drawdown, and most platforms cut a portfolio manager's capital, or the manager, when a loss passes a pre-set line. Giuseppe Paleologo, who ran risk at large multi-manager firms, described pod stop-losses on *Odd Lots* as a crude form of insurance on a manager's left tail, needed because a manager paid a share of profits is in effect holding a call option on the book (*Odd Lots*, "How to Succeed at Multi-Strategy Hedge Funds," 20 May 2024; paraphrased). A short leg that moves 40% in a day against a heavily shorted float does not wait for the bond leg to catch up; it forces the pod to buy back stock into the squeeze, which is part of why the squeeze happens. Same capital structure, two seats: one owns the outcome and is paid for patience, the other owns a path and is stopped out by it.

### 6.10.2 Hertz: the hand that hit

The Carvana post-mortem runs long, and at some point in the second hour somebody asks the question every desk asks after a squeeze: "Is this just Hertz again?" Mags groans, because she lived through Hertz, and Theo, who was on the other side of the restructuring industry at the time, says he would like to work it out from scratch. Ines tells him to do it at the whiteboard, live, in front of everyone. This is one of the desk's rituals and it is not a kind one.

"Chapter 11 in May 2020," Theo says, writing. "Rental demand gone overnight, a fleet financed in asset-backed facilities, stock under a dollar. In my old job we'd have run the recovery waterfall and told the old equity it was getting nothing." He draws the hockey stick from §6.2.1. "But the equity is a call. In a bankruptcy the strike is roughly everything owed, and if the firm is worth about what it owes, the call is at the money. There's a rule of thumb for an at-the-money call: it's worth about $0.4\,\sigma_V\sqrt T$ times the asset value." He picks numbers. Asset volatility of 30%, a year to plan confirmation. "Point four times point three is point one two. So even in Chapter 11, with assets equal to debt, the equity is worth about 12% of the firm. Not zero."

"Closer to fourteen," Kaz says. "Your rule of thumb is for zero rates. Put in 4% and the strike's present value drops, and the call is worth about 13.8% of $F$." Kaz has, of course, already done Exercise 6. Theo writes 13.8 next to 12 and moves on, slightly annoyed, which is the correct emotion.

"Fine. The equity has option value. But it's a call on the fleet, and the fleet is the problem. They're going to have to dump cars into a used-car market that's collapsing with everything else, because the asset-backed lenders want their collateral back. $V$ goes down, not up. The option expires worthless."

Mags lets him finish, and then says, "No." Used-vehicle prices did not collapse. Through 2020 and into 2021, as new-car production stalled and demand for used cars rose, the value of a fleet of rental cars went *up* (approx.). "You were modelling the fleet as a pile of depreciating metal," she says. "It was a pile of metal everyone suddenly wanted. The asset wasn't the business. The asset was the cars, and the cars had their own volatility, and the equity owned that volatility."

Theo looks at his hockey stick for a while. He was wrong about the fleet in exactly the way a recovery analyst is trained to be wrong: he priced the asset at its downside, because in his old job the downside was where the claims got settled. Merton prices the asset at its whole distribution, and the part of the distribution that mattered for Hertz's old shareholders was the right tail. "So the model was right and I was wrong," he says.

"The model was right about the *value*," Ines says. "Hold on to that word. We'll get to the trade."

The second half of the story is about who bought the right tail. By the spring of 2021 the cars were worth more, the business was recovering, and the question in court was no longer whether creditors would be paid but who would own the company after they were. The answer was a group of plan sponsors. Knighthead, Certares and Apollo-affiliated funds led an investor group that committed "over \$5.9 billion of new equity capital," and the company's emergence announcement described the result as "paying our nearly \$19 billion of creditors in full" (Hertz Global Holdings, Form 8-K, Exhibit 99.1, filed at emergence). The offering documents show the shape of the money: about \$2.781 billion of common stock bought by the sponsors, \$1.5 billion of preferred placed with "Apollo and certain syndicated investors," and a \$1.635 billion rights offering backstopped by the group (Hertz, Form 424B4 prospectus). In Merton's language, the sponsors were buying a call that had moved into the money while the company was still in court, at a strike they negotiated, and paying the unsecured creditors par plus accrued so that the old equity's sliver could survive. Apollo, as the offering documents describe the structure, appears as a buyer of post-reorganization equity and preferred in a solvent estate rather than as a creditor.

Theo does the last piece of arithmetic himself, because Ines is waiting for it. "So the old shareholders who held through got \$1.53 a share in cash, plus a share of 3% of the new company, plus warrants or rights. The people who bought at the June 2020 peak paid \$5.50." He stops. "They lost money on a stock that survived bankruptcy."

"And the people who bought at fifty-nine cents made a fortune," Mags says. "Same stock, same bankruptcy. That's not a model problem. That's a price problem."

Ines takes the marker. What follows is the hand history.

**The facts.** Hertz filed for Chapter 11 on May 22, 2020. Its stock then rose from about \$0.59 to about \$5.50 in June 2020, roughly a 1,000% move. On June 11, 2020 the company sought to sell up to \$1 billion of new stock under a prospectus warning that the shares "could ultimately be worthless"; it sold about \$29 million before the SEC raised objections and the program stopped, and the stock was delisted in October 2020 (secondary sources). At emergence on June 30, 2021, old common stockholders received \$1.53 in cash per share, a pro rata share of 3% of the new common stock, and a choice of warrants (89,049,029 warrants with a \$13.80 strike, expiring June 30, 2051) or rights to buy new stock at \$10.00 per share as part of a \$1.6 billion offering, of which \$1.3 billion was subscribed (SEC Form S-1, October 2021). The fleet, Hertz's main asset, had risen in value as used-vehicle prices climbed through 2020–21 (approx.).

**The Merton reading.** Equity is a call on $V$. In June 2020 the call looked dead by every recovery analysis Theo's old shop would have run. Then $V$ moved: the asset was a fleet of cars, and used-car prices rose. The out-of-the-money call moved into the money while the company was still in court, so old equity was not wiped out. Merton's payoff diagram was right. What broke was the RSSG instinct that a bankruptcy filing means the strike has already been evaluated. In Chapter 11 it has not. The option keeps its time value until confirmation.

> **Poker Table.** Ines replays the hand. "Retail paid \$5.50 for a ticket that eventually paid \$1.53, plus 3% of the company, plus warrants. The pot odds were terrible. The hand still hit." She turns over the cards. "Don't confuse the two. Ninety-nine times out of a hundred, post-petition equity at a 1,000% rally is the worst bet at the table." Then the markout: who sold the stock to those buyers? The company itself, through an equity offering that said, in writing, that the stock could be worthless. "When the most informed seller at the table offers to sell you chips and tells you they might be fake, the hand history writes itself." That the buyers got lucky later does not change who knew more at the time.

### 6.10.3 How the desk would have flagged both

Three systematic signals were available from public data, and the easiest way to see them is to walk the desk's screens on the morning of 19 July 2023, the day Carvana's support agreement hit the tape, with Theo doing the walking and Kaz narrating.

The first screen is the structural one, and it shows **DD momentum turning up while the CDS or bond curve stayed inverted**. Carvana's distance to default had been climbing since January, as the stock rose from under \$5 toward \$40, while the notes, even at the company's own end-of-June mark of about 74% of carrying value, still priced a restructuring rather than a recovery to par. Equity says the distance is growing while credit still prices near-term default, which is the equity-leads-credit pattern of §6.9.2. "The stock had been telling us for six months," Kaz says, "and we had been hedging it instead of listening to it." Hertz's equity told the same story in June 2020, louder and less reliably.

The second screen is the risk report, and its red cell is a **hedge-ratio breakdown**. The pair's realized bond-to-equity sensitivity over the spring had fallen far below the Merton ratio the position was sized at: the stock was moving many times as much as the bonds per unit of model-implied asset move. When the empirical bond-to-equity sensitivity falls far below the Merton ratio, the capital structure is being renegotiated rather than diffused, and Dynkin's p.87 warning about analytical hedge ratios applies with force in the HY tail. Theo, looking at the cell, says what Ines had said in June in different words: the hedge was measuring a world in which the strike was fixed, and the world had stopped cooperating.

The third screen is not a model at all. It is the news and filings feed, and on 19 July it carries the support agreement itself, which is the purest example of a **liability-management covariate**. A transaction support agreement, a cooperation agreement among holders, or new secured capacity under the indenture should shift the model from "barrier fixed" to "barrier negotiable," with a categorical outcome (exchange, bankruptcy, survival) instead of a binary one (Chapter 5, §5.6). In Carvana's case every one of those had been visible for months: the March exchange offer revealed the secured capacity, the ADESA designation revealed the unrestricted-subsidiary capacity, and the failed minimum in June revealed that the holders were acting together. A desk whose model had a slot for any of them would have changed its view before the morning of the 19th rather than on it.

The trade in Carvana's case was the capital-structure pair: long the unsecured notes that would be invited into the exchange, against equity or against the notes that would be left behind (Chapter 22). Scene B is the long argument for which of those two hedges you wanted. Against the notes left behind, the pair was long the claim that got restruck and short the claim that got stripped; against equity, it was short the one security that benefited most from the restrike.

## 6.11 The Engine: One Module for Structural and Intensity Models

### Specification for AI Implementation

The blocks below run in order in one interpreter.

Before any finance happens, the engine needs a way to complain. The first time ROBO built this chapter's engine, it reported a one-year PD of 0.00% for every name in the high-yield index and did so with perfect composure, because nothing in the run had been given the job of objecting. A desk automates its sanity checks for the same reason it automates the morning P&L explain: the model is right ninety-nine days out of a hundred, the person who would have noticed on the hundredth is asleep, and the hundredth day has money in it. Block 0 is therefore not finance. It is the harness that makes every later block answer for itself.

Block 0 does three small things. It imports the toolkit once: `numpy`, the normal distribution and Spearman rank correlation from `scipy.stats`, the two root-finders the chapter needs (`fsolve` for the two-equation KMV system and `brentq` for the one-dimensional CDS bootstrap), and `warnings`, which the bootstrap uses to flag trouble. It creates `rng = np.random.default_rng(42)`, a single module-level generator from which every Monte Carlo block draws. And it defines `check(label, ok, detail="")`: a name, a boolean, and an optional string carrying the evidence. It prints one line that begins `[PASS]` or `[FAIL]` and then runs `assert ok, label`. There is no return value. The output is the printed line and, on failure, an `AssertionError` that halts the interpreter at the first broken claim instead of letting later blocks build on it.

The pattern is worth copying for anything you intend to leave running while you sleep, with one habit: print the evidence even when the check passes, so the log says `err=0.0e+00` and not merely "OK". Months later, a log that shows the margin is how you learn that a tolerance has been drifting toward its limit. These are acceptance tests on synthetic inputs where the right answer is known, not monitors on production data, and they should not be confused with each other.

The edge case that bites is the shared generator. Every simulation draws from one stream, so inserting a new Monte Carlo block, or swapping the order of two, changes every random number downstream. A check that passed at 2.4 standard errors can then fail at 3.1 with nothing wrong in the model. That is also why the simulation checks are written as "within 3 SE" rather than "equals": about one correct run in 370 lands outside three standard errors by chance. When a simulation check fails after a refactor, restore the draw order before hunting for a bug. A smaller trap: `assert` is removed when Python runs with `-O`, in which case `FAIL` still prints and nothing stops, so run the checks unoptimized.

Block 1 is Merton under $\mathbb{Q}$ for prices and $\mathbb{P}$ for PDs. The spread uses `log1p` on the put fraction so that tiny spreads do not vanish into rounding.

A whiteboard Merton is a pleasure: \$100 of assets, \$80 of zero-coupon debt due in a year, a normal table, and a calm Friday. The desk's version of the same problem arrives at 5 p.m. as three thousand firms, each with its own leverage and volatility, plus a risk committee that wants to know why today's spread differs from yesterday's. That is the case for turning the formula into one vectorized function. Pricing the debt becomes a single call, the whiteboard example becomes the unit test, and the argument about measures, which is the argument that costs real money, gets settled in the function names instead of in the meeting.

Start with the helper `_d1d2`. It clamps `sigma_V` into the range 0.0001 to 5.0, raises `ValueError` if `V0`, `F`, or `T` is not strictly positive, and returns $d_1$ and $d_2=d_1-\sigma_V\sqrt T$. `merton_prices` then returns four numbers in a fixed order: equity $E_0$ as the call, debt $B_0$ as discounted face times $N(d_2)$ plus $V_0N(-d_1)$, the continuously compounded spread, and the risk-neutral default probability $N(-d_2)$. The spread is computed as $-\ln(1-\text{put}/Fe^{-rT})/T$, with the put fraction floored at zero and passed through `log1p`, which keeps a spread of a tenth of a basis point from rounding away. `merton_pd_P` is the other half: it takes a real-world drift `mu`, shifts the log drift to $\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2$, and returns the distance to default and $N(-DD)$. The $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{P}$ outputs never share a function, so a $\mathbb{Q}$ number cannot wander into a loss forecast without someone typing its name.

The demonstration is the §6.2.4 firm: $V_0=100$, $F=80$, $r=4\%$, $\sigma_V=25\%$, one year. It prints $E_0=24.78$, $B_0=75.22$, a 216 bp spread, a $\mathbb{Q}$ PD of 17.7%, and, with $\mu=8\%$, a distance to default of 1.09 and a $\mathbb{P}$ PD of 13.8%. Three checks follow. Equity plus debt equals assets to $10^{-10}$. A Monte Carlo of the discounted payoff $\min(V_T,F)$, using 100,000 antithetic pairs and a standard error built from the pair averages so that the antithetic correlation is respected, lands within three standard errors of the closed form. And the spread at $T=0.001$ is below $10^{-8}$, the short-end embarrassment turned into an assertion.

Use it as the kernel for single-maturity, zero-coupon capital structures with liquid equity. Do not use it for banks, for structures with coupons and several maturities, or for pricing anything shorter than a few months, where its spread is zero by construction. The edge case that bites is asymmetric policing: nonpositive `V0`, `F`, or `T` raises, but `sigma_V` is clamped silently. Pass 25 where you meant 0.25 and you get a tidy, well-formatted answer for 500% asset volatility. The guard that catches a unit error on volatility lives one block later, in the KMV solver.

Block 2 is KMV: the two-equation solver with the analytic Jacobian of §6.4.2, the vectorized equity inversion, the VK iteration, and the Bharath–Shumway naive DD.

The screen shows a stock price. Merton needs the value of the firm. Nobody quotes $V_0$, and nobody quotes the volatility of assets either, because assets do not trade; they are inferred, which means the pricing formula has to be run backward. Run backward for one name, it is a pleasant exercise in Newton's method. Run backward for three thousand names every morning before the open, including the one whose equity just halved and whose solver wandered off into negative volatility, it is the part of the pipeline that ends up in the incident report. So the desk wants three things, not one: an exact solver for today's cross-section, a time-series method that does not trust a single day's equity volatility, and a cheap fallback that always returns a number.

`merton_equity_vol` runs the model forward: given $(V_0,\sigma_V)$ it returns equity value and the Itô-implied equity volatility $\sigma_E=N(d_1)\sigma_VV_0/E_0$. The tests use it to manufacture consistent inputs. `solve_kmv(E0, sigma_E, F, r, T)` is the backward direction. It first rejects any `sigma_E` outside 5% to 300% with a message asking whether the number was annualized, then solves the two equations for $(\ln V_0,\ln\sigma_V)$ with `fsolve` and the analytic Jacobian of §6.4.2, so positivity is automatic. Both residuals are relative errors, and the start is equity plus discounted face. After the solve it checks three things (residual below $10^{-9}$, asset volatility between 1% and 300%, asset value at least equity) and raises `RuntimeError` otherwise, with a message telling the caller to fall back to the naive DD and flag it.

`invert_equity` is the time-series workhorse: a vectorized Newton iteration that finds $V$ for every weekly equity value at once, starting right of the root, where convexity guarantees monotone convergence. `kmv_iterate` wraps it in the Vasicek–Kealhofer loop. It starts from the equity-return volatility delevered by $E/(E+Fe^{-rT})$, inverts all weeks, re-estimates annualized volatility from the implied asset returns, and repeats until the change is under $10^{-6}$, returning the asset path, $\sigma_V$ and the iteration count. `naive_dd` is Bharath–Shumway: no solver, with $\sigma_V=w\sigma_E+(1-w)(0.05+0.25\sigma_E)$ and $w=E/(E+F)$.

The checks: the true firm's $\sigma_E$ of 88.8% round-trips to $V_0=100$ and $\sigma_V=25\%$ within $10^{-6}$. VK on a simulated 156-week path returns 29.4% after 15 iterations; the five-point tolerance is loose on purpose, since three years of weekly returns pin volatility down to a point or two at best and the path's own realized volatility is 27.4%. Across 200 random firms the naive DD ranks with a Spearman correlation of 0.99 against the solved one.

Use the exact solver for the daily screen and the naive DD as the labeled fallback. VK holds $F$ constant across the window, so it misleads for a name that re-levered mid-sample. The edge case that bites: `kmv_iterate` annualizes with `periods_per_year=52` and neither raises nor warns if you hand it daily closes, so the volatility comes out about 2.2 times too small.

Block 3 is Black–Cox, with the second term of the passage formula computed in log space so that a large $e^{-2mx_0/\sigma_V^2}$ cannot multiply a tiny $N(\cdot)$ into `inf * 0`.

Merton asks one question, once, on the maturity date. A covenant asks every day, a cross-default asks whenever a cousin of the issuer misses a payment, and anyone who has read a credit agreement knows the firm does not wait politely until year five to find out whether it has defaulted. First passage turns the question into the right one: has the asset value touched the line at any time before $T$? There is a closed form for a constant-volatility firm and an exponentially moving barrier, and there is a simulation that can be bent to fit a barrier nobody has written a formula for. The desk wants both in the engine. The closed form is the answer you trust, the simulation is the thing you will modify, and a simulation you have never seen agree with the closed form is one you cannot modify safely.

`black_cox_pd` works in the log distance to the barrier, $x_0=\ln\big(V_0/(Ke^{-gT})\big)$, and raises `ValueError` if it is not positive. The log distance drifts at $m=\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2-g$, and the function returns the sum of two terms: the probability of being below the barrier at $T$, and the probability of having crossed and come back, $e^{-2mx_0/\sigma_V^2}N(\cdot)$. The second is computed as the exponential of a sum using `norm.logcdf`, so a large exponent never multiplies a tiny tail probability into `inf * 0`. Under $\mathbb{Q}$ the caller passes $\mu=r-q$; under $\mathbb{P}$, the real-world drift.

`black_cox_mc` simulates the same log distance for 200,000 paths at once, with a Python loop only over time steps. A path dies if it lands at or below zero. With `bridge=True` each path also draws a uniform number and dies if it falls under the Brownian-bridge probability of having touched the barrier between two grid dates, $\exp(-2x_ix_{i+1}/\sigma_V^2\Delta t)$. It returns the PD and its binomial standard error.

The test is deliberately cruel: five years on only twelve time steps, with $V_0=100$, $K=70$, $g=2\%$, $\mu=6\%$, $\sigma_V=25\%$. The closed form gives 38.8%. The bridge simulation gives 38.7%, with a standard error near a tenth of a point. The naive simulation gives about 30.6%, eight points low, because it misses every dip below the line that starts and ends between two grid dates. A third check sets $\mu$ so that $m=0$ and confirms the PD is exactly twice $N(-x_0/\sigma_V\sqrt T)$.

Use the closed form for the constant-barrier problem and the simulation as the scaffold for variants: stepped barriers, jumps, discretely tested covenants. The bridge assumes continuous monitoring; a covenant tested only at quarter-end on reported numbers is closer to the naive grid, and the bridge would overstate it. The edge case that bites is the meaning of `K`: it is the barrier at the horizon $T$, and today's barrier is $Ke^{-gT}$. Read today's covenant level off the credit agreement, pass it as `K`, and whenever $g\neq0$ you have quietly moved the barrier.

Block 4 is the CIR intensity: closed form, exact simulation, and a diagnostic that shows how often naive Euler goes negative when Feller fails.

A structural model says default is the day assets touch a line. A reduced-form model says default is a surprise that arrives at a rate, and that the rate itself moves: it climbs when the market gets nervous and drifts back when the nerves settle. The square-root (CIR) intensity is the standard tractable version, and its survival formula is a clean closed form built from two functions, $A$ and $B$, that every textbook writes with slightly different conventions. A formula copied from a PDF is a hypothesis. The desk's habit, which this block automates, is to turn the hypothesis into a test by simulating the same process with a method that shares no algebra with the formula, and to refuse to proceed unless the two agree. Add a diagnostic that shows what the lazy simulation does to you, and the block earns its place.

`cir_survival` computes the risk-neutral survival probability to $T$ as $A(T)e^{-B(T)\lambda_0}$. It builds $\eta=\sqrt{\kappa^2+2\sigma^2}$ and uses `expm1` for $e^{\eta T}-1$ so that short horizons do not lose precision to cancellation. All rates are annual, and `sigma` is the volatility of the intensity, not of assets.

`cir_mc` is the independent check. It raises if $2\kappa\theta<\sigma^2$, then steps the intensity with the exact transition law, a scaled noncentral chi-square with $4\kappa\theta/\sigma^2$ degrees of freedom, so the dynamics carry no discretization error. The integral of the intensity along each path uses the trapezoid rule, and the function returns the mean of $e^{-\int\lambda}$ across 50,000 paths with its standard error. `cir_euler_negative_share` is a diagnostic, not a pricer: it runs the naive Euler scheme with the square root floored at zero, so it never produces a NaN, and reports the fraction of paths that visit a negative intensity at any point.

With $\lambda_0=3\%$, $\kappa=0.5$, $\theta=4\%$, $\sigma=0.10$ and five years, the closed form gives 0.83523 and the simulation 0.83522, with a standard error of 0.0002. Then the diagnostic at $\sigma=0.30$, where $2\kappa\theta=0.04$ falls short of $\sigma^2=0.09$: 84% of the Euler paths go negative at some point.

Use CIR where you need dynamics rather than today's curve: scenarios, options on spreads, stress tests. Do not use it to separate $\kappa$ from $\theta$ with a single CDS curve, which barely identifies either. The edge case that bites: the Feller guard lives only in the simulation. The closed form returns a survival probability for any positive parameters, while the sampler refuses to run when Feller fails, so a calibration that drifts across the line keeps pricing on one screen and stops scenarios on another. Validate parameters once, upstream.

Block 5 is the CDS engine of §6.8: piecewise-constant hazards, both legs, a sequential Brent bootstrap that flags rather than hides negative buckets, and the credit triangle.

The market hands the desk par spreads at a handful of tenors. The risk system wants a survival curve, because marking, counterparty adjustments and bond-versus-CDS relative value all need probabilities and not quotes. The map from one to the other has no closed form once real discounting and quarterly coupons are in the contract, so every CDS desk solves it numerically, every morning, for every name. This is the 13-week model industrialized: Theo's spreadsheet rolled cash forward one week at a time, and the bootstrap rolls a hazard forward one tenor at a time, each bucket solved given the ones before it. What turns a routine solve into a risk is silence. A bootstrap that quietly floors a negative hazard has just told the risk system a lie in the one place where the market was trying to say something.

`cum_hazard` integrates a piecewise-constant hazard. It builds the overlap between $(0,t]$ and each bucket, extends the last bucket to infinity, and takes the dot product with the hazards, for any array of $t$ at once; survival is then $e^{-\Lambda(t)}$. `cds_legs` prices a contract on quarterly dates. The premium leg per unit coupon (`rpv01`) sums, over the dates, the accrual fraction times the discount factor times the end-of-period survival probability plus half of that period's default probability, which is the accrual-on-default approximation. The protection leg sums $(1-R)$ times the midpoint discount factor times the probability of defaulting in each of 12 sub-intervals per quarter. It returns the coupon-weighted premium, the protection value and `rpv01`, and `par_spread` is protection over `rpv01`.

`bootstrap_hazard` does the work. For each tenor in turn it runs Brent's method on $[-1,5]$ for the hazard that makes the par spread of the contract maturing at that tenor equal the quote, holding earlier buckets fixed. The bracket includes negative values on purpose, so that the solver is able to return one. A negative result goes into a boolean mask and a warning, and is never clipped. `credit_triangle` is just $s/(1-R)$.

The checks: a five-tenor curve from 150 to 310 bp at 40% recovery and a 4% discount rate reprices every quote to $10^{-15}$, with hazards of 2.5%, 4.0%, 5.9%, 6.6% and 6.4%, none negative. A single 300 bp five-year quote gives 5.000%, which is $300/0.60$, when discounting is flat at zero, and 4.975% at a 4% rate, the gap being quarterly coupons paid in arrears. An inverted curve of 1,500, 300 and 250 bp at one, three and five years gives a middle bucket of $-6.3\%$, and the flag trips.

Use this for curves built from par spreads; it is not the ISDA standard model, which converts upfronts and uses ACT/360. The edge case that bites: once a bucket goes negative, every later bucket is solved conditional on it, so the 2.8% in the last bucket of the inverted curve is an answer to a question the first two buckets already got wrong. Fix the quotes or the recovery, then bootstrap again. A smaller trap: `T` must be a multiple of a quarter. A 2.3-year contract rounds to nine premium dates, so the premium leg stops at 2.25 years while the protection grid still runs to 2.3.

Block 6 closes the loop: it computes the Merton term structure of spreads for the §6.2.4 firm and asks what a CDS desk would infer from it.

The chapter has so far been two models talking past each other, and the sharpest disagreement is the short end. Merton says a firm that is solvent today cannot default tomorrow, and the CDS market has never believed it. Stated in prose, that is a talking point. Run through the machine, it is a number you can look at. The last block treats Merton as if it were a dealer quoting a CDS curve: take the structural spreads for the §6.2.4 firm at a ladder of tenors, bootstrap hazards out of them the way a CDS desk would, and see what comes back. It exists to turn the argument into a pass/fail, and to leave on the screen the fact that the two model families do not merely price differently. They use the word "default" in different dialects.

The block reuses $V_0$, $F$, $r$ and $\sigma_V$ from Block 1 and calls `merton_prices` once on a vector of seven maturities, from 18 days to ten years, which works because every function is vectorized over $T$. The spreads print as 0.1 bp at 0.05 years, 68 bp at three months, 216 bp at one year, then 213, 183, 160 and 135 bp at three, five, seven and ten years. Check L asserts the two features that matter: the shortest spread is under 5% of the maximum, and the peak sits strictly inside the grid (here, at one year), so the curve is a hump and not a ramp.

Then it plays CDS desk. It drops the two sub-year points, 18 days and three months, which no single-name strip would normally quote, passes the rest to `bootstrap_hazard` with 40% recovery and a 4% discount rate, and prints the hazards: 3.6%, 3.5%, 2.2%, 1.5% and 0.9%, with no negative buckets. Warnings are suppressed because the mask is printed anyway. The final line, "All chapter 6 checks passed," is reachable only because `check` halts on the first failure.

Read the hazards as a statement about shape: they fall with tenor, so the model's danger sits in the first few years. Use the exercise to compare a structural model with a quoted curve. Do not treat the hazards it produces as tradable, because they inherit the model's recovery and its blind short end.

The edge case that bites is the recovery convention. Merton's own one-year $\mathbb{Q}$ default probability for this firm is 17.7%, an intensity near 19% a year, yet the bootstrapped first-bucket hazard is 3.6%. Nothing is broken. A spread identifies only the product of hazard and loss given default, Merton's implied loss is about 12%, and the CDS convention assumes 60%. Compare the two models' hazards only after putting them on the same recovery, and ship $R$ with any hazard you hand to someone else.

## 6.12 Ways This Blows Up: The Consolidated List

Years after the Friday of the cold open, when the structural engine goes to the risk committee for its annual review, the desk holds a dry run the evening before, and Kaz, who wrote the list below, reads it aloud. The rule, which Ines invents on the spot, is that nobody may let an item pass without naming the time it happened to them or to someone they know. It turns out to be easy.

Kaz reads the first, $\mathbb{P}/\mathbb{Q}$ mixing, and Theo names his first week: the 2% vendor PD, the 3.4% CDS hazard, and the protection he wanted to sell because "the market is too scared." The second, the unannualized sigma, belongs to the bank desk whose vendor quietly switched to daily volatility over a weekend and turned a CCC packaging company into a utility. The third, equity vol used as asset vol, is the one Kaz admits to personally, from graduate school, "in a paper nobody read, which is the only reason nobody noticed." The fourth, Merton at the short end, is Mags's alphabetized list of firms that defaulted the week after the model said they couldn't. The fifth, the Gaussian mapping, is the 60% of Bohn and Stein's 25,000 firms whose DD of 4 would have rounded to never. The sixth, the unbridged Monte Carlo, is the covenant breach that happened in mid-February between two month-end snapshots and was recorded as a survival. The seventh, the silently floored negative hazard, is every distressed curve Ines has ever been shown by a junior quant who was proud of the fit. The eighth, RMV confused with RFV, gets Mags's "forty percent of what?" and a story about a recovery argument settled by lunch. The ninth, CIR going negative, gets an embarrassed silence from Kaz and a promise about full truncation. The tenth, banks treated as Merton firms, gets a single word from the whole table: "SVB." The eleventh, off-balance-sheet liabilities, gets "First Brands," and Mags adds, "and Enron, and every factoring programme I ever found in a footnote." The twelfth, the barrier that 50.1% of the lenders can amend by Friday, gets Theo's answer, because it was his old job: Serta for the left-behind lenders, Carvana for the equity short. By the end of the list nobody has had to invent anything, which is the point of it: every line is somebody's Tuesday.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** *Tape this next to the risk system.* (1) $\mathbb{P}/\mathbb{Q}$ mixing: a $\mathbb{Q}$ hazard in a loss forecast, or a premium shift applied twice. (2) Unannualized $\sigma_E$, which turns the high-yield index into a list of utilities. (3) $\sigma_E$ used as $\sigma_V$, which overstates PD by the leverage multiplier. (4) Trusting Merton at the short end, where its spread is zero by construction. (5) Gaussian DD-to-PD mapping, which assigns zero risk to most of the universe. (6) Discrete-monitoring bias in barrier Monte Carlo; bridge it. (7) Negative hazards from inverted curves, silently floored. (8) RMV recovery confused with RFV recovery; the numeraire matters. (9) CIR Euler schemes that go negative, and $\kappa$ and $\theta$ that one curve cannot separate. (10) Banks treated as Merton firms (concave assets, run risk). (11) Off-balance-sheet liabilities left out of the default point. (12) A barrier read off a credit agreement that 50.1% of the lenders can amend by Friday.

- **Every credit model answers two questions: when is $\tau$, and what is $R$.** Structural models make $\tau$ a barrier crossing you read off a balance sheet; reduced-form models make it a surprise whose rate $\lambda_t$ you read off a curve. The desk runs both, in labeled measures.
- **Itô's tax is $\tfrac12\sigma^2$, and the convexity refund goes to whoever owns the option.** In a levered firm that is the equity; the bondholders sold it.
- **Equity is a call, debt is a riskless bond minus a put, and the fulcrum is the strike where the asset distribution has mass.** Merton gets the shape right and the short end wrong: zero spread at zero maturity. Jumps and noisy information fix it.
- **$\mathbb{Q}$ PDs exceed $\mathbb{P}$ PDs by a premium that grows with $\sqrt T$ and is proportionally largest for safe names.** The premium is carry, not alpha. IDO or it didn't happen.
- **Distance to default is a ranking variable first.** The probability scale comes from at least 400 defaults, preferably 1,000, through an empirical, monotone mapping. A naive DD that never fails beats a solver that fails silently.
- **A maintenance covenant is a Black–Cox barrier; cov-lite deleted it; an uptier moves it for someone else.** In 2026, default is a negotiation, so model it as a categorical event with a negotiable barrier, and bridge your Monte Carlo.
- **$s\approx\lambda(1-R)$, and $R$ is a quoting convention.** Bootstrap piecewise-constant hazards, reprice every quote, and flag negative buckets rather than hiding them.
- **The thirteen-week model was a hazard curve all along.** Its liquidity runway goes into the intensity as a covariate, next to DD momentum.
