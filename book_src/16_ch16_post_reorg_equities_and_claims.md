# Chapter 16: Post-Reorganization Equities, Rights Offerings & Bankruptcy Claims

> *"The latter, of course, suggests that knowledgeable insiders had a strong view that the equity was undervalued."* (Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.374)

## Cold Open: Effective Date, 9:31 a.m.

Tuesday, 9:31 a.m. A trucking-and-warehousing company that yesterday existed only as a first-lien term loan opens on the OTC market as common stock. The new ticker is five consonants long. At the plan's equity value the market cap is \$140mm. *(The company, its numbers and the banker in Mags's story, a composite of several early-2000s workouts, are fictional; Wolfspeed and the mechanics are not.)*

"Eleven bids," Theo says. "One offer."

"Whose offer?" Ines doesn't look up.

Theo has three windows open: the plan's distribution schedule, the ad hoc group's Rule 2019 statement, and a CLO trustee report he still has from his old life. "A CLO manager. They held twenty-two percent of the term loan. Now they hold twenty-two percent of the equity, and their indenture carries equity at zero in the overcollateralization test."

He scrolls the distribution schedule while he talks, because three weeks on this desk have taught him that the first answer is usually the summary line and the second answer is in the rows underneath it. The rows underneath are not one holder. The manager's twenty-two percent was allocated across eleven separate CLO vehicles, each with its own trustee, its own indenture and its own reinvestment period. Theo pastes the vehicle names into a spreadsheet next to the dates he can find in the trustee reports. Four of the eleven are past the end of their reinvestment periods and are amortizing, which means every dollar they raise goes to paying down their own notes and none of it can be recycled into a loan that pays a coupon. Three end their reinvestment periods inside the next six months. Four have more than two years left. The smallest vehicle holds 0.6% of the new company; the largest holds 4.1%.

"It's not one seller," he says. "It's eleven sellers who share a phone number. And they're on eleven different clocks."

"So every share they keep costs them cushion and pays them nothing." Now Ines looks. "What's the pot, what are the odds, and who at this table is *required* to fold, and by when?"

"I've been reading the disclosure statement," Kaz says. "It says the first-lien lenders were the fulcrum. Before anyone starts: I know it isn't a duration."

"It's the class where the value runs out," Theo says. "The debt above it gets paid, the debt below it gets warrants and apologies, and the fulcrum gets the company."

"So it's the point where the beam balances." Kaz considers this. "Mechanically, that's correct. Why does the beam want to sell itself?"

Mags arrives with coffee and the air of someone who has seen this film. "In 2003 we called these back-end equities. We bought them by phone from people who were embarrassed to own them. The trick was never the valuation. The trick was knowing who *had* to sell and waiting for them to get bored."

She sets the coffee down on the one corner of Theo's desk that is not a keyboard, which tells him a story is coming.

"There was a man at a regional bank," she says. "Workout group, nice suits, terrible tie. The bank had been a lender to a manufacturer that went through Chapter 11 and came out the other side owned by its lenders, so one morning he woke up holding a block of stock in a company he had spent two years trying to foreclose on. His credit committee did not want it on the books. Every Friday at 3:55 he called me and offered fifty thousand shares. Every Friday. I thought he was a nervous seller. I thought he got to the end of the week, looked at the position, felt sick and picked up the phone. So I'd bid him a little cheap, he'd grumble, and we'd trade."

"A mood," Kaz says.

"That's what I thought. Then one Friday in March he called at 3:55 and I asked him, just making conversation, why he never called on a Tuesday. And he told me. His committee had given him a reduction target in shares per week, and his position report went to the committee at 4:15 on Friday. He wasn't sick. He was filing a report." Mags drinks her coffee. "That was the day I realised the Friday calls were a schedule, not a mood. A mood you have to guess. A schedule you can *read*. I stopped bidding on Thursdays, because why would I compete with the other desks for stock that was going to walk in my door in twenty-four hours? I started keeping a little card with how much he'd sold and how much the committee wanted gone. And in June he didn't call. Friday, 3:55, nothing. I phoned him at four and he said, 'We're done.' The stock went up eighteen percent in the next three weeks, and the only thing that had changed about the company was that a man at a bank had stopped filing a report."

"We don't wait for people to get bored anymore," Ines says. "We model how fast they get bored."

Kaz has been looking at the bid stack. "Here's what I don't understand. Eleven bids, and they're all within a quarter of each other. If there are eleven buyers who each think this company is worth something different, the bids should be spread out. Somebody should think it's worth plan value. Somebody should think it's worth half. Instead it looks like the bids were set by a committee."

"They were," Ines says. "Just not a committee that met." She turns her chair. "Eleven bids at the same level isn't eleven opinions about trucking. It's a table where everyone is waiting for the same player to fold. Every one of those desks has read the same distribution schedule Theo has open. They all know who has to sell. So nobody wants to be the one who bids up, because the person you'd be paying is a forced seller who was going to come to you anyway. The bid isn't a valuation of the company. It's a valuation of the seller's patience."

"So the price is a statement about the seller," Kaz says slowly, "not about the asset."

"For now. That's the whole trick of this chapter. For some window, the price is about the seller. After the window closes, it's about the asset again, and you'd better have a view of the asset by then."

"How do you know which one you're looking at?" Theo asks.

Ines leans over and opens a chart on his second monitor. "Pull up Wolfspeed. Twenty-ninth of September, last year."

Theo knows the name. Wolfspeed, the silicon-carbide chipmaker, had come out of a prepackaged Chapter 11 in Houston that morning, with the old stock cancelled and a new stock on the same ticker. Old stockholders had received about 0.008352 new shares for every old share they had held, rising to about 0.013920 once a regulatory clearance released another tranche months later (company 8-Ks; ratios calculated from the share counts). The new stock opened at about \$18, against a plan equity value of about \$22 a share. What happened next was a range so wide that trading was halted for volatility for part of the session, and the stock still closed the day within a dollar or so of plan value (vendors differ on the exact opening and closing prints). Reuters reported it up about a third the following day.

"That looks like a disaster," Theo says, "and then it looks like nothing happened."

"It looks like price discovery," Ines says. "Who got Wolfspeed's new stock?"

Theo scrolls. "Convertible noteholders, mostly. The converts were the fulcrum. About twenty-four and a half million of the roughly twenty-six million shares went to them."

"And who holds converts?"

"Hedge funds. Convert arb, distressed funds, crossover."

"Who has an OC test?"

"Nobody." Theo sees it. "There's no CLO in that stock. Nobody in that cap table is carrying the equity at zero against a ratio test. Nobody has a disposition clock."

"So that wild first morning was a few thousand people working out, in real time, what a silicon-carbide company is worth with the debt cut, and they got there by lunch. Violent, but it was about the asset from the first minute. There was no seller to schedule." Ines taps Theo's first monitor. "This one's fulcrum was a CLO-heavy term loan. Twenty-two percent of the stock is held by eleven vehicles that are paid to hold loans and penalized for holding stock. Whatever range this thing trades in for the next three months, a large part of it isn't price discovery. It's supply."

"Supply that runs out," Mags says. "Eventually the man at the bank stops calling."

Theo looks at the screen. The plan says \$140mm. The bids say less. The CLO's offer sits there like a confession. Somewhere in this stock's first ninety days there is a return. The desk's question is how much of it is a *flow we can schedule*, meaning a known seller working down a known position against slow-arriving capital, and how much is *value we have to underwrite* the old way, with a model of a trucking business and a strong stomach.

He does the arithmetic on the offer. It is 11% under plan value. The bids are a quarter of a point below that. In his old job he would have known exactly what to do with an 11% discount to a number he had helped negotiate: he would have known it was too high, or too low, and by roughly how much, and he would have known which exhibit of the disclosure statement it came from. Here the number is the same and the question is different.

"So," Ines says. "The offer is eleven percent under plan value. Do you lift it, do you wait, or do you short the open and buy the trough? And what would you need to know first?"

Theo opens a fourth window and starts typing a list. *Which of the eleven vehicles can sell today. How fast the other seven have to follow. Who else got stock and what they're allowed to hold. Whether there's a rights offering, and who backstopped it. Whether there are warrants, and who has them. Whether the trade creditors are about to sell their claims and buy the stock.* He stops. "And what the plan value is," he says. "Not the number. Where the number came from."

"Good," Ines says. "Now you know what the chapter is for."

Theo's list is the chapter, one item at a time, and every item gets the same treatment: find the holder the waterfall chose and the market would not have, write down what forces their hand (an OC test, a disposition clock, a capital charge, a payroll), and price what they hold as the option it is. Hertz runs the whole circuit in one company, Spirit Airlines shows how fast the left tail can arrive, and at the end we come back to this screen at 9:31 a.m. to say which part of the discount is flow and which part is value.

A word on measures before we start. None of these instruments has a liquid hedge on the day it is born. Every expectation in this chapter is therefore under the physical measure $\mathbb{P}$ unless we say otherwise, and any $\mathbb{Q}$ that appears is the measure of some traded proxy (when-issued stock, peer equities, the high-yield index), not a true risk-neutral measure for the instrument itself. Kaz will try to write $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}$ out of habit. We will stop Kaz.

A word on notation, too. Arbitrage capital is $M_t$ (not $K$, which is a strike in §16.3); the warrant dilution ratio is $\omega$ (not $q$, which is inventory elsewhere in the book); distributable value and the allowed claim pool in §16.5 are $\mathcal{V}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ (calligraphic, so they cannot be confused with firm value $V_t$, a call price, or duration $D$).

## 16.1 The Back-End Equity Problem: What Emerges and Who Is Not Allowed to Hold It

### 16.1.1 Desk framing: a plan is a security factory with a distribution problem

At PJT you built the factory. You sat in the room where the waterfall was negotiated, you ran the recovery tables, and you watched the plan equity value move \$300mm in an afternoon because one side wanted more shares per dollar of claim and the other side wanted the juniors out of the money. You therefore already know the most important fact in this chapter: **the plan equity value is a negotiated number, not an estimate.** Moyer makes the same point from the buy side (Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.355 and p.369): investors who will control the reorganized company prefer conservative valuations, because a lower value means more shares for their claims and a cleaner path past the classes below them.

A restructuring partner would put the consequence in one sentence, and it is worth having it in your head before you look at any day-one tape: when the fulcrum class controls the process, confirmed plan values tend to sit below the values at which the same equity later trades or sells (Moyer p.355 and p.369 give the incentive; the regularity is the practitioner's, not a statistic we have measured). Envision Healthcare is a recent dated instance. Its AmSurg business emerged on 3 Nov 2023 with the second-lien lenders taking all of the equity, alongside a \$300mm rights offering priced at a 30% discount, and the plan projected a 73% recovery for that class. In June 2025 Ascension agreed to buy AmSurg for a reported \$3.9bn, which the trade press put above the top of the plan's valuation range (Octus, Jul 2025; Becker's; treat the exact gap as approximate). The second-lien lenders had not been wrong about the business. They had been on the side of the table that benefits from a low number, and the number had been low. Who negotiates the plan value, against which exhibit of the disclosure statement, and what a judge does when the two sides' bankers are \$300mm apart, is the subject of §12B.6 and Chapter 12-C; here the plan value is one side's opening bid that the other side accepted.

A plan converts claims into a basket of new instruments, and the desk translates each one into option language, because that is the only language in which they can be compared. The base case is the share itself, a call on enterprise value struck at the new debt (Chapter 1, §1.5, has the picture; Chapter 6 has Merton's version): a reorganized company worth 100 with 80 of new debt has equity worth 20, and if the business later ends at 70 the equity is worth 0, not −10. A warrant that pays only the equity above 30 is then a call on that call, worthless unless enterprise value tops 110 (§16.4).

To see the basket arrive, follow one fictional creditor through an effective date. Call it Ninefold Credit Partners, a mid-sized fund that owned a little of everything in the trucking company's capital structure. On the effective date its first-lien term loan disappears and is replaced by two things: a stack of new common stock, because the first lien was the fulcrum, and a smaller new term loan, because the plan handed part of the old claim back as take-back debt. As a member of the first-lien class Ninefold also received subscription rights, a short-lived right to buy more new stock at a fixed price, and because it sat in the ad hoc group and signed the backstop commitment it was obliged to buy whatever the rest of the class did not, in exchange for a fee. Its small second-lien position, which the waterfall left out of the money, came back as warrants. A trade claim it had bought from a tire supplier came back as an interest in a litigation trust, and a handful of tiny claims it had picked up in a bundle were paid in cash under the convenience class. One fund, seven instruments, and only some of them are things Ninefold would have chosen to buy:

| Instrument | In option language | Typical recipient | Who cannot comfortably hold it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| New common stock | A call on enterprise value struck at the new debt (Merton, Chapter 6) | Fulcrum class | CLOs (carried at zero in OC tests), banks (disposition clock), insurers (capital charges) |
| Warrants | A call on that call, usually long-dated and out of the money | Junior classes, old equity | Anyone who needs liquidity (see §16.4) |
| Subscription rights | A short-dated call struck at the rights price | Eligible members of a class | Holders with no cash, ineligible holders |
| Backstop commitment | A short put plus a fee | "The party" (§16.3) | Everyone not invited into the room |
| Take-back debt | Debt, with a recovery haircut baked in | Senior classes | Nobody, which is why CLOs ask for it |
| Litigation-trust interests | A non-transferable lottery ticket on lawsuits | General unsecured creditors | Anyone who wants to sell it |
| Cash | Cash | Administrative, 503(b)(9) and convenience claims | Nobody has ever objected |

If you read only one column of that table, read the last one: it says who will be selling each instrument to you and why they have no choice, and it is where the trading lives. Each instrument is born in the hands of a holder chosen by the legal waterfall, not by the market, and the waterfall does not care whether that holder is permitted to own what it receives. A CLO that held the fulcrum loan gets stock its documents treat as worthless. A bank that took equity in a debt-for-equity swap has a regulatory clock and an unpleasant capital charge while it waits (Moyer p.370). An insurer pays a far higher risk-based capital charge on common stock than on the bond it used to own. Index funds cannot own the stock until it lists and seasons. The old equity holders, who were told for a year that they would get nothing, get warrants they did not ask for and cannot value. The only natural holders on the first day are hedge funds, and they know they are the only natural holders.

So the first quantity the desk writes down is not a valuation. It is the **overhang share**: constrained holdings as a fraction of the stock that can actually trade.
$$\phi^{\text{OH}} = \frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{c}} n_j}{n^{\text{float}}}, \qquad n^{\text{float}} = n^{\text{out}} - n^{\text{ctrl}} - n^{\text{lock}},$$
where $\mathcal{J}^{c}$ is the set of constrained holders (CLOs, banks, insurers), $n_j$ is holder $j$'s share count from the plan's distribution schedule, $n^{\text{out}}$ is shares outstanding, $n^{\text{ctrl}}$ is shares held by a controlling holder who will not sell, and $n^{\text{lock}}$ is shares under a post-confirmation lock-up (Moyer p.372 discusses why controllers sometimes restrict trading early on). In the cold open, if a sponsor holds a locked 35% and the CLOs hold 22%, the investable float is 65% of the company and the overhang share is $22/65 \approx 34\%$ *(illustrative)*. A third of everything that can trade is in the hands of someone who has to sell it. Moyer's related point (p.369) is that the investable equity cap, not the headline cap, is the "technical factor" that determines who can even look at the stock: a controller holding half the shares halves the cap a fund can buy into.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.370:* "Banks and collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) are not natural holders of equity securities. The business model of these entities causes them to significantly prefer income earning assets as opposed to equities. ... Similarly, CDOs usually have contractual limitations on the absolute amount of and length of time they are allowed to hold equity."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer wrote this in 2004 about CDOs. In 2026 the relevant vehicle is the broadly syndicated loan CLO, and the sentence has become more true, not less. Moyer treats the forced seller as a qualitative hazard that a careful investor "will certainly consider" (same page) and that a careful plan designer avoids by offering the constrained class a non-equity recovery (p.371). The systematic desk reads the same paragraph as a *specification*: "absolute amount" is a position cap, "length of time" is a deadline, and together they define a supply schedule with a start date, a size, and an end date. That is something you can forecast, and anything you can forecast you can trade ahead of. The modern departure is in the docs: post-2020 CLO indentures let managers hold restructured *loans* more patiently (Trend Watch in §16.2), which means plans now hand CLOs take-back paper wherever the debt capacity exists. Where it does not, because the business can only support so much new debt, the equity overhang that remains is *more* concentrated in CLO hands and *more* predictable.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The CLO manager.* Sit for a moment in the chair of the portfolio manager who owns those eleven vehicles. From that seat the new stock is not an investment opportunity; it is a hole in a ratio. A CLO's overcollateralization test compares the par of its loans to the par of the notes it issued, and the public commentary on these documents is blunt about what happens to a restructured asset that comes back as stock: Weil's June 2024 note on CLOs as restructuring creditors observes that managers generally want to avoid that outcome because equity may be discounted entirely in the OC test, and S&P's *Par Wars* (23 Sep 2022) describes how assets far from the collateral-obligation definition are carried at zero. So a \$10mm loan position that becomes \$8mm of stock at plan value removes \$10mm from the numerator and puts back nothing, and the stock pays no coupon to cover the notes' interest. Sell it for \$7mm, an eighth below plan value, and buy a performing loan at 98, and the manager puts about \$7.1mm of par back into the test the same afternoon *(illustrative)*. If the test is close to its trigger, holding the stock risks diverting cash away from the CLO's own equity investors and the manager's subordinated fee; selling it at a discount repairs the ratio immediately. That is why, on the creditor committee months earlier, this manager argued for take-back paper instead of shares and lost only because the business could not carry more debt. The manager is not a fool for selling cheap; the indenture makes holding cost more than the discount. So the discount is not a mistake to be exploited once. It is a price the seller is willing to pay, every week, until the position is gone.

### 16.1.2 What history says the stock does next

The academic record on post-reorg equity is a textbook example of an anomaly that was published, studied, and then eaten by the people who read the studies.

> **Trend Watch.** *The post-reorg premium: discovered 1999, gone by 2010.*
> - **1980–1993.** Eberhart, Altman & Aggarwal (1999), *The Equity Performance of Firms Emerging from Bankruptcy*, *Journal of Finance* 54(5): 131 emerging firms earned large positive excess returns over the first 200 trading days on every benchmark the authors tried (check the exact range in the paper). The authors read it as an expectational error, not a risk premium.
> - **2001–2003.** Moyer (p.374), citing Imperial Capital and Bloomberg data, reports that back-end equities from the 2001 and 2002 emergence vintages beat the S&P 500 handily in 2003, and cites a study of 117 post-reorg equities over 1986–2003 that also found outperformance.
> - **1981–2019.** Jiang, Wang & Yang, *The Disappearing Outperformance of Post-reorg Equity* (summarized on the Duke FinReg Blog, 17 Sep 2021), track every US public filer with at least \$50mm of assets that filed in 1981–2016 and emerged public. Mean 200-day abnormal returns are 40% to 50%, but **the median abnormal return is zero**: the gains live in the right tail. An equal-weighted, monthly rebalanced portfolio from 1992 (about 20 stocks at a time) beat risk-adjusted benchmarks by 7.2% a year, concentrated in OTC names. Five-factor alpha was 0.8% to 1.2% a month in 2000–2010 and **zero after 2010**, and exchange-listed post-reorg stocks earned *negative* alpha in the most recent decade. Institutional ownership of post-reorg equity rose from about 40% in the 1990s to about 80% in the 2000s.
> - **What it means for the desk.** Buy-and-hold "post-reorg beta" is dead. What survives is (a) right-tail skew, which only a diversified book harvests (§16.8), (b) the OTC and illiquid segment where institutions still cannot or will not go, and (c) the *timing* of forced flow, which is the subject of §16.2.

Read the third bullet twice. "Mean 40%, median zero" is not a footnote; it is the whole strategy. Buy three post-reorg stocks: one halves, one goes nowhere, one rises 170%. The mean is $(-50 + 0 + 170)/3 = +40\%$ and the median, the middle one, is 0%. The mean is what a portfolio of many such stocks earns; the median is what happens to *your* stock if you own only one. A thing that can lose at most 100% but gain 1,000% cannot have a symmetric distribution, and its long right tail drags the mean above the median, like one tall person dragging up a room's average height (Chapter 5 tours the distribution family). So a single post-reorg stock is a lottery ticket with a positive expected value and a coin-flip chance of doing nothing, and the premium is a property of a *portfolio*, available only to someone who can own twenty of them at once without moving the price. We come back to that arithmetic in §16.8, with a jump-to-zero added, because Spirit Airlines insists.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.374:* "A 1998 study concluded that outperformance is most pronounced during the first 200 days after a stock emerges and in those situations where institutional investors demanded to receive only equity in the restructuring."
>
> **Annotation.** Two features in one sentence. The first is the 200-day window, which reappears in every later study and which §16.2 explains mechanically: 200 trading days is about four supply half-lives for a seller that executes at the rate the fallen-angel data imply, which is the point by which roughly 93% of the forced supply has cleared. The second is the more interesting one for a former restructuring banker: *who elected equity.* Many plans offer creditors a choice between a cash-out (or take-back paper) and stock, and the election results are disclosed in ballot certifications and distribution notices. A sophisticated holder that elects stock over cash at the plan value is revealing a private valuation, which is exactly Moyer's next sentence about insiders who thought the equity was cheap. The desk turns this into a feature, the equity-election share of informed holders. The modern departure is that Jiang, Wang & Yang's rise in institutional ownership means the informed electors are now usually the marginal holders on day one, so the signal is weaker than it was in 1998, and it is strongest precisely where the electors are few and the forced sellers are many.

## 16.2 Forced Selling as a Supply Process: From Fallen Angels to Orphan Equity

### 16.2.1 Desk framing: borrow the cleanest experiment on the shelf

We do not have a clean, large-sample study of CLO-driven selling in post-reorg stocks. We do have one of the cleanest natural experiments in all of credit: the fallen angel. When a bond is downgraded from investment grade to high yield, a large population of holders with investment-grade mandates is *required* to sell, regardless of what they think the bond is worth. That is the post-reorg CLO problem with a different label on the folder. Dynkin's account (Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.140, p.151, p.158) and Ben Dor's (Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.27, p.341) give us the shape: trading volume roughly quadruples around the event while bid-ask spreads *widen* (sellers paying for immediacy, not information), downgraded issuers underperform their peers by 15.09% into the event, and the price recovers over the following one to two years.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.27:* "We present strong empirical evidence of the transient price pressure experienced by these securities due to forced selling driven by investment constraints. A study of the time course of this effect shows that this price pressure tends to peak about three months after the downgrade. Over a period of one to two years following the downgrade, most securities recover."
>
> **Annotation.** Three facts a model must reproduce: the pressure is *transient* (it mean-reverts, so it is not information), it *peaks after the event* (so the sellers do not all hit the bid on day one), and it decays over *one to two years* (so there is a slow component, too). A model in which constrained holders dump everything at the open fails the second fact; a model with a single exponential decay will fail the third, as we show in §16.2.3. For post-reorg equity, the "event" is the effective date or the end of a lock-up, and the "investment constraint" is a CLO indenture rather than an investment-grade mandate. The departure from the bond setting is that equity has no coupon to pay you while you wait, and the post-reorg stock may have no analyst coverage at all (Moyer p.371), so the arbitrage capital that eventually absorbs the overhang arrives even more slowly.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.151:* "In the case of forced selling, upgraded issuers should not exhibit a reversal, since they are not subject to induced buying (or selling) by investors."
>
> **Annotation.** This is identification by asymmetry, and it is the best idea in this section. Overreaction would predict reversals in both directions; a constraint predicts a reversal only on the side where somebody is forced to trade. Dynkin finds upgraded issuers outperform by only about a third as much (5.66% vs. −15.09%, same page) and without the reversal. The post-reorg analogue gives us a cross-sectional test the desk can actually run: compare emergences where the fulcrum class was held mainly by CLOs (large forced supply) with emergences where it was held mainly by hedge funds (almost none). If the post-emergence drift is a constraint effect, it should scale with the CLO share of the fulcrum, and it should be absent where that share is near zero.

Theo's 13-week cash flow model makes its appearance here in an unexpected costume. In his old life the 13-week model was a schedule of cash *inflows and outflows by week*, with known dates and estimated sizes. The overhang model is the same object for shares: a schedule of forced *outflows by week*, with known start dates (the effective date, the lock-up expiry) and estimated sizes (the distribution schedule). Theo's USB stick, it turns out, contained the right data structure all along.

He has the template half built when Mags rolls her chair over. "Who sold?"

"The CLOs. Eleven vehicles." He has the names and the sizes from the distribution schedule, the Rule 2019 statement and the trustee reports, and in a \$140mm company 22% is a month or two of trading on its own; in a multibillion-dollar, index-eligible emergence the same percentage would be noise. "The sellers are identifiable and they *are* the volume."

"Did they elect?"

He does not know what she means, and says so. Many plans let the fulcrum choose between more stock and more new debt, and a CLO manager with any choice chooses the debt. The result sits in the ballot certification, three exhibits from where he had stopped reading. "Half the time," Mags says, "the forced seller you're excited about already sold you their problem in the form of a term loan, and the stock is owned by people who wanted it." Theo opens the exhibit. The eleven vehicles had asked for paper and been told the business could not carry it, so the overhang exists. The other ways the borrowed shape fails, a hedge-fund fulcrum like Wolfspeed's, an earnings release inside the half-life, and sellers who speed up into a bid, are on the list at the end of §16.2.4.

### 16.2.2 Derivation: overhang, slow capital, and the drift you can schedule

Mags's banker gives us the whole model in one anecdote: a fixed position, a seller working it down on a schedule, buyers who arrive when they notice, and a price that is depressed while the seller is still calling and recovers when he stops. What follows writes that story in three equations and then asks what return it leaves on the table for the patient buyer.

Let $v_t$ be the fundamental log value of the stock, which we treat as a $\mathbb{P}$-martingale over a 90-day horizon (we ignore the equity risk premium, which is small over that horizon compared with the effect we are measuring, and we will strip peer returns before fitting, as in Chapter 8). Let $Q_t \ge 0$ be the **overhang**: shares that constrained holders are required to sell and have not yet sold, measured in dollars at the plan value. Let $M_t > 0$ be the **arbitrage capital** willing to absorb that supply, in the same units. Three assumptions define the model.

> **Math, Gently.** *A random walk and a fair game.* Flip a coin every day; heads the stock goes up a step, tails it goes down a step. The path it traces is a random walk, and its most useful property is that your best forecast of where it will be next month is exactly where it is right now: the ups and downs you expect cancel. A process with that property is called a *martingale*, and that is all the word means: a fair game, in which no amount of staring at the past tells you which way the next step goes. Saying that the fundamental value $v_t$ is a martingale is therefore a modest assumption with a big consequence. It says the *business* gives you no predictable drift over the next 90 days, so any drift we can predict must come from somewhere else, here the seller and the buyers. Tiny number: if $v_t$ is 4.60 today (a log value, about \$100), our expectation for $v_t$ in three months is 4.60, even though it might end at 4.40 or 4.80. There is one wrinkle, about the difference between a fair game in log value and a fair game in dollars, involving a $\tfrac12\sigma^2$; Chapter 6 introduces it and §16.8.1 shows why it matters for this chapter's lottery tickets.

*Supply.* Constrained holders execute a fixed fraction of what remains per unit time:
$$dQ_t = -\theta\, Q_t\,dt, \qquad Q_{t_0} = Q_0,$$
where $\theta > 0$ is the execution rate (per year) and $t_0$ is the effective date or the lock-up expiry, whichever comes later.

> **Math, Gently.** *Exponential decay and half-life.* Picture a bathtub with the plug pulled, where the water drains faster when the tub is full and slower as it empties, because the flow is proportional to what is left. That is what $dQ = -\theta Q\,dt$ says: each instant, the sellers dispose of a fixed *fraction* $\theta$ of whatever they still hold, so big positions shrink quickly in shares and small ones slowly. The solution is $Q_t = Q_0 e^{-\theta(t-t_0)}$, and the natural way to read it is by its half-life, the time it takes to sell half of what remains: $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/\theta$. With $\theta = 3.4$ per year, the half-life is $0.693/3.4 \approx 0.20$ years, about 2.4 months. After one half-life half the overhang is gone; after two, three-quarters; after three, seven-eighths. The tub never quite empties in the equation, which is the mathematician's way of saying that the last few CLO vehicles always take longer than you planned. We fit $\theta$ to data in §16.2.3.

*Capital.* Arbitrage capital arrives slowly, in the spirit of Duffie's (2010) slow-moving capital:
$$dM_t = \rho\,(M^{*} - M_t)\,dt, \qquad M_{t_0} = M_0,$$
with $M^{*}$ the long-run capital that will eventually cover the name and $\rho$ its arrival speed (per year). The solution is $M_t = M^{*} + (M_0 - M^{*})e^{-\rho(t-t_0)}$.

*Price.* The observed log price carries a liquidity discount proportional to overhang per unit of capital:
$$p_t = v_t - \kappa\,\frac{Q_t}{M_t},$$
where $\kappa > 0$ is the price-impact coefficient (log points per unit of overhang-to-capital ratio).

Read the price equation as a sentence: the stock trades below what the business is worth by an amount that grows with how much the forced sellers still hold and shrinks with how much patient money has shown up. The tradeable object is the expected *excess* drift, the part of the return that is not fundamental. Because the business is a fair game, the only predictable motion in the price is the motion of that discount, and the discount shrinks for two reasons at once: the sellers are running out, and the buyers are arriving. The two-line calculation (quotient rule, then the martingale property) is in *The Full Derivation* below; the result is
$$\mu_t \equiv \frac{d}{dt}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}_{t_0}\!\left[p_t - v_t\right] = \kappa\left(\theta\,\frac{Q_t}{M_t} + \frac{Q_t\,\dot M_t}{M_t^{2}}\right).$$
Both terms are positive while capital is still arriving ($\dot M_t > 0$): the stock drifts up because the sellers are running out *and* because the buyers are showing up. With constant capital ($\rho = 0$, $M_t \equiv M$) everything is closed-form:
$$p_t - v_t = -\frac{\kappa Q_0}{M}\,e^{-\theta (t - t_0)}, \qquad \int_{t_0}^{\infty}\mu_t\,dt = \frac{\kappa Q_0}{M}, \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\theta}.$$
The total abnormal return available to a buyer at $t_0$ is the initial discount $\kappa Q_0/M$, and half of it is gone after one half-life. The boundary conditions are sensible: if $Q_0 = 0$ (hedge funds held the fulcrum) there is no drift at all, and as $M \to \infty$ (a large, well-covered name) the discount vanishes.

**Why the discount peaks late.** The constant-capital solution has its largest discount at $t_0$, which contradicts Ben Dor's "about three months after." The fix is to recognize that constrained holders do not receive all their shares on the effective date. Distributions settle over weeks, claims are allowed in batches, a when-issued market may trade before the shares are delivered (Moyer p.370), and lock-ups expire on a ladder. Think of the cold open's eleven vehicles: some got their shares on day one, some are waiting for the claims agent, and some cannot sell until a lock-up rolls off. So there are two taps on the bathtub. One pours shares *into* constrained hands at a rate that starts high and fades, with a mean lag of $1/\alpha$; the other drains them out at rate $\theta$. Early on, the inflow beats the drain and the overhang grows; later, the drain wins and it shrinks. The overhang, and with constant capital the discount, peaks at the moment the two taps run at the same speed, and that moment is always *after* the event, which is the second fact in Ben Dor's paragraph. For the cold open's parameters ($\theta = 3.4$ and $\alpha = 5$ per year) the peak lands at $s^{*} = \ln(\theta/\alpha)/(\theta-\alpha) \approx 0.24$ years, about 2.9 months. Its timing tells you the ratio of two speeds that you can estimate separately: how fast shares are delivered (from the claims agent's distribution notices) and how fast constrained holders sell (from volume and holdings filings). Figure 16.1 plots both cases for the cold open's parameters.

![Figure 16.1: When shares reach constrained holders over weeks, the liquidity discount troughs about 2.9 months after the event rather than on day zero, so buying the open means buying before the worst price. Illustrative: the §16.2.2 two-compartment model with θ = 3.4 per year, α = 5 per year and κQ₀/M = 15%.](assets/figures/ch16_overhang_delivery_lag.png)

Kaz, reading over Theo's shoulder, says this is "just a two-compartment pharmacokinetic model, the kind used for a drug dose moving from the gut into the bloodstream." Kaz is right, and we will let Kaz have this one, because the analogy is exact: the plan is the dose, the CLO is the gut, the market is the bloodstream, and the alpha is the side effect.

The practical consequence is the one in the figure's caption. A desk that buys the open is buying before the worst price, and a desk that waits for the trough needs to know roughly when the trough comes. That is a question about delivery mechanics, not valuation, and it is answerable from documents.

#### The Full Derivation

*The drift.* Differentiate the ratio with the quotient rule:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{Q_t}{M_t}\right) = \frac{\dot Q_t}{M_t} - \frac{Q_t\,\dot M_t}{M_t^{2}} = -\theta\,\frac{Q_t}{M_t} - \frac{Q_t\,\dot M_t}{M_t^{2}}.$$
Since $v_t$ is a martingale, $\tfrac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}_{t_0}[v_t] = 0$, and the deterministic discount is all that remains. Multiplying by $-\kappa$ gives the drift $\mu_t$ stated in the main text.

*The two-compartment solution.* Let shares flow *into* constrained hands at rate $Q_0\,\alpha e^{-\alpha (t-t_0)}$, so that a total of $Q_0$ arrives with mean lag $1/\alpha$, while those holders keep selling at rate $\theta$. Writing $s = t - t_0$ and starting from $Q = 0$:
$$\dot Q_s = Q_0\,\alpha\,e^{-\alpha s} - \theta\,Q_s \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q_s = \frac{Q_0\,\alpha}{\theta - \alpha}\left(e^{-\alpha s} - e^{-\theta s}\right), \qquad \theta \neq \alpha .$$
(Verify by substitution: $\dot Q_s + \theta Q_s = \frac{Q_0\alpha}{\theta-\alpha}\left[(\theta - \alpha)e^{-\alpha s}\right] = Q_0\alpha e^{-\alpha s}$.)

*The peak.* The overhang, and with constant capital the discount, peaks where inflow equals outflow, $\dot Q_s = 0$:
$$-\alpha e^{-\alpha s^{*}} + \theta e^{-\theta s^{*}} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s^{*} = \frac{\ln(\theta/\alpha)}{\theta - \alpha},$$
with the limit $s^{*} = 1/\theta$ when $\theta = \alpha$. The peak is always *after* the event, which is the second fact in Ben Dor's paragraph, and its timing tells you the ratio of two speeds that you can estimate separately: how fast shares are delivered (from the claims agent's distribution notices) and how fast constrained holders sell (from volume and holdings filings).

### 16.2.3 Fitting the execution rate to published aggregates

We can calibrate $\theta$ without a single post-reorg trade, by borrowing the fallen-angel reversal. Dynkin p.158 reports the reversal for the worst-hit bucket of downgraded bonds over three successive six-month windows after formation: +5.75%, +1.04% and +0.86%. Under the single-exponential model with constant capital, the reversal earned in window $k$ (each of length $\Delta = 0.5$ years) is the drop in the discount over that window:
$$w_k = A\,e^{-\theta(k-1)\Delta}\left(1 - e^{-\theta\Delta}\right), \qquad A = \frac{\kappa Q_0}{M}.$$
The ratio of consecutive windows depends only on $\theta$:
$$\frac{w_2}{w_1} = e^{-\theta\Delta} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\theta = -\frac{1}{\Delta}\ln\frac{w_2}{w_1} = -2\ln\frac{1.04}{5.75} \approx 3.42 \text{ per year},$$
a half-life of $\ln 2/3.42 \approx 0.20$ years, or about 2.4 months, and an implied total reversal $\hat A = 5.75/(1 - e^{-1.71}) \approx 7.0\%$ *(illustrative fit to published aggregates)*. The model then predicts a third window of $w_3 = w_2\,e^{-\theta\Delta} \approx 0.19\%$. The data say 0.86%. The single exponential is wrong in the tail, and it is wrong in exactly the direction Ben Dor's "one to two years" warned about: there is a second, slower component. In our model that component already exists. It is the capital-arrival term $Q_t\dot M_t/M_t^2$, which decays at the speed of the slow money ($\rho$), not the speed of the sellers ($\theta$). Fitting $(\theta, \rho)$ jointly needs more than three numbers, which is why Exercise 2 hands you a panel instead.

> **Math, Gently.** *Logs turn ratios into differences.* The fit above looks like a trick, so here is why it works. We do not know $A$, the size of the total reversal, and we do not need to, because it appears in both windows and cancels when we divide one by the other. What is left, $e^{-\theta\Delta}$, has the unknown $\theta$ stuck in an exponent, and the logarithm is the tool that brings exponents down to earth: $\ln(e^{x}) = x$, and more generally $\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b$, so a ratio of two numbers becomes a difference of their logs. Tiny number: the second window over the first is $1.04/5.75 = 0.181$. Its natural log is $-1.71$. That $-1.71$ must equal $-\theta \times 0.5$, so $\theta = 1.71/0.5 = 3.42$ per year. Two windows pin the speed without knowing the size, the same way two photographs of a draining bathtub, taken a known time apart, tell you the drain rate without telling you how big the tub was. The catch is that the trick assumes one drain; the third window's 0.86% is the tub telling you there are two.

Ines, who has been listening with the expression of someone watching a stranger misplay a hand, translates: "The fast decay is the forced seller folding. The slow decay is the table filling up with players who could have sat down on day one but didn't know the game was running. You get paid for the first by being early. You get paid for the second by being *patient.* Different edges, different capital."

### 16.2.4 From model to signal: momentum with an expiry date

The signal the desk actually trades is the model's expected excess drift, estimated name by name:
$$\hat\mu_t = \hat\kappa\,\hat\theta\,\frac{\hat Q_t}{\hat M_t}, \qquad \hat Q_t = \hat Q_{t_0}\,\big(1 - \hat x_t\big),$$
where $\hat Q_{t_0}$ is the constrained holders' allocation from the plan's distribution schedule (cross-referenced against the ad hoc group's Rule 2019 statements and CLO trustee reports, which list loan positions by holder), $\hat x_t \in [0,1]$ is the fraction already executed (from 13D/13G amendments, Form 4 filings for 10% holders, quarterly 13F changes, and volume above a peer-implied baseline), and $\hat M_t$ is a capital proxy (13F holder count, average daily volume, borrow availability).

This is time-series momentum, the subject of Chapter 10, with two differences that make it better than ordinary momentum. First, the *sign* is known before the first return prints: as long as $\hat Q_t > 0$, the expected drift is positive. Second, the signal carries its own **expiry date**. When the constrained holders' filings show them out, $\hat Q_t \approx 0$ and the drift is zero by construction. At that point the desk is no longer trading a flow; it is long an uncovered small-cap, and it should either underwrite the business properly or leave. Ordinary trend-followers find out that a trend has ended by losing money. This one tells you in a 13G amendment.

> **Quant-shop life: the holder registry that did not tie.** *(A fictional composite of a systematic credit asset manager; no real firm or person is depicted.)* Monday, 8:40 a.m., a mid-sized systematic credit fund in a converted office park. Dana, a second-year data analyst, has one job this week: reconcile the "executed fraction" feature, the $\hat x_t$ above, for the forty post-reorg names in the research universe before the quarterly model review on Thursday. The feature is supposed to be simple. For each name, take the constrained holders' allocations from the distribution schedule, subtract what their filings say they still own, and divide.
>
> By ten she has thirty-one names that tie within a rounding error and nine that do not. By lunch she is down to one that matters. The fund's 13F parser says a CLO manager still holds 1.9mm shares of a reorganized freight broker. The CLO trustee reports, which the fund buys monthly, say the vehicles sold the last of their stock in June. One of these is wrong, and the feature for the name is either 0.31 or 1.00 depending on which.
>
> The afternoon is archaeology. The 13F is not wrong, exactly; it is old. Holdings reports are filed up to 45 days after quarter end, and the fund's vendor loaded this one late, so the "current" position is a snapshot of 31 March. Worse, the stock traded over the counter until May, and an OTC stock that is not on the SEC's list of 13(f) securities need not appear on a 13F at all, so the 1.9mm shares may be a manual entry the manager chose to include. The trustee reports have their own problem: they identify the position by the issuer's *pre-bankruptcy* name and an internal asset identifier, not the new CUSIP, because the trustee's system was set up when the asset was a term loan. Dana's join on CUSIP had silently dropped every row.
>
> Tuesday and Wednesday go to an entity-resolution table: old legal name, new legal name, old loan identifiers, new equity CUSIP, ticker, the dates each became valid. It is four hundred rows of typing and checking against the plan's distribution notices, the kind of work a model-review note will one day call "manual and error prone." She opens a ticket with the data vendor asking for an issuer-history mapping for reorganized companies. The ticket is acknowledged, assigned, and, as of the time of writing, still open.
>
> On Thursday the model review takes eleven minutes. The head of research asks why the freight broker's signal changed since last quarter. Dana explains, and then says the part that makes everyone in the room put down their coffee: the signal for that name was already zero. The trustee reports were right; the CLO vehicles had sold in June; $\hat Q_t$ had been near zero for two months, and the model had stopped trading the name on its own, because the volume had fallen back to baseline. The reconciliation changed nothing in the portfolio. It changed what the fund *knew* about why. That is the point, and it is not a small one. The expiry date in this section is only as good as the holder registry behind it, and the registry is a thing someone builds by hand, on a Tuesday, in a spreadsheet nobody will ever thank them for.

> **Ways This Blows Up.**
> 1. *Using the plan value as $v_t$.* Plan values are biased low by the controlling creditors (Moyer p.355, p.369), so the stock can open *above* the plan value with a large overhang, the measured "discount" is negative, and the fit explodes. Anchor $v_t$ to the when-issued price or a peer-multiple band, never to the disclosure statement.
> 2. *Stale OTC prints.* Equal-weighted portfolios of illiquid stocks inherit bid-ask bounce as fake alpha (Blume and Stambaugh's classic bias). Jiang, Wang & Yang's surviving alpha is concentrated in OTC names, partly for this reason. Use quote midpoints and skip-a-day returns.
> 3. *Survivorship.* Spirit Airlines' first post-reorg equity went from emergence to a second Chapter 11 in under six months (§16.7). A backtest that drops delisted names will report a beautiful drift.
> 4. *Fundamental news in the residual.* The first earnings release after emergence moves the stock more than the overhang does. Strip peer and factor returns first (Chapter 8's IDO step) and exclude event windows. IDO or it didn't happen.
> 5. *$\theta$ is not a constant.* Sellers accelerate into liquidity. Dynkin p.140 reports volumes about four times normal with *wider* spreads around the fallen-angel event; a seller who sees bids will hit them. Let $\theta$ depend on depth, or at least refit it after any volume spike.

> **Trend Watch.** *CLO documents got patient with debt, not with equity, 2020–2026.*
> - **Before 2020.** Most broadly syndicated CLO indentures gave managers little room to hold or fund anything that was not a conforming loan. Law-firm guidance from the 2020 downturn (O'Melveny, *CLO Issues in Workouts and Debt Restructurings*, 2020; Seward & Kissel client notes) describes severe restrictions on acquiring and holding equity, and general prohibitions on providing new money in exchange for equity or other non-conforming assets.
> - **2021–2022.** S&P Global Ratings described the spread of "loss mitigation" and workout-loan language (a video briefing of 10 Mar 2021, and *Par Wars: U.S. CLO Document Provisions Evolve To Provide Managers More Flexibility*, 23 Sep 2022): workout loans that broadly meet the collateral-obligation definition are typically carried as defaulted obligations, while restructured assets further from that definition are carried at zero. Industry tallies reported a sharp rise in the share of new European CLOs with such language between 2020 and 2021 *(approx.; secondary summaries, primary source not verified)*.
> - **2023–2024.** S&P published a Credit FAQ on uptier priming and drop-down provisions in US CLOs (26 Jul 2023), and Weil's June 2024 note on CLOs as restructuring creditors observed that managers generally want to avoid a restructured asset being treated as equity, because equity may be discounted entirely in the OC test or, if it qualifies as a loss-mitigation loan, receive punitive treatment.
> - **What it means for the desk.** The forced-seller channel is contractual, not regulatory, and the contracts now distinguish sharply between restructured debt (holdable) and equity (not). Plans respond by giving CLOs take-back term loans wherever the business can carry them. The equity overhang that survives is therefore concentrated in cases where the fulcrum was a CLO-heavy loan *and* the debt capacity was thin: precisely the cases you can identify from the plan's capital structure before the effective date.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**The Spec You Hand the Robot.** *Vague version:* "Buy post-reorg stocks where CLOs have to sell." *What ROBO built:* a strategy that buys at the open on the effective date, the moment the overhang is largest and before a single share has been delivered, and then holds forever, because nobody told it when "have to sell" stops. *Precise version:* build `ForcedSellerOverhang`, a deterministic model of overhang $Q_s$, capital $M_s$, discount $-\kappa Q_s/M_s$ and drift $\mu_s$ on $s = t - t_0 \ge 0$ in years; support both instantaneous release and exponential delivery at rate $\alpha$; fit $\theta$ from window reversals and from a panel of IDO-stripped residuals; emit a signal that is exactly zero once the constrained holders have executed.

*Input schema (holder registry, one row per holder per instrument per as-of date):* `instrument_id` (internal string; never a guessed CUSIP), `holder_id`, `holder_type` in {`CLO`, `bank`, `insurer`, `hedge_fund`, `sponsor`, `retail`, `other`}, `shares` (float, millions), `source` in {`plan_schedule`, `rule_2019`, `13D`, `13G`, `form_4`, `13F`, `trustee_report`}, `as_of` (date), `lockup_end` (date or null). *Panel shapes:* time grid `s` of shape `(n_days,)` on a 252-day year; residual panel of shape `(n_names, n_days)`. *Edge cases:* $\theta = \alpha$ (use the limit formula), $M_0 \le 0$ (reject), zero overhang (signal zero, no division), negative mean residuals near zero in the log fit (mask them).

The spec above reads like a finished thought, which is exactly when it is most dangerous. The model has seven parameters, a closed form that changes shape when two speeds happen to coincide, and a fitting step that must recover an execution rate from a panel of noisy residuals nobody has seen yet. Each of those is a place where a sign error can sit quietly for a quarter. A desk that rebuilds this in a spreadsheet for every new emergence will find the bug in the P&L, which is the most expensive place to find anything. So the model goes into code, and the code arrives with its own proof obligations.

The listing has five layers. First, `ForcedSellerOverhang` holds the parameters of §16.2.2 and refuses nonsense at construction: negative overhang, price impact or capital speed, or a non-positive execution rate, capital or delivery rate, raises an error rather than drawing a plausible curve. Its methods return the overhang (single exponential when `alpha` is `None`, the two-compartment formula otherwise, with the $\theta = \alpha$ limit handled separately), the capital path, the log-price discount $-\kappa Q_s/M_s$, and the drift by the quotient rule, so slow capital is included. `half_life` and `peak_time` are the closed forms. Second, `theta_from_windows` is the §16.2.3 calculation, and it raises unless the first reversal exceeds the second and both are positive. Third, `simulate_residual_panel` builds what an IDO-stripped panel would look like (the model's discount plus independent daily noise for 200 names), and `fit_theta_loglinear` recovers $\theta$ by regressing the log of the negative cross-sectional mean on time over the first half-year. Fourth, `overhang_signal` computes $\hat\kappa\hat\theta\hat Q_{\text{rem}}/\hat M$ with remaining overhang clipped at zero, so a fully executed holder base gives a signal of exactly zero. Fifth, the assertions.

The assertions are the reason to prefer this to a spreadsheet, which will cheerfully compute a half-life from the wrong sign and say nothing. The calibration must reproduce 3.42 per year and the 2.4-month half-life, and must show the single exponential undershooting the observed third window, which is the model admitting it lacks a slow component. The drift, integrated with the listing's own `trap`, must equal the change in the discount. The numerically located trough must land on `peak_time`. Slow capital must add drift, and the simulated panel must return $\theta$ and the starting discount within ten percent.

Use it when you have a defensible allocation and a capital proxy and want an expiry-dated drift per name. Do not read it as a price forecast: it is a deterministic expected discount under $\mathbb{P}$ with fundamentals held as a martingale, and `overhang_signal` drops the delivery schedule and the capital-arrival term. The edge case that bites is the fit's `floor`. When the true discount in the window is close to the noise, the mean residual flips sign at random and its log is undefined, so the code keeps only days whose mean deviation is below the negative floor. That is a selection on the outcome, and it biases the slope flatter, meaning a slower $\theta$, if the window is long or the discount is small. Keep `max_years` short.

## 16.3 Rights Offerings and Backstops: Pricing the Put You Sold

### 16.3.1 Desk framing: the backstop is the player who agrees to call any bet

Start with an insurance company that almost never pays a claim. Three scholars, Vincent Buccola of the University of Chicago Law School with Adi Marcovich Gross and Matthew McBrady, went through 49 Chapter 11 cases since 2016 in which a group of creditors had agreed in advance to buy whatever part of a rights offering the rest of their class did not take. That promise is the backstop, and its whole justification is risk: the backstop party is said to be paid for standing ready to buy shares that might turn out to be worth less than the price. So the authors asked the obvious question. How often did that risk actually bite? In 19 of the deals there was an arm's-length estimate of what the securities were really worth, a market price or a later transaction, against which to check the exercise price. In 18 of those 19, the securities sold were worth more than the price paid for them, on average about twice as much (Buccola, Marcovich Gross & McBrady, *The Backstop Party*, *American Bankruptcy Law Journal* 100(1), 2026). The insurance policy paid out once in nineteen tries, and the people selling it were paid as if it paid out often. The rest of this section is about why that happens, how to price the policy, and how to tell when the premium is a fair price for risk and when it is a toll for being in the room.

A rights offering is a capital raise in which a class of creditors is offered new shares at a price set off the plan equity value, which (see §16.1) is a number the people setting it had every reason to set low. A backstop is a commitment, signed weeks or months before the offering funds, to buy every share the class does not take. The backstop party is paid a fee for this, usually in shares.

"Translate," says Ines, when Theo explains this. Then she does it herself. "The backstop is the player who agrees in advance to call any bet. Nobody does that for free. You do it if you're paid a fee, if you get to buy chips at a discount, and if you get to see everybody's cards before they decide whether to play. In a rights offering you get all three. That's why the seat is so crowded and the door is so narrow."

The reason the instrument exists at all is legal, and Moyer states it precisely.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.362:* "Thus, an unsecured creditor is extremely vulnerable when the debtor's circumstances are such that exit financing cannot be obtained in an amount sufficient to pay off all administrative claims; frequently this is a reason why a rights offering to raise new cash from creditors is included in plans of reorganization."
>
> **Annotation.** Administrative claims must be paid in full in cash on the effective date unless the holder agrees otherwise, so a plan that cannot raise enough exit cash cannot be confirmed. The rights offering is the plan's way of making its own creditors the lender of last resort. Moyer frames it as protection for unsecured creditors; the modern data (Trend Watch below) show that in practice it has become the dominant form of exit equity and a large transfer *within* a class, from the members who are not in the backstop group to the members who are. The desk's departure from Moyer is to stop asking "does the plan need the cash?" and to start asking "what is the put worth, and who got to sell it?"

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Houlihan_Lokey_Distressed_MA p.24:* "A backstopped rights offering maintains fairness for all existing shareholders and assures that the needed capital is raised, but, in turn, requires extra consideration for the "backstop" investor."
>
> **Annotation.** "Extra consideration" is the polite phrase for an option premium. Houlihan's example is a family-owned company with a well-capitalized insider as backstop, and its "fairness" claim holds when every holder can participate on equal terms. The 2025 *ConvergeOne* decision (Trend Watch below) is the legal system discovering what the derivation below shows mathematically: when only *some* members of a class are allowed to sell the put and collect the premium, the offering is not fair to the members who are left out, however equal the rights themselves look on paper.

#### When the put view applies, and when it breaks

The model below treats the backstop as a short put and asks what fee would compensate for it. That is the right lens when the commitment window is short, a few months from signing to funding, and when the reorganized equity has some tradable proxy, a when-issued market, listed peers, or the company's own bonds, from which a volatility can be borrowed with a straight face. Under those conditions the put has a meaningful value and the fee can be compared with it.

The lens breaks when the discount is so deep that the put is nearly worthless. If shares are offered at half of plan value, the equity has to lose half its value before the backstop party loses a dollar, and the fair fee for the put rounds to zero, yet the backstop party is still paid. In that case the economics are not in the put at all. They are in the reserved allocation (a slice of the offering set aside for the backstop group at the discounted price) and in exclusivity (the right to take up whatever others leave unexercised). Pricing the fee as insurance in such a deal is like pricing a lottery syndicate by the cost of the ticket. The money is in who gets to join.

So the first thing a seasoned practitioner checks is not the volatility. It is whether *the whole class was offered the seat*. Since 25 Sep 2025 that question has carried legal as well as economic weight. In *ConvergeOne*, Judge Hanen of the Southern District of Texas reversed confirmation of a prepackaged plan insofar as it gave a subset of first-lien lenders the exclusive right to backstop a \$245mm rights offering for a 10% premium, holding on §1123(a)(4) grounds that similarly situated members of the class had been treated unequally; in October 2024 the same court had refused to dismiss the excluded lenders' appeal as equitably moot. The debtors noticed an appeal to the Fifth Circuit on 22 Oct 2025. A Simpson Thacher note of 5 Feb 2026 reports that the debtors and majority lenders then entered a stipulation opening the backstop opportunity to the minority lenders; we found no Fifth Circuit merits opinion, so treat the appellate law as untested and the district court's reasoning as persuasive rather than settled. For the desk the point is narrower and more durable: in an exclusive backstop with no market test, the fee is no longer just an option premium. It is an option premium with a reversal risk attached, and the reversal risk is priced by forum, not by volatility.

### 16.3.2 Derivation: the right is a call, the backstop is a put

**Setup.** Let $n_0$ be the plan shares distributed before the offering (to creditors and direct investors, excluding fee shares), $m$ the number of rights-offering shares, and $K$ the subscription price, so the offering raises $mK$. Let $E$ be the pre-money value of the reorganized equity at the funding date, random as of the commitment date, and write the per-share pre-money value as $x \equiv E/n_0$. After the offering the company holds $E + mK$ of value across $n_0 + m$ shares:
$$S^{\text{post}} = \frac{E + mK}{n_0 + m} = \frac{n_0 x + mK}{n_0 + m}.$$
Subtracting $K$ from both sides gives the identity that drives everything:
$$S^{\text{post}} - K = \frac{n_0\,(x - K)}{n_0 + m}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad S^{\text{post}} > K \iff x > K.$$

**The right.** A rational holder subscribes if and only if $x > K$. Each eligible plan share carries $m/n_0$ rights, so the value of the rights attached to one plan share at funding is
$$\text{Right}(x) = \frac{m}{n_0}\left(S^{\text{post}} - K\right)^{+} = \frac{m}{n_0 + m}\,(x - K)^{+},$$
a call on the *pre-money* per-share value, struck at the subscription price, scaled by a single dilution factor. (The "theoretical ex-rights price" of equity-capital-markets folklore is just $S^{\text{post}}$ evaluated at today's $x$.)

**The backstop.** If $x < K$, nobody subscribes, and the backstop party must buy all $m$ shares at $K$, each worth $S^{\text{post}}$. Its loss is $m(K - S^{\text{post}}) = \frac{m n_0}{n_0+m}(K - x)$. If $x > K$, everybody subscribes and the backstop buys nothing. So the backstop's payoff is a short put:
$$\Pi^{\text{BS}}(x) = -N_{\text{eff}}\,(K - x)^{+}, \qquad N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{m\,n_0}{n_0 + m}.$$
Let $\text{Put}(x_0, K; \sigma, T)$ be the value of a put on $x$ struck at $K$, with $\sigma$ the volatility of the reorganized equity and $T$ the time from commitment to funding. The fair fee, expressed as a fraction of the capital raised, sets the fee equal to the put:
$$f^{*}\,mK = N_{\text{eff}}\,\text{Put}(x_0, K;\sigma,T) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f^{*} = \frac{n_0}{n_0+m}\cdot\frac{\text{Put}(x_0, K;\sigma,T)}{K}.$$
Because no liquid option exists on a company that has not emerged yet, we evaluate the put with the Black–Scholes formula under the assumption that $x$ is a driftless lognormal under $\mathbb{P}$ over the short commitment window (so the "$\mathbb{Q}$" here is nominal: zero rate, zero drift). Kaz asks why we don't just use the risk-neutral measure. Theo asks Kaz to name the traded instrument that spans it. Kaz goes quiet.

> **Math, Gently.** *What a put is, and what "risk-neutral" means.* Ines, who played cash games for a living, describes a put as the insurance some card rooms used to sell on an all-in hand: you pay a premium now, and if your hand loses, the insurer pays you the shortfall. A put on $x$ struck at $K$ pays $K - x$ if $x$ ends below $K$ and nothing otherwise, so the backstop party, which must buy shares at $K$ when they are worth only $x$, has *written* that insurance. Two things make the premium larger: more time before the cards are turned (a longer window $T$), and a wilder deck (a higher volatility $\sigma$), because both widen the range of places $x$ can land. Tiny number, using the worked example below: with $x_0 = 1$, $K = 0.75$, $\sigma = 0.70$ and $T = 0.5$, the model says $x$ finishes below 0.75 a bit more than a third of the time (about 37%), and when it does, the average shortfall is about 0.20. Multiply the two and you get the put's value, about 0.0734. Now the honest part. In textbooks a put is priced "risk-neutrally," which sounds like an assumption about attitudes and is really a statement about hedging: if you can trade the underlying stock continuously, you can build the put's payoff out of stock and cash, and the price of the put is simply the cost of that recipe, whatever anyone's appetite for risk. Nobody can hedge with a stock that has not emerged yet. So the zero-drift lognormal we use here is a *convention*, a reasonable way to turn a volatility guess into a number, and not a measure that any trade enforces. That is why Theo stops Kaz: the formula is the same, but the authority behind it is not.

**Worked number.** Put $x_0 = 1$, a rights price at a 25% discount to fair value, $K = 0.75$ (the average plan-value discount Buccola, Marcovich Gross & McBrady report; Trend Watch below), $\sigma = 0.70$ and $T = 0.5$. The Black–Scholes put is then worth about 0.0734 per unit of pre-money value, which is $\text{Put}/K \approx 9.8\%$ of the subscription price (the $d_1$, $d_2$ arithmetic is in *The Full Derivation* at the end of this subsection). Before reading the next sentence, notice what that 9.8% is: the price of insuring a buyer against a fall of more than a quarter in six months, for a stock as volatile as a newly reorganized company. With Hertz's dilution factor, $n_0/(n_0 + m) = 291.25/454.75 \approx 0.64$ (share counts in §16.3.4), the fair fee is $f^{*} \approx 0.64 \times 9.8\% \approx 6.3\%$ *(illustrative)*. That is almost exactly the 6% average fee that Seth reports for hedge-fund backstops. On this metric the market prices the backstop like a Black–Scholes put at 70% volatility.

Now vary the two inputs nobody negotiates in public:

| Discount to fair value | Fair fee, $T = 0.25$ y | Fair fee, $T = 0.50$ y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0% ($K = x_0$) | ≈ 8.9% | ≈ 12.5% |
| 25% ($K = 0.75\,x_0$) | ≈ 3.0% | ≈ 6.3% |
| 50% ($K = 0.50\,x_0$) | ≈ 0.3% | ≈ 1.6% |

*(Illustrative: $\sigma = 0.70$, dilution factor 0.64, zero rate; the code below reproduces the table.)* The table makes the chapter's central point about backstops. **The fee and the discount are substitutes.** A deep discount makes the put nearly worthless, because the equity would have to fall by half before the backstop lost a dollar. A backstop party that receives a 50% discount *and* a 10% fee is being paid twice for one risk, and the second payment is the larger one. Figure 16.2 draws the two payoffs and the fee curve behind the table.

**The all-in transfer.** The backstop party's take has four legs, not one:
$$\Pi^{\text{BP}} = \underbrace{f\,m\,S^{\text{post}}}_{\text{fee, paid in shares}} \;+\; \underbrace{a\,m\,(S^{\text{post}} - K)}_{\text{reserved allocation at a discount}} \;+\; \underbrace{(1-a)\,\eta\,m\,(S^{\text{post}} - K)^{+}}_{\text{exclusivity option}} \;-\; \underbrace{N_{\text{eff}}\,(K - x)^{+}}_{\text{the put}},$$
where $a$ is the fraction of the offering reserved for the backstop parties outright and $\eta$ is the fraction of in-the-money rights that other holders fail to exercise (ineligible holders, holders without cash, retail holders who never read the notice). Three things in this expression are routinely mispriced. The fee is paid in *shares*, so it is worth $f\,m\,S^{\text{post}}$, not $f\,m\,K$: when the discount is real, the fee is worth more than its headline. The reserved allocation is a pure transfer whenever $x_0 > K$. And the exclusivity option, the right to absorb whatever others leave on the table, is a free call. (We have ignored the small extra dilution from the fee shares themselves, which the code handles by valuing fee shares at the post-money price.)

#### The Full Derivation

*The Black–Scholes put in the worked number.* With zero rate and zero drift, the put on $x$ struck at $K$ is $\text{Put} = K\,\Phi(-d_2) - x_0\,\Phi(-d_1)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function. For $x_0 = 1$, $K = 0.75$, $\sigma = 0.70$ and $T = 0.5$:
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(1/0.75) + \tfrac12(0.49)(0.5)}{0.70\sqrt{0.5}} = \frac{0.2877 + 0.1225}{0.4950} \approx 0.829, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - 0.4950 \approx 0.334,$$
$$\text{Put} = K\,\Phi(-d_2) - x_0\,\Phi(-d_1) \approx 0.75(0.3693) - 0.2036 \approx 0.0734, \qquad \frac{\text{Put}}{K} \approx 9.8\%.$$
The term $\Phi(-d_2) \approx 0.3693$ is the model's probability that $x$ finishes below $K$, which is where the Math, Gently box's "about 37%" comes from, and dividing the put by it gives the average shortfall of about 0.20. Multiplying $\text{Put}/K$ by the dilution factor $n_0/(n_0+m) \approx 0.64$ gives the 6.3% fair fee in the main text.

> **Trend Watch.** *Rights offerings took over exit financing, and the courts noticed, 2003–2026.*
> - **Prevalence.** Gunjan Seth (USC Marshall), *Do Rights Offerings Reduce Bargaining Complexity in Chapter 11?* (CLS Blue Sky Blog, 11 Mar 2025): rights offerings financed about 35% of large Chapter 11 cases over 2003–2021 on an asset-weighted basis, rising to about 87% by 2019, with roughly \$46bn raised; hedge-fund backstoppers charge about 6% of the amount raised, buy about 43% of the equity, and appoint about 40% of the board; participants earn about 50% within three months; instrumental-variable estimates suggest rights offerings raise total creditor recoveries by about 40% and shorten cases by about seven months. (The paper's NBER abstract, February 2024, gives 47% of bankruptcies on a different basis.)
> - **The transfer.** Buccola, Marcovich Gross & McBrady, *The Backstop Party*, *American Bankruptcy Law Journal* 100(1) (2026): 49 confirmed plans with backstopped rights offerings, January 2016 to June 2024. Average discount to plan value 25%, about 50% effective; all-in backstop compensation 20.1% of the amount raised (25.1% in cases with market-priced values), against 3–7% underwriting spreads for IPOs and seasoned offerings. Per dollar of *identical* claim, backstop parties came away with about 21 cents of value against about 8 cents for other holders measured at plan values, and about 35 cents against 19 cents measured at market values.
> - **The legal regime shift.** The Fifth Circuit's *Serta* decision (31 Dec 2024) held that an uptier indemnity for some lenders violated the equal-treatment rule of §1123(a)(4), looking at a plan's actual effect rather than its form. On 25 Sep 2025, in *ConvergeOne*, the district court for the Southern District of Texas reversed confirmation of a prepackaged plan insofar as it gave a subset of first-lien lenders an exclusive backstop of a \$245mm rights offering, with a 10% fee and no market test, as unequal treatment. The debtors noticed an appeal on 22 Oct 2025; a Simpson Thacher note of 5 Feb 2026 reports that the parties then stipulated to open the backstop to the minority lenders, and we found no Fifth Circuit merits ruling as of October 2026. In contrast, LATAM's \$5.4bn backstop for a subset of unsecured creditors was approved after court-ordered mediation, on business-judgment grounds.
> - **What it means for the desk.** Model the fee leg as fee times $\Pr(\text{exclusive backstop survives} \mid \text{circuit, market test})$, with a jump in that probability at 25 Sep 2025 for Fifth Circuit cases with no market test. Commentators noted that the *ConvergeOne* court pointed to *Serta*'s "open market purchase" analysis as guidance on what a market test might look like, which means the most expensive three words in finance now also help decide who may sell the put.

### 16.3.3 Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality

> **Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality.** *Myth:* "The backstop fee is the price of the backstop." *Reality:* The fee is the smallest of four legs. In a 25%-discount offering at 70% volatility the put is worth about 6% of the raise; the discount on the backstop party's reserved allocation and the exclusivity option are worth more, and the fee itself is paid in shares that are worth more than their face whenever the discount is real. If you are not in the backstop group, your rights are worth exactly what the derivation says. If you are, your seat is worth several times that.

### 16.3.4 Case Study: the Hertz rights offering, June 2021

Tuesday, 2:10 p.m., a different week. Harlan, who runs the desk's event-driven book from two rows over, walks up to Theo's desk with a printout, which on this floor is a sign of either seriousness or age. *(Harlan and his deal are fictional; Hertz is not.)* The printout is the backstop agreement for a \$300mm rights offering by a reorganized building-products distributor. The rights are priced at a 30% discount to plan value. Six funds from the ad hoc group have committed to backstop it for a 10% premium paid in new shares. The desk owns some of the class's debt, so it will receive rights; it is not one of the six. The rights expire in three weeks.

"The committee wants to know if ten percent is rich," Harlan says. "I said I'd ask the guy who used to write these."

"I used to *negotiate* these," Theo says. "Writing them was the lawyers' problem." He thinks about it for a second, which is a second longer than he would have taken a month ago. "Rich compared to what?"

"Compared to the risk, presumably."

"Then we need a yardstick where we can see every share." Theo opens EDGAR. "Hertz. Same fee, ten percent, paid in stock. And the share counts are public to the last share, because the effective-date 8-K lists them."

Kaz wheels over, smelling an option. "We have the formula from this morning. Seventy percent vol, six months, twenty-five percent discount, the fee comes out around six percent. So ten is rich. Done."

"Six months is your number," Theo says. "What was Hertz's?"

The answer is in the dates, and Theo reads it to them from the filings while Ines, who has drifted over because a crowd at someone's desk is a market signal, listens with her arms folded.

The Hertz plan (§16.6 has the whole docket) raised \$1.635bn in a rights offering at \$10.00 per share, priced off an equity value of about \$4.7bn (Hertz 8-K/A, 14 May 2021). The offering was made first to Hertz's old stockholders, who could take either the rights *or* a package of 30-year warrants, but not both, and unexercised rights flowed to eligible holders of unsecured funded debt. The plan sponsors and other commitment parties backstopped it for a 10% premium paid in common stock at the same valuation. The rights expired on 15 Jun 2021. The share counts in the 8-K filed after the effective date (7 Jul 2021) are worth writing down exactly, because they let us audit every number:

| Item | Shares | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Rights shares to participants | 127,362,114 | Includes 7,858,805 to commitment parties |
| Unsubscribed shares to commitment parties | 36,137,887 | The put "exercised" against the backstop: the shares nobody else took |
| Backstop fee shares | 16,350,000 | $16.35/163.5 = 10.0\%$ of the offering, paid in stock at \$10 |
| Plan sponsors' direct purchase | 277,119,438 | \$2.781bn at the rights price |
| Old stockholders' 3% | 14,133,075 | |
| Total new common outstanding | 471,102,514 | The five rows sum exactly |

So $m = 127.36 + 36.14 = 163.50$mm shares (\$1.635bn at \$10), $n_0 = 277.12 + 14.13 = 291.25$mm, and the dilution factor in the derivation is $291.25/454.75 \approx 0.64$.

"Five rows that sum exactly," Ines says. "I like a table that ties."

"So here is what it says," Theo tells Harlan, and he gives the committee its answer in three parts, each of which, he points out, a systematic desk could have computed in real time from the filings, without anyone's permission. First, Kaz's six months was wrong for Hertz; the put lived for a few weeks, not half a year, and short-lived insurance is cheap. Second, the backstop group got something more valuable than the fee: a large, in-the-money block that other holders were structurally prevented from taking. Third, after the fact, the seat was worth more than a billion dollars, and almost none of that was payment for risk. Written down for the committee, the three observations a systematic desk would have made in real time:

1. **The put was short-dated.** The commitment-to-funding window ran from the May 2021 auction result to the 30 Jun 2021 effective date, about $T \approx 0.1$ to $0.15$ years. At $T = 0.1$ and 70% volatility, even an at-the-money put is worth only about 5.6% of the raise after dilution *(illustrative)*. A 10% fee was rich by the Black–Scholes yardstick before counting any discount.
2. **The unsubscribed block was large and in the money.** About 22% of the offering went unsubscribed, largely because old stockholders who preferred warrants could not also take rights. That is the exclusivity option in our formula, with a large $\eta$ supplied by the plan's own either-or election.
3. **Ex post, the seat was worth a fortune.** At the \$29 re-IPO price of 8 Nov 2021, the 36.14mm unsubscribed shares bought at \$10 carried a gain of about \$687mm, and the 16.35mm fee shares were worth about \$474mm, together about \$1.16bn on a \$1.635bn commitment *(approx.; derived from sourced share counts and prices, before any hedging or financing cost)*. Most of that is the stock's re-rating, not the put premium, which is exactly the point of the Buccola findings: the backstop seat is a levered long position in the plan's own discount.

Mags, who backstopped a rights offering in 2009 "with a fax machine and a prayer," reads the table twice. "Back in my day the backstop was the guy nobody else would lend to. Now it's the guy everybody wants to be. That's how you know the price is wrong."

Harlan looks at his printout. "So what do I tell the committee about *our* deal?"

"That the fee is rich and that doesn't matter much," Theo says. "Our discount is thirty percent, not twenty-five. When the six funds signed, with about three months to funding, the put they wrote was worth roughly two percent of the raise after dilution. Today, with three weeks left, it rounds to nothing *(illustrative; the §16.3.2 model at 70% volatility)*. They're being paid ten percent to insure against a fall that would have to be more than thirty percent in three weeks. Nobody is paying them for the insurance. They're being paid for the seat."

"Can we get the seat?"

"Not now. But that's the question to ask next time, before the RSA is signed, and since *ConvergeOne* it's a question the excluded members can ask a judge." Theo turns his printout over and writes three numbers on the back: the discount, the window, and the fraction of the offering reserved for the six funds. "Those three tell you who's being paid for what. The ten percent is the least interesting number on the page."

Ines unfolds her arms. "He's right about the fee and wrong about one thing. Ask who the backstop parties *are*. In Hertz the seat belonged to sponsors who were buying the equity of a solvent company, not creditors defending a claim."

> **View From Another Seat.** *The plan sponsor.* Hertz is the cleanest public example of a seat this chapter otherwise sees only from across the table. The sponsors were not lenders hoping to be made whole; they were buyers. In the company's own announcement, Knighthead, Certares and Apollo-affiliated funds committed "over \$5.9 billion of new equity capital" while Hertz paid "nearly \$19 billion of creditors in full" (Hertz Form 8-K, Exhibit 99.1, 2021). As the offering documents describe the structure, Apollo bought into the equity of a solvent estate rather than defending a claim. That changes the logic of the seat. A creditor in a backstop group is trying to improve the recovery on a claim it already owns. A sponsor has no claim to improve. Its return comes entirely from buying the reorganized equity at the plan's price, adding the backstop fee and the reserved allocation on top, and then controlling the company through the period when the discount closes. Seen from that seat, a low plan value is not a negotiating loss to be minimized; it is the purchase price. A sponsor is long the plan's own discount, with control, which is the most comfortable way to own it. (We describe the logic of the seat generically; the quoted words are the company's, and nothing here is any sponsor's stated view of its own motives.)

Look at what we just did by hand. We audited a share table row by row, evaluated a Black–Scholes put with a pocket calculator, scaled it by a dilution factor, and compared the answer with a fee someone else negotiated. That is fine once, in a chapter. It is not fine on a Tuesday in the commitment window of an offering that closes in three weeks, when the fee, the discount and the funding date are all moving, the share count in the latest 8-K has a different footnote than the last one, and a portfolio manager wants to know whether the backstop is cheap before the rights expire. The desk automates this step because the arithmetic is simple and the ways to get it subtly wrong are many: the wrong share count in $n_0$, a price where a pre-money value belongs, a fee valued at cash when it is paid in stock.

The listing has three parts. First, `bs_call` and `bs_put` are a bare-bones Black–Scholes pair that fall back to intrinsic value when time or volatility is zero, so the same function works on the day the offering funds. Second, `RightsOffering` holds $n_0$, $m$ and $K$ and rejects any that are not positive. It exposes the dilution factor $n_0/(n_0+m)$, the effective share count $N_{\text{eff}}$, the post-money price, the value of the rights attached to one plan share, the backstop's payoff $-N_{\text{eff}}(K-x)^{+}$, and `fair_fee`, which is the put scaled by dilution and divided by $K$, expressed as a fraction of the raise. Third, a block of checks. The Hertz share table must sum exactly to 471.1mm, the fee must equal 10% of the offering in shares, put-call parity must hold, and the dilution factor must land near 0.64. A Monte Carlo of 400,000 driftless lognormal paths must match the closed-form short put within two percent. The fee table must show fee falling as the discount deepens, and the ex post Hertz arithmetic at \$29 must come to about \$1.16bn.

Use it to screen announced backstops against a model fee, and to see how much of a headline fee the discount has already paid. Do not use it to value the whole seat: it prices only the put, not the reserved allocation or the exclusivity option, and it assumes a driftless lognormal over a window that is short precisely because nothing trades on the reorganized equity yet. The edge case that bites is units. The argument `x` is the pre-money value per *plan* share, $E/n_0$, not the quoted post-money price, and `n0` must exclude the fee shares. Feed it the \$29 print as `x0` with a \$10 strike, or add the fee shares to $n_0$, and the fee looks absurdly cheap or absurdly rich with no error to warn you.

## 16.4 Warrants: Long-Dated Calls Handed to People Who Were Told They'd Get Nothing

### 16.4.1 Desk framing: the consolation prize is the most convex thing in the plan

Plans hand out warrants for the same reason game shows hand out consolation prizes: the losing contestant has to leave the stage quietly. The class that is out of the money at the plan value, often old equity and sometimes the unsecured creditors, receives a long-dated call on the reorganized company, struck at or above the plan value, in exchange for not objecting. The plan describes this as a premium to the firm. Moyer agrees, with an important qualifier.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.373:* "Warrants give the holder an economic interest in a firm, but also require the holder to pay cash upon exercise (i.e. the strike price of the warrant) to purchase a share of its stock. Investors presumably are better off because they generally would not exercise warrants unless the stock was higher than the exercise price. On the other hand, where the strike price is above the plan of reorganization's projected value, the firm is effectively, relative to the plan, receiving a premium."
>
> **Annotation.** The load-bearing words are "relative to the plan." A strike set above a plan value that was negotiated *down* (§16.1) may be at or below fair value. The desk therefore never measures warrant moneyness against the disclosure statement; it measures it against the price at which informed insiders are buying stock in the same plan, which in a rights-offering case is printed in the plan itself. Moyer adds on the same page that warrants are freely tradable when issued but generally less liquid than the stock, so a warrant quote is not a stock quote with a strike subtracted. The departure for a 2026 desk is that we can price the warrant properly (dilution, jump-to-default, a long-dated volatility) and use the plan's *own* warrant valuation to back out the volatility the plan's advisors assumed, which is a free read on how the other side of the table modeled the company.

### 16.4.2 Derivation: dilution, the fixed point, and the jump that Kaz gets backwards

**Dilution.** Let $n_0$ be shares outstanding, $n_w$ the number of warrants, each exercisable into one new share at strike $K$ at expiry $T$, and $\omega = n_w/n_0$ the dilution ratio. Let $S_t$ be the share price and $W_t$ the warrant price. The total market value of the equity claims, shares plus warrants, is $n_0 S_t + n_w W_t$. If the warrants are exercised at $T$, the company receives $n_w K$ in cash and has $n_0 + n_w$ shares, so each warrant holder receives
$$\frac{n_0 S_T + n_w W_T + n_w K}{n_0 + n_w} - K = \frac{n_0}{n_0 + n_w}\left(S_T + \omega W_T - K\right) = \frac{1}{1+\omega}\left(\hat S_T - K\right),$$
where $\hat S_t \equiv S_t + \omega W_t$ is the equity value per *existing* share including the warrants' claim. Exercise is rational iff $\hat S_T > K$. If $\hat S$ follows a lognormal with volatility $\sigma$, the warrant is $1/(1+\omega)$ calls on $\hat S$:
$$W = \frac{1}{1+\omega}\,C_{\text{BS}}\!\left(S + \omega W,\;K,\;T,\;\sigma,\;r\right).$$
This is a fixed point in $W$, the classic Galai–Schneller formulation. It is a contraction, since the derivative of the right-hand side in $W$ is $\omega\,\Delta/(1+\omega) < 1$ with $\Delta = \partial C_{\text{BS}}/\partial S \in (0,1)$, so iterating from $W^{(0)} = C_{\text{BS}}(S, K)$ converges. One more bound we will assert in code: because $C_{\text{BS}}$ is convex with slope below one, $C_{\text{BS}}(S + \omega W) \le C_{\text{BS}}(S) + \omega W$, which rearranges to $W \le C_{\text{BS}}(S)$. Dilution never makes a warrant worth more than the equivalent call, so backing out volatility while ignoring dilution always *understates* the volatility.

> **Math, Gently.** *A fixed point.* The warrant formula has $W$ on both sides: to know the warrant's price you need the equity value including the warrants, which needs the warrant's price. That sounds circular, and it is, but it is the useful kind of circular. Guess an answer, plug it in, get a better answer, and repeat. Each pass corrects the previous one by a fraction of its error, because the right-hand side changes by less than one dollar for every dollar you change $W$ (the slope is $\omega\Delta/(1+\omega)$, which is below one). It is like adjusting a shower that responds weakly to the tap: you overshoot a little, then a little less, then the water is right. Tiny number with Hertz's inputs ($S = 10$, $K = 13.80$, $T = 30$, $r = 2\%$, $\omega = 0.19$) at a 52% volatility: the plain call, our first guess, is about \$8.66. Plug it in: the spot becomes $10 + 0.19 \times 8.66 \approx 11.65$, the call on that is about \$10.21, and dividing by $1.19$ gives \$8.58. Plug \$8.58 in and the answer is \$8.56. The first pass moved the price by about 8 cents and the second by about 1 cent, a seventh as much, because the slope here is about $0.19 \times 0.94/1.19 \approx 0.15$; a few more passes change nothing you could trade. Dilution "adds a little": it shaves about a dime, roughly 1%, off this warrant, so recovering a given price needs a slightly higher volatility *(illustrative; computed from the stated inputs)*.

**The jump to zero.** Add a default event, at intensity $\lambda$ per year, at which the stock goes to zero (Chapter 6 notation; the warrant then expires worthless). To keep the stock price itself fairly priced in a nominal pricing measure that earns $r$, the pre-default stock must drift at $r + \lambda$: the survivors are compensated for the jump.

> **Math, Gently.** *Poisson arrivals and the survival curve.* An intensity $\lambda$ is a rate of surprise: an event with intensity 2% a year has a 2% chance of arriving in any given year, a tiny chance in any given week, and no memory of how long it has already waited. Count how many such events arrive in a period and you get a Poisson distribution; measure how long you wait for the first one and you get an exponential distribution. The exponential's defining quirk is that it is *memoryless*: a company that has survived ten years without a second filing faces the same 2% next year as one that emerged yesterday. That is a simplification, and the right one for a first model. The quantity we need is the chance of *no* arrival over the life of the warrant, which is $e^{-\lambda T}$: with $\lambda = 2\%$ and $T = 30$, it is $e^{-0.6} \approx 55\%$. Odds slightly better than a coin flip that a company emerging in 2021 reaches 2051 without another bankruptcy, which is a sobering number to attach to a 30-year option, and it is the number Kaz is about to misuse. Chapter 6 builds hazard rates properly.

The striking result, derived in *The Full Derivation* below, is that holding the observed share price and the diffusive volatility fixed, the warrant priced with the jump is simply the Black–Scholes call at the rate $r + \lambda$, $C^{\text{JD}} = C_{\text{BS}}(S, K, T, \sigma, r + \lambda)$, and so **adding a jump to zero makes the call worth more, not less**: the strike is paid only in survival states, and the stock must outperform while it survives.

Kaz's first draft multiplied the Black–Scholes warrant by $e^{-\lambda T}$. At $\lambda = 2\%$ for 30 years that is $e^{-0.6} \approx 0.55$, a 45% haircut, and Kaz presented it with the confidence of someone who has never been wrong in front of a restructuring banker. Ines put it gently: "The share price already knows the company can die. You charged the warrant holder for the funeral twice." The haircut is correct only if your spot is a *going-concern* value, such as a plan DCF that assumes survival; then the survival factor belongs. If your spot is a market price, it does not.

**What the plan assumed.** Hertz's plan valued the warrants issued to old stockholders at \$769mm (Third Circuit opinion, footnote 5), and the effective-date 8-K reports 89,049,029 warrants with a \$13.80 strike expiring 30 Jun 2051. That is \$8.64 per warrant. The rights offering priced the stock at \$10.00, and the plan valued the old holders' 3% at \$141mm, which is \$10 a share on 14.13mm shares. Taking $S = 10$, $K = 13.80$, $T = 30$, $r \approx 2\%$ *(assumption)* and $\omega = 89.05/471.10 \approx 0.19$, the volatility that reproduces \$8.64 is in the low 50s, both with and without dilution (the code below solves it; dilution adds a little). That is a perfectly reasonable long-run volatility for a levered rental-car company. Had the advisors instead used the \$13.80 implied by the \$6.5bn "warrant basis" as spot, the same \$8.64 would imply a volatility in the mid-20s, which no one who has watched a rental-car equity would sign. The plan's numbers are internally consistent only at the lower spot, which is the honest one. The \$769mm was not a gift; the gift, as the next case shows, was the spot the public market chose four months later.

#### The Full Derivation

**The compound-option view.** In the Merton model of Chapter 6 the share is itself a call on firm value $V_t$ struck at debt face $F$. A plan warrant is therefore a call on a call, and it inherits the leverage-driven skew: as $V_t$ falls toward $F$, equity volatility $\sigma_E$ rises, and an out-of-the-money warrant is mostly a bet on the right tail of $V$. For a 30-year tenor, the warrant price is dominated by two things that are not delta: vega, and the probability that the company does not need a second Chapter 11 before expiry.

*The jump-to-zero expectation.* Writing $\tilde S_T = S\,e^{(r+\lambda-\sigma^2/2)T + \sigma W_T}$ for the pre-default path (here $W_T$ is the Brownian motion, not the warrant):
$$C^{\text{JD}} = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf 1_{\{\tau > T\}}(\tilde S_T - K)^{+}\right] = e^{-rT}e^{-\lambda T}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\tilde S_T - K)^{+}\right] = C_{\text{BS}}(S, K, T, \sigma, r + \lambda),$$
because $e^{-(r+\lambda)T}\mathbb{E}[(\tilde S_T - K)^+]$ is exactly the Black–Scholes call at rate $r + \lambda$. Holding the observed share price and the diffusive volatility fixed, **adding a jump to zero makes the call worth more, not less**: the strike is paid only in survival states, and the stock must outperform while it survives.

### 16.4.3 Case Study: the Hertz warrants, from "worthless" to \$1.35bn of intrinsic value

In June 2020, while selling stock into a retail rally during its bankruptcy, Hertz's prospectus supplement warned of "a significant risk that the [Stockholders] will receive no recovery under the Chapter 11 [c]ases and that our common stock will be worthless" (quoted in the Third Circuit opinion, 10 Sep 2024). Twelve months later the plan sponsor auction turned on a different question: how much old equity would get, not whether. On 8 Nov 2021, selling stockholders priced 44.52mm shares at \$29.00 in a re-IPO, and the stock and warrants began trading on Nasdaq as HTZ and HTZWW the next day (Hertz press release, 8 Nov 2021).

At \$29 a \$13.80-strike warrant had \$15.20 of intrinsic value. Across 89.05mm warrants that is about \$1.35bn, roughly 1.76 times the plan's \$769mm estimate *(derived from sourced counts and prices)*. Averaged over all ≈156mm old shares, the package an old stockholder received was:

| Component | Per old share | Value at the \$29 re-IPO |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cash | \$1.53 | \$1.53 |
| New common (14.13mm ÷ 156mm ≈ 0.091 shares) | 0.091 × \$29 | ≈ \$2.63 |
| Warrants (89.05mm ÷ 156mm ≈ 0.571 warrants) | 0.571 × \$15.20 intrinsic | ≈ \$8.68 |
| **Total** | | **≈ \$12.8** |

*(Approx.; averaged across all old shares, warrants at intrinsic value only, before time value. Holders who elected rights instead of warrants received a different mix.)* The stock that its own prospectus said might be worthless delivered roughly \$12.8 of value per share 4.5 months after emergence. The systematic desk's lesson is not "buy meme stocks in bankruptcy." It is that the warrant's value was almost entirely *timing and volatility*: priced at a plan volatility in the low 50s with a spot the sponsors chose, and then marked at a spot the public market chose. Anyone long the warrant at the plan's mark was long a 30-year option at a reasonable volatility on a spot that was about to be re-rated by a factor of nearly three.

Kaz takes the \$29 print personally. The morning after Ines's funeral line, they rebuild the warrant from scratch with the dilution fixed point and no haircut, put the spot at \$29, keep the plan's 52% volatility and the roughly 29.6 years left to 2051, and get about \$26.70 a warrant, of which \$15.20 is intrinsic and the rest is three decades of time value *(illustrative; computed from the stated inputs)*. Then they ask the question a quant always asks next. "So what volatility is the market implying now? If I can get the warrant quote, I can back it out and see whether the plan's advisors were too low or too high."

"You can," Theo says, "and you'll get a number, and it won't tell you why anybody made money." He pulls the two marks up side by side: \$8.64 per warrant at the plan, roughly \$15.20 of intrinsic value alone at the re-IPO. "Between June and November nobody changed their mind about how volatile a rental-car company is. What changed was the spot. The plan priced the warrant on a \$10 stock. The market priced it on a \$29 stock. The volatility was a reasonable guess both times. The spot was the decision."

"Whose decision?"

"The sponsors'. Ten dollars was the price at which they were buying \$2.8 billion of stock for themselves. Of course it was the conservative number. They weren't estimating the value of the company. They were naming their purchase price, and the warrant was valued off the same number because it had to be." Theo closes the window. "If you want to know whether a plan warrant is cheap, don't start with the vol surface. Start by asking who chose the spot and what they were doing with it that day."

Kaz writes this down, which Theo has learned is the highest compliment Kaz pays.

Mags has been listening from the next desk. "Thirty-year warrants," she says. "In my day we called a thirty-year warrant a weather forecast for 2051. Everybody agreed it was going to be sunny, and nobody was going to be around to check."

The warrants expire on 30 Jun 2051, and the forecast has already been revised once. As §16.6 shows, by October 2026 Hertz was back in the restructuring room, with a stock that had fallen to about \$3 after a June 2026 financing that the press reported sent the shares down about 41% in a day *(per press reports; check against filings)*. At that spot the same machinery, at the plan's 52% volatility, still values each warrant at about \$2 *(illustrative)*, two-thirds of the stock's own price for a call struck more than four times above it. That is not the model saying the warrant is cheap or rich. It is the model saying that, at a 25-year tenor, nearly all of the warrant's value is the right tail plus the question of whether the company needs Chapter 11 again before 2051, which is less a modeling assumption than Mags's weather forecast.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**The Spec You Hand the Robot.** *Vague version:* "Price the Hertz warrants." *What ROBO built:* a Black–Scholes call with the stock's 30-day realized volatility from the meme week of June 2020, which priced a 30-year option at roughly the value of the stock and confidently recommended selling the stock to buy the warrant. *Precise version:* implement `warrant_price` as the dilution fixed point with an optional jump-to-zero intensity entering as $r + \lambda$, raise if the iteration does not converge, and implement `implied_vol_warrant` by bracketing root-finding on $\sigma \in [0.01, 3.0]$. Inputs: `S` (USD per share, the spot you believe, labeled market or going-concern), `K` (USD), `T` (years), `r` (continuously compounded, stated), `omega` = warrants / shares outstanding (dimensionless, $\ge 0$), `lam` (per year, $\ge 0$). Guards: reject negative $\omega$; never apply an $e^{-\lambda T}$ haircut to a market spot.

A warrant whose value appears on both sides of its own pricing equation is the kind of object that a spreadsheet handles with a circular-reference warning and a prayer. Then the question that actually matters, what volatility did the plan's advisors assume, requires running that equation backwards, inside a root-finder, across four variants of the spot and the jump. This is the moment to stop doing it by hand, because the answer is only useful if you can re-run it on the next plan's warrants before the other side has finished its second coffee.

The listing leans on `bs_call` from the previous listing and does two jobs. `warrant_price` starts from the plain Black–Scholes call, feeds the answer back into the spot as $S + \omega W$, divides by $1 + \omega$, and repeats until two passes agree to $10^{-12}$. The contraction argument in the derivation guarantees this works, but the loop still raises an error after a thousand passes rather than returning a half-converged number. The jump intensity enters only by raising the discount rate to $r + \lambda$, which is the correct treatment of a market spot. `implied_vol_warrant` wraps that function in `brentq` over volatilities from 1% to 300%, and refuses to run unless the target price is bracketed.

The checks then replay Hertz. The plan's \$769mm over 89.05mm warrants gives about \$8.64 each, and the fixed point must satisfy its own equation and never exceed the plain call. Implied volatility must be at least as high with dilution as without, the jump variant must come in below the diluted one, and the \$13.80-basis spot must give an implausibly low volatility. Kaz's funeral is encoded as an ordering: jump price above no-jump price above haircut price. The last block reproduces the \$29 intrinsic value and the roughly \$12.8 per old share package.

Use it for any long-dated plan warrant where dilution is more than a few percent of the share count. Skip it for short-dated warrants on a deep-pocketed issuer, where the fixed point and the plain call agree to the cent. The edge case that bites is the bracket. At a 30-year tenor the price has a floor even at near-zero volatility, about \$2.43 in the Hertz setup, and a ceiling at the spot. A target below the floor, such as a quote carrying a heavy illiquidity discount, cannot be matched by any volatility, and the function correctly raises. Treat that error as information about the quote, not as a bug to widen away.

## 16.5 Claims: 503(b)(9) vs. General Unsecured, and the Denominator Problem

### 16.5.1 Desk framing: a claim is a zero-coupon bond with a random face, a random maturity, and a random number of siblings

A trade claim is the least glamorous instrument in this chapter and the one a former RSSG banker understands best. It pays no coupon (Moyer p.413, n.26, notes that trade claims do not accrue interest, which is also why hedging one with a short bond position costs carry), it matures on an effective date nobody can promise, and its recovery is a fraction whose *denominator*, the total pool of allowed claims in the class, is unknown for most of the case. Sellers are usually not investors at all.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.310:* "1. The claim is significantly less liquid and thus purchasers often insist on a discount to compensate for this illiquidity. 2. The claim holder may not have the financial wherewithal to wait until the completion of a bankruptcy case to realize full value of the claim and thus is a forced seller."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer lists five reasons a trade creditor sells below the trading level of similarly ranked bonds; the first two are illiquidity and the forced seller, and the others (preferring cash to stock, being a business rather than an investor, unfamiliarity with the process) are variations on the same theme. Notice that this is §16.2's overhang again, one level down the capital structure: the vendor who shipped goods on credit is the CLO of the claims market, constrained not by an indenture but by payroll. The modern desk departs from Moyer in two ways. First, it prices the discount as a sum of separable premia (allowance, timing, pool size, illiquidity) rather than as a single haircut. Second, the claims market now includes customer claims in crypto and fintech bankruptcies, where the seller is a retail depositor and the forced-selling motive is acute (Trend Watch below).

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.311:* "This "nonallowance" risk can be managed through a variety of mechanisms. The first is to only purchase claims that have been deemed allowed by the bankruptcy court or at least have been admitted by the debtor."
>
> **Annotation.** The parameter $p_A$ in the pricing formula below. Moyer immediately notes the cost of this safety: claims are often allowed late, sometimes after confirmation, so buying only allowed claims means buying after most of the discount has gone. The desk's version is a classifier: given the claim's documentation, its match to the debtor's schedules, its type, and the objection history of similar claims in the same case, estimate $p_A$. Transfers are noticed on the docket under Bankruptcy Rule 3001(e) (Moyer p.311), which makes the claims register and the transfer notices a public, machine-readable record of who is buying what.

Moyer also describes (p.269) the classic hedge: a trade claim and a senior unsecured bond of the same debtor are *pari passu*, so long claim / short bond isolates the claim's discount while neutralizing the enterprise-value risk they share. The desk keeps that trade and adds a sharper one, the 503(b)(9) pair.

### 16.5.2 The statute block: what decides the face value

The legal rules below are the entire source of the pool-size randomness. Each one is a truncation, a priority switch, or an estimation procedure. The easiest way to see them is to follow two creditors through the building.

Picture two vendors to a fictional home-goods retailer that has just filed for Chapter 11, each owed \$2mm for goods they sold it on credit, each sitting in the hallway outside the claims agent's office with the same proof-of-claim form and the same question for Theo: *what do I actually own?* *(The retailer and vendors are fictional; the fact pattern is Exercise 5's.)* Vendor A imports ceramics. Its container left the port of origin 25 days before the petition, free on board at the shipping point, so title and risk of loss passed to the retailer the moment the goods were loaded; the ship docked and the goods were unloaded at the retailer's warehouse 18 days before the petition. Vendor B makes corrugated boxes an hour away, and its truck backed up to the same warehouse and was unloaded 22 days before the petition.

Theo starts them at the loading dock, because that is where the statute starts. **§503(b)(9)**, added by the 2005 bankruptcy amendments after Moyer was written, grants administrative-expense priority to "the value of any goods received by the debtor within 20 days before the date of commencement of a case under this title in which the goods have been sold to the debtor in the ordinary course of such debtor's business" (11 U.S.C. §503, Cornell LII). Everything turns on the word *received*. Vendor A's instinct is that its goods were "received" when the retailer took title at the foreign port, 25 days out, which would put them outside the window. Vendor B assumes a truck at the dock 22 days out is obviously "received" and obviously too early. Theo's answer is that the Third Circuit settled what the word means: in *In re World Imports, Ltd.*, 862 F.3d 338 (3d Cir. 2017), goods are received when the debtor or its agent takes physical possession, and delivery to a common carrier with risk of loss passing at the shipping point does not count. Bankruptcy courts in Delaware have since denied 503(b)(9) status to drop-shipped goods (*In re SRC Liquidation*). The vendor whose goods left the port 25 days out and the vendor whose truck was unloaded 22 days out hold different instruments, and the difference is decided at the dock, not at the port. Which of today's two vendors holds the priority claim is Exercise 5; the point for now is that the answer depends on a delivery receipt, not on a contract.

The reason the dock matters so much is one corridor over. **§1129(a)(9)(A)** requires administrative claims, including 503(b)(9) claims, to be paid in full in cash on the effective date unless the holder agrees otherwise. That is what makes a 503(b)(9) claim a near-par instrument. The vendor whose goods were physically received inside the window is holding something close to cash with a date attached. The other vendor is holding a general unsecured claim, and Theo walks that one down the hall to the room where the denominator lives.

The first thing that vendor learns there is what it does *not* get. **§502(b)(2)** disallows claims "for unmatured interest": no post-petition coupon accrues on a general unsecured claim, however long the case takes, with the solvent-debtor exception that §16.6 shows Hertz paying for. The second thing it learns is who its siblings are, because its recovery is a share of a pool, and the pool is still being written.

At the landlord's door, the pool is capped. When the retailer rejects its store leases, the landlords file damages claims, and **§502(b)(6)** caps a landlord's lease-rejection damages at "the rent reserved by such lease, without acceleration, for the greater of one year, or 15 percent, not to exceed three years, of the remaining term," plus unpaid rent (11 U.S.C. §502, Cornell LII). A landlord with fifteen years left on a lease does not get fifteen years of rent into the pool; it gets the greater of one year and 15% of the remaining term (about 2.25 years here), never more than three. Down the corridor in human resources, **§502(b)(7)** does the same thing to employment contracts, capping termination claims at one year's compensation plus unpaid compensation. Every rejected lease and terminated contract is a sibling that the statute has trimmed before it joins the family.

And in front of the judge, the pool is estimated. Some claims cannot be counted at all yet: a product-liability suit with no verdict, a guarantee that may never be called, a tax audit. **§502(c)** requires estimation of contingent or unliquidated claims whose liquidation would unduly delay the case, so the court puts a number on them for voting or reserves and the case moves on. By the time Theo has walked both vendors through the building, they have seen every source of randomness in the denominator: a truncation at the dock, a priority switch in the corridor, caps at the landlord's door and in human resources, and an estimate from the bench.

Kaz is delighted by §502(c). "The statute requires estimation! Which estimator? Maximum likelihood? Bayesian with a conjugate prior?" Theo explains that the estimator is a bankruptcy judge. Kaz writes this down as "an M-estimator with a robe," which is not wrong: a §502(c) estimate is a court's point estimate for the purpose of voting or reserves, not the mean of anything, and the desk should treat it as one more noisy observation of the pool, not as the pool.

### 16.5.3 Derivation: the random denominator

**Setup.** Let $\mathcal{V}$ be the value distributable to the class and $\mathcal{C}$ the total allowed claims in the class. A claim's recovery per dollar of allowed face is
$$R = \min\!\left(1, \frac{\mathcal{V}}{\mathcal{C}}\right).$$
Let $Y \equiv \ln(\mathcal{V}/\mathcal{C})$ and assume $Y \sim N(\mu_Y, s_Y^2)$ under $\mathbb{P}$, which holds if $\mathcal{V}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ are jointly lognormal; their correlation enters through $s_Y^2 = s_{\mathcal{V}}^2 + s_{\mathcal{C}}^2 - 2\varrho\, s_{\mathcal{V}} s_{\mathcal{C}}$.

> **Math, Gently.** *A lognormal pool.* Why should the claims pool be lognormal? Think about how it gets built. The final pool is the filed amount, times the fraction that survives objections, times the fraction left after the lease caps, times whatever the judge's estimate does to the contingent claims, times the effect of the next batch of rejected contracts. Each of those is a positive number, and they *multiply*. When you multiply many independent positive uncertainties, their logarithms *add*, and a sum of many small independent pieces is close to normal; so the log of the pool is roughly normal, which is what "lognormal" means. The shape has two features worth remembering. It cannot go below zero, which is right for a pool of claims. And it leans to the right: a few cases blow up (a mass-tort estimate, a surprise wave of lease rejections) while none can shrink below nothing, so the mean sits above the median. In logs the gap is exact: the log of the mean exceeds the log of the median by half the log-variance, $s^2/2$. Tiny number: with $s = 0.30$, the mean pool is about $e^{0.045} \approx 1.046$ times the median pool, so a "typical" case comes in roughly 4% below the average one. That half-variance gap is a preview of §16.8.1, where it decides what a post-reorg stock's average return means.

Split the expectation at $Y = 0$:
$$\mathbb{E}[R] = \Pr(Y \ge 0) + \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{Y}\mathbf 1_{\{Y<0\}}\right].$$
The first term is $\Phi(\mu_Y/s_Y)$, the chance the class is fully covered. The second is the average of the ratio over the states in which it is not, a partial expectation of a lognormal that one standard trick (completing the square, in *The Full Derivation* after Figure 16.3) turns into a normal probability times a constant. Putting the pieces together:
$$\mathbb{E}[R] = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\mu_Y}{s_Y}\right) + e^{\mu_Y + s_Y^2/2}\,\Phi\!\left(-\frac{\mu_Y}{s_Y} - s_Y\right).$$
This is Black–Scholes bookkeeping with $\mathcal{V}/\mathcal{C}$ in the role of $S/K$: a claim is a long position in the ratio with a short call at 1 on top (the cap at par). Boundary checks: as $s_Y \to 0$ the formula collapses to $\min(1, e^{\mu_Y})$, and as $\mu_Y \to +\infty$ it tends to 1.

**The special case that matters most.** Fix $\mathcal{V}$ and let the pool be lognormal with mean $m_{\mathcal{C}}$ and log-standard deviation $s$, so $\ln\mathcal{C} \sim N(\ln m_{\mathcal{C}} - s^2/2,\, s^2)$ and $\mu_Y = \ln(\mathcal{V}/m_{\mathcal{C}}) + s^2/2$. With $a \equiv \mu_Y/s$:
$$\mathbb{E}[R] = \Phi(a) + \frac{\mathcal{V}}{m_{\mathcal{C}}}\,e^{s^2}\,\Phi(-a - s).$$
At $\mathcal{V} = m_{\mathcal{C}}$ (the class is covered *on average*) and $s = 0.30$: $a = 0.15$ and $\mathbb{E}[R] \approx 0.5596 + 1.0942 \times 0.3264 \approx 0.917$ *(illustrative)*. More than eight points of recovery disappear purely because the denominator is uncertain, even though the expected pool exactly equals the value available. That is the cap at work: when the pool comes in small you cannot recover more than par, and when it comes in large you lose.

**Jensen cuts both ways.** Now take a deeply impaired class, $\mathcal{V}/m_{\mathcal{C}} = 0.30$, with the same $s$. The cap almost never binds, and the reciprocal $1/\mathcal{C}$ is *convex*, so variance helps: the formula gives $\mathbb{E}[R] \approx 0.328 > 0.30$. Near par, pool uncertainty is a cost; deep out of the money, it is a (small) gift. The desk must know which side of the kink a class sits on before it decides whether it wants to own the uncertainty or sell it. Figure 16.3 draws the kink.

> **Math, Gently.** *Jensen's inequality, both ways.* Jensen's inequality says that for a curved function, the average of the function is not the function of the average, and the direction of the error depends on which way the curve bends. Here is the whole idea in two coin flips. Suppose the claims pool will be either 80 or 120 with equal odds, so its average is 100. If the value available to the class is 30, the recovery is either $30/80 = 0.375$ or $30/120 = 0.25$, which averages to 0.3125, *more* than the 0.30 you would get from the average pool. That is because $1/\mathcal{C}$ bends upward: a small pool helps you more than a large pool hurts. Now make the value 100. The recovery is either $100/80 = 1.25$, which the cap at par cuts to 1.0, or $100/120 = 0.833$, averaging 0.917, *less* than the 1.0 you would get from the average pool. The cap is a bend in the other direction: the good state is clipped and the bad state is not. Same coin, same pool, opposite answers, and which answer you get depends only on where the class sits relative to par. That is why the closed form above gives 0.328 for the impaired class and 0.917 for the covered one.

Illustrative: the closed form of §16.5.3 with a lognormal pool, s = 0.30 and s = 0.60.](assets/figures/ch16_claim_recovery_pool_risk.png)

#### The Full Derivation

*The partial expectation.* For the second term of $\mathbb{E}[R]$, complete the square in the exponent of the normal density:
$$y - \frac{(y - \mu_Y)^2}{2 s_Y^2} = -\frac{(y - \mu_Y - s_Y^2)^2}{2 s_Y^2} + \mu_Y + \frac{s_Y^2}{2},$$
so the exponential tilts the normal's mean by $s_Y^2$, and
$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{Y}\mathbf 1_{\{Y<0\}}\right] = e^{\mu_Y + s_Y^2/2}\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{0 - \mu_Y - s_Y^2}{s_Y}\right).$$
Adding $\Phi(\mu_Y/s_Y)$ gives the closed form for $\mathbb{E}[R]$ in the main text. The special case follows by substituting $\mu_Y = \ln(\mathcal{V}/m_{\mathcal{C}}) + s^2/2$, so that $e^{\mu_Y + s^2/2} = (\mathcal{V}/m_{\mathcal{C}})\,e^{s^2}$.

**Pricing the claim.** With allowance probability $p_A$, an effective date $T$ that arrives at hazard rate $h$ per year (so $T$ is exponential with mean $1/h$ and independent of $R$), a discount rate $r$ and an illiquidity premium $\psi$, the expected discount factor is
$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-(r+\psi)T}\right] = \int_0^\infty h\,e^{-ht}\,e^{-(r+\psi)t}\,dt = \frac{h}{h + r + \psi},$$
and the price per dollar of face of a general unsecured claim is
$$\pi^{\text{GUC}} = p_A\,\frac{h}{h + r + \psi}\,\mathbb{E}[R] \;+\; \underbrace{p_A\,\Pr(\text{solvent})\,\mathbb{E}[\text{post-petition interest} \mid \text{solvent}]}_{\text{the Hertz option, usually} \;\approx\; 0}.$$
No coupon appears anywhere, because §502(b)(2) bars one. A **503(b)(9) claim** is the same bond with the face problem removed: it is paid in full in cash on the effective date unless the estate is administratively insolvent (probability $p_{AI}$, loss given that event $L_{AI}$):
$$\pi^{(9)} = p_A^{(9)}\,\frac{h}{h + r + \psi}\,\left(1 - p_{AI}L_{AI}\right), \qquad p_A^{(9)} = \Pr(\text{goods, ordinary course, physical receipt within 20 days}).$$

> **Math, Gently.** *Hazard rates and the cost of waiting.* The effective date is modeled the way Chapter 6 models default: not as a date but as an event that arrives at a rate. A hazard rate $h = 1.5$ per year means that in any short stretch of time there is a steady chance the plan goes effective, and the waiting time is exponential with an average of $1/h$, here $1/1.5 \approx 0.67$ years, about eight months. The factor $h/(h + r + \psi)$ is what that wait costs. With $r = 4\%$ and an illiquidity premium $\psi = 8\%$, it is $1.5/(1.5 + 0.12) = 1.5/1.62 \approx 0.93$: a claim certain to be paid par on the effective date is worth about 93 cents today, purely because nobody knows when the effective date will come and money tied up in a claim earns nothing while it waits. A case that drags (a lower $h$) costs more; at $h = 0.75$, a sixteen-month average wait, the factor falls to about 0.86. Notice that the formula is not $e^{-(r+\psi)/h}$, the discount for waiting exactly the average time; averaging the discount over all the possible waits gives a slightly higher number, which is Jensen again.

**The pair.** Because both claims share the same timing factor, the log price spread cancels it exactly:
$$\ln \pi^{(9)} - \ln \pi^{\text{GUC}} = \ln\frac{p_A^{(9)}}{p_A} + \ln\!\left(1 - p_{AI}L_{AI}\right) - \ln \mathbb{E}[R].$$
Duration is held fixed and what remains is allowance risk, administrative-insolvency risk, and pool risk. For a near-par class, where more pool uncertainty lowers $\mathbb{E}[R]$, *long 503(b)(9) / short GUC is long pool uncertainty*: it pays if the denominator surprises the market by coming in large or ragged. The reverse trade, long the GUC and short the 503(b)(9), is the trade for the person who has *shrunk* $s$ by doing the work: counting rejected leases and applying the §502(b)(6) cap lease by lease, matching filed claims to the schedules, striking duplicates and superseded amendments, sizing WARN Act and litigation claims. That person is the former RSSG associate. This is the chapter's cleanest example of being paid for a variance everyone else is afraid of.

#### When the pair works, and when it breaks

The pair is at its best in a specific kind of case: a retailer or distributor with a large trade-creditor body, where both 503(b)(9) and general unsecured claims change hands in the same case, the claims agent posts a register that can be downloaded and parsed, and transfer notices show who is buying. Then both legs have prices, the timing factor really does cancel, and the residual really is allowance, administrative solvency and pool risk.

It breaks in three ways, each of which has cost someone money. It breaks when the estate is administratively insolvent, or close to it. The 503(b)(9) leg is "near par" only because §1129(a)(9)(A) says administrative claims must be paid in full; if the estate cannot pay them, the near-par leg becomes a second impaired claim, $p_{AI}L_{AI}$ stops being a rounding error, and the pair turns into two correlated bets on the same shortfall. It breaks when the claims are dollarized in a volatile asset, as in the crypto cases, because then the denomination term moves both legs by more than the pool term moves the spread, and the trade you thought was about the denominator is really about bitcoin. And it breaks when the register is not machine-readable, or not posted at all, because a pair whose edge is the work of shrinking $s$ has no edge if the work cannot be done faster than the market can do it.

The first thing a practitioner checks is not the model's output but the calendar: *has the bar date passed?* Before the deadline for filing proofs of claim, the pool is still being written; anyone can file, and the right tail of $\mathcal{C}$ is wide open. After it, the pool is a finite list of documents that can be read, matched and objected to, and $s$ starts to fall as fast as someone is willing to do the reading. The pair is a bet on that reading. Placing it before the bar date is a bet on the reading of claims that do not exist yet.

**Denomination risk.** In customer bankruptcies the claim is often fixed in petition-date dollars. If a claim on an asset worth $A_{t_0}$ at the petition date recovers $R$ in cash at $T$, the recovery *in kind*, in units of the asset the customer actually had, is
$$R^{\text{in-kind}} = R\,\frac{A_{t_0}}{A_T}.$$
A 120% cash recovery on a bitcoin claim dollarized at about \$17k on 11 Nov 2022 is roughly a 20% in-kind recovery if bitcoin trades at \$100k on the distribution date *(illustrative)*. The claimant is short a call on the asset, struck at the petition-date price, and was paid nothing for it.

> **Trend Watch.** *Claims trading went crypto, 2023–2026.*
> - **Price path.** Some lower-ranked FTX claims traded at roughly 12 to 15% of face at the start of 2023 and about 35% by September 2023, according to Claims Market pricing (Bloomberg, 21 Sep 2023; Decrypt, 22 Sep 2023), which also reported more than \$250mm of claims bought by Silver Point Capital, Diameter Capital Partners and Attestor Capital, a \$23mm claim bought by Hudson Bay, and possibly more than \$100mm of trades not yet reported because of transfer-paperwork lags.
> - **Distributions.** Under the confirmed plan, convenience-class claims (Class 7) received 100% of petition-date value plus 9% annual post-petition interest from 11 Nov 2022, about 120% cumulative; distributions to larger customer classes began on 30 May 2025 with more than \$5bn, followed by about \$1.6bn in September 2025 and about \$2.2bn on 31 Mar 2026, bringing the dotcom customer class (5A) to about 96% and the US customer class (5B) to about 100% cumulative; a fifth distribution of about \$900mm, announced for 31 Jul 2026, takes both classes to about 105% *(per press reports; verify against FTX Recovery Trust notices)*.
> - **Scale then and now.** In the Lehman era, trade-press estimates put monthly claims trading at roughly \$2.2–3.6bn *(approx.; 2011 Reuters report on SecondMarket data)*. FTX was the Lehman of this cycle: a right-tail buyer who read the dollarization clause correctly went from about 15 cents to more than 100 cents in cash in roughly two years, roughly seven to nine times the money.
> - **What it means for the desk.** Claims are now a data problem as much as a legal one: public claims registers, Rule 3001(e) transfer notices, distribution notices and docketed objections are machine-readable. The pricing edge is in $p_A$ and $s$, and the risk the market most often misprices is denomination.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The claims buyer at a distressed fund.* The names in that Trend Watch are a reminder that the other side of a retail claimant's sale is usually a professional. Bloomberg reported Silver Point Capital, Diameter Capital Partners and Attestor Capital among the buyers of more than \$250mm of FTX claims by September 2023 (Bloomberg, 21 Sep 2023); we know nothing about their reasoning beyond the fact of the purchases, so describe the seat generically. A distressed fund's claims desk buys what a systematic desk models: allowance risk, pool risk and timing, bought from sellers who need cash now. Its edge is work, the same work that shrinks $s$, and its constraint is information. Moyer's account of the restricted-list calendar (pp.62–64) explains the constraint. A fund that joins a creditor committee to influence the case signs a confidentiality agreement, "signing 'confi'" in Moyer's words, and the holder becomes "restricted" until the information it received becomes public or stale, typically via an 8-K or a 10-Q. While restricted, it can still trade the debtor's other paper only through a "big-boy letter," an acknowledgment and waiver arranged through a broker-dealer in which the counterparty accepts that the fund may know more. The view from that seat is that the claim and the bond are one position managed through a wall. The view from the systematic desk is a pattern in the data: when the largest holders of a name go restricted, its bonds and claims go quiet, transfer notices slow, and the price stops carrying their information. A quiet name in the middle of a case is not necessarily a boring name. It may be a name in which the people who know the most have agreed, for a while, to say nothing.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**The Spec You Hand the Robot.** *Vague version:* "Value the claims in this case." *What ROBO built:* filed amount times the plan's estimated recovery percentage, summed over the register, which counted every amended claim alongside the claim it superseded, valued a \$40mm landlord claim at its full remaining rent, and produced an estimated recovery higher than the debtor's total assets. *Precise version:* implement `ClaimsWaterfallPricer` with closed-form $\mathbb{E}[R]$ under lognormal $Y$, the fixed-value special case, a Monte Carlo check, per-claim pricing for `GUC` and `503b9` kinds, the priority-spread decomposition, and the in-kind recovery transform.

*Claims-register schema (one row per proof of claim, from the claims agent's public register export and the docket):* `claim_id`, `debtor_entity`, `claimant_type` in {`trade`, `landlord`, `employee`, `customer`, `litigation`, `tax`, `bond_trustee`, `other`}, `filed_amount` (USD), `scheduled_amount` (USD or null), `asserted_priority` in {`secured`, `admin`, `503b9`, `priority`, `GUC`, `convenience`}, `status` in {`filed`, `objected`, `allowed`, `disallowed`, `withdrawn`, `superseded`}, `amends_claim_id` (nullable), `filed_date`, `receipt_date_evidence` (for 503(b)(9): delivery date and mode), `denomination` (USD or asset ticker) and `petition_price` (USD per unit), `transfer_notice_dates` (list, Rule 3001(e)). *Guards:* drop superseded and withdrawn claims before summing; apply §502(b)(6) and (b)(7) caps per claim; reject $s_Y \le 0$; clip convenience-class elections at the class cap before computing the pool.

Picture the call: a vendor's controller, owed a few million dollars for goods shipped before the petition, would like a bid by noon, and she would prefer not to hear the word "estimate." Theo can recite the statute block from memory. What he cannot do in the time available is turn a lognormal pool, an allowance probability, an uncertain effective date and an illiquidity premium into a single price, and then show that his 503(b)(9) bid and his general unsecured bid differ for the reasons he says they do. The closed form of §16.5.3 is short, but it has an exponential and two normal CDFs in it, and the version that gets typed into a cell at noon is usually the one with the minus sign in the wrong place. So the desk keeps one function, tested to the fourth decimal, and prices every claim through it.

The listing is built from small pieces on purpose. `expected_recovery_lognormal` is the closed form for $\mathbb{E}[\min(1, e^{Y})]$ and raises if the log-standard deviation is not positive. `expected_recovery_fixed_value` takes the question as a desk actually poses it, fixed distributable value $V$ against a pool whose *mean* is $m_{\mathcal{C}}$, and converts it by setting $\mu_Y = \ln(V/m_{\mathcal{C}}) + s^2/2$. `ClaimSpec` bundles the inputs of one claim: kind, allowance probability, exit hazard, discount rate, illiquidity premium, pool parameters, and the administrative-insolvency pair for 503(b)(9). `timing_factor` is $h/(h + r + \psi)$. `price_claim` multiplies allowance, timing and recovery, with the recovery term for a 503(b)(9) claim replaced by one minus the insolvency loss. `in_kind_recovery` rescales a cash recovery by the petition price over the distribution price.

The checks mirror the derivation: a million-draw Monte Carlo against the closed form, the 0.917 near-par figure, deep impairment recovering more than its 30% of value, the small-$s$ limit returning $\min(1, V/m_{\mathcal{C}})$, and the log spread between the pair decomposing exactly into allowance, insolvency and pool terms. A wider pool distribution must widen that spread. The in-kind example turns 120% in cash into 20.4%.

Use it to bid on claims in classes whose pool you can bound, and to size the 503(b)(9)-versus-GUC pair. Do not use it to replace the work of shrinking $s$: the model is only as good as your pool estimate. The edge case that bites is the omitted solvency option. `price_claim` carries no post-petition interest, as its docstring warns, so on a solvent debtor, where §502(b)(2) yields to the Hertz-style exception, it prices the general unsecured claim too low.

## 16.6 Case Study: Hertz 2020–2026, the Whole Circuit

Hertz is the only case we know of that runs every instrument in this chapter through one company: equity trading during bankruptcy, a competitive plan auction, a rights offering with a backstop, 30-year warrants, a re-IPO, a solvent-debtor fight over post-petition interest, and then, five years later, a return to the liability-management zone. Read the docket as a sequence of option exercises. Figure 16.4 puts the dates on one axis.

> - **Late May to mid-June 2020.** Old Hertz stock, already in bankruptcy, rallies on retail flows. Public Robinhood-holder trackers showed the holder count roughly tripling between the filing and 11 June *(approx.; Robintrack data as reported in the press)*.
> - **12 Jun 2020.** The bankruptcy court authorizes an at-the-market program to sell up to \$1bn of new common stock. The prospectus warns that the stock may be "worthless." After the SEC raises questions, Hertz suspends the program, having sold about \$29mm of stock *(approx.; per press reports)*.

The Third Circuit's opening paragraph is the cleanest statement of the absolute priority rule a systematic desk will ever read: "The debtors here, able to pay their creditors in full, believe they have the leverage to deny their unsecured noteholders more than a quarter billion dollars of interest they promised to pay pre-bankruptcy, all while giving lower priority equityholders four times that amount" (*In re The Hertz Corp.*, 3d Cir., 10 Sep 2024). Footnote 5 of the same opinion gives us the plan's own marks for what equity received: about \$240mm of cash, a 3% stake valued at \$141mm, and warrants valued at \$769mm, which is the roughly \$1.1bn the court describes the stockholders receiving.

**How a systematic desk would have traded each leg.**

Late on a Thursday, with the screens quiet, Ines prints the Docket Diary, cuts it into six strips with a pair of scissors she keeps for exactly this purpose, and deals them face down across the desk. "Six streets," she says. "One hand. Hertz played every one of them. We're going to play them back and decide, street by street, what a desk like ours would have done with what was public at the time. No hindsight. If you use hindsight I take your chips."

She turns over the first strip. *June 2020.*

**Street one: June 2020, equity in bankruptcy.** Theo goes first, because it is his kind of question. "Easy. Old equity in a Chapter 11 case is a deep out-of-the-money call on enterprise value struck at *all* claims, including post-petition interest. The useful number is the enterprise value implied by the equity market cap plus every claim ahead of it, compared with any plausible range for the business. In June 2020 that comparison said sell, and the debtor itself tried to sell. I'd have been short."

"You'd have been short the right thing at the wrong strike," Mags says. "You left out the meter." She takes the strip and writes on the back of it. "The strike isn't all the claims. It's all the claims *plus* the administrative claims and priority claims that get paid ahead of everyone, *plus* the professional fees that run every month until the effective date. In a case Hertz's size that fee meter runs to nine figures by the time anyone gets out *(Hertz's 10-K reports professional fees and other bankruptcy-related costs of roughly \$175mm for 2020 and \$257mm for 2021; treat these as approximate, since the line also holds non-fee items)*. Every month the case drags, the strike goes up. That's the theta of bankruptcy equity. Admin burn. You're short a call whose strike rises every time a lawyer opens a file."

Theo takes the correction, because it is correct, and because it makes his short look *better*. "So the call was even further out of the money than I said."

"And it still finished in the money," Ines says, turning the strip over to the part everyone knows. The call finished in the money, helped by the recovery in travel and the 2021 surge in used-car prices, which raised the value of the fleet. "Your analysis was right and the trade would have hurt. Why?"

"Because the main asset was a commodity in a supply shock," Theo says slowly. "A rental-car company is a fleet of used cars with a reservations system attached. When nobody could buy a new car, the old ones went up in price, and the fleet was suddenly worth more than any plausible range I'd have used in June."

"So the lesson isn't 'don't short bankrupt equity,'" Mags says. "It's humility about deep out-of-the-money calls on a company whose main asset is a commodity in a supply shock. And discipline about borrow cost and locates if you are short them, because a meme rally is when the borrow disappears." She hands the strip back. "Back in my day you couldn't short these at all. You just complained about them at lunch."

**Street two: May 2021, the auction.** The second strip is the plan sponsor auction, and Kaz takes it, because it has two options in it. "Two competing bids," Kaz says, "is a free market test of the equity. We can price each bid's package for the old holders with the warrant and rights machinery from §16.3 and §16.4 and see which one the equity should prefer, and by how much the auction moved old-equity value."

"And what does the paperwork tell you that the prices don't?" Ines asks.

Kaz reads. "The warrant strike was set off a \$6.5bn equity value. The rights were priced off about \$4.7bn. That's a 38% gap. The plan calls the higher one a premium."

"It was only a premium relative to the sponsors' own lowball," Theo says. "That's what we showed in §16.4. When the people buying stock at \$4.7bn tell you the warrants are struck at a premium, ask them whether they'd buy at \$6.5bn."

**Street three: June 2021, the rights and the backstop.** Ines barely turns this one over; the desk has already played it. A short-dated put sold for a 10% fee paid in shares, plus a 22% unsubscribed block created by the plan's own warrant-or-rights election (§16.3.4). "Next."

**Street four: July to November 2021, emergence to re-IPO.** "This is where you'd expect the overhang model," Theo says, "and it's the wrong model." Hertz is an unusual post-reorg equity because its unsecured creditors were paid in cash, so there was little CLO-driven overhang. "The supply wasn't a forced seller. It was the sponsors' and the backstop parties' wish to monetize, released on a schedule through an underwritten secondary."

"So $Q_0$ was large," Kaz says, "but its release date was known."

"And the company absorbed part of the release itself," Theo adds: the buyback of \$300mm at the offering price. "Same equation, different seller. A sponsor isn't forced to sell, but it does want to, and it tells you when."

**Street five: 2021 to 2024, the make-whole fight.** Ines turns the fifth strip, and Kaz reads it with the expression of someone who has found a term they recognize in a document they do not. "This is the 'Hertz option' in the GUC pricing formula of §16.5.3. Post-petition interest that exists only in the solvent branch. Usually it's worth approximately zero and we drop it."

"In Hertz it was worth more than \$270mm," Mags says, "and the noteholders were paid for having written the option into their indentures in the first place. Which, by the way, is the part your formula can't see. Whether the option exists depends on the indenture's words and on which courthouse you're in."

That last sentence deserves a table, because it is the kind of thing a systematic desk is tempted to encode as a constant. It is not a constant. The appellate record on make-whole premiums in bankruptcy splits on two separate questions: whether the indenture's own wording makes the premium payable when the debt is repaid or accelerated in a bankruptcy, and whether the Bankruptcy Code then disallows it as unmatured interest. A rough map of the four decisions the desk cites most often:

| Case | Court and date | What the court decided (summary) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| *Energy Future Holdings* | 3d Cir., 2016 | Make-whole payable: the post-petition refinancing was read as an optional redemption under the indenture's terms |
| *Momentive (MPM Silicones)* | 2d Cir., 2017 | Make-whole not payable: repayment after automatic acceleration was not an optional redemption, and the acceleration clause did not provide for the premium |
| *Ultra Petroleum* | 5th Cir., 2022 | Make-whole is the economic equivalent of unmatured interest and disallowed under §502(b)(2) in the ordinary case, but a solvent debtor must pay it to unimpaired creditors |
| *Hertz* | 3d Cir., 10 Sep 2024 | Same structure as *Ultra*: disallowed in the ordinary case, owed by a solvent debtor to unimpaired creditors at the contract rate; more than \$270mm at stake |

*(Summaries for orientation; read the opinions before relying on any of them. The honest one-line rule is that the outcome depends on indenture wording and forum.)* For the desk the table turns the "Hertz option" from a footnote into a feature with three inputs: whether the estate is solvent, what the indenture says about acceleration and redemption, and which circuit the case sits in. In most cases the first input is zero and the rest does not matter. In the cases where it is not zero, the rest is worth a quarter of a billion dollars.

**Street six: 2026, round two.** The last strip is the one nobody wants. "At a \$3 stock," Kaz says, "the \$13.80-strike warrants expiring in 2051 are a 25-year call roughly 4.6 times out of the money. That's a pure bet on volatility and survival." Kaz has already run it: at the plan's 52% volatility the dilution fixed point still says about \$2 a warrant *(illustrative)*, which, as §16.4.3 noted, is mostly the right tail and the question of a second Chapter 11.

Theo reads the rest. The new exchangeable first-lien PIK notes rank ahead of existing unsecured debt, and the creditor groups that organized in October 2026 put Hertz squarely in the territory of the Priming Risk Score of Chapter 13. Then he stops, because the next line is about him. Theo, who did not work on Hertz at PJT, notes that his former colleagues are now on the other side of a trade his current desk might want to make, and that the wall between the two is exactly as thick as the compliance manual says.

Mags reads the whole Docket Diary twice and puts down her coffee. "Back in my day a company went through Chapter 11 once and spent the rest of its life embarrassed about it. These kids treat it like a pit stop." She pauses. "I'll grant you the pit crew is better."

Ines gathers the six strips into a stack. "So here's the hand as it stands tonight." It is October 2026. Bloomberg Law has reported (16 Oct 2026) that Hertz's revolver lenders have hired Milbank, that a secured creditor group has hired Evercore and Gibson Dunn, and that Hertz is working with PJT Partners to extend its runway. Adviser hires and creditor-group formation are among the earliest public signals that a liability-management negotiation has begun, and a company hiring an adviser to extend its runway is usually telling you it intends to avoid a filing, not prepare for one. The stock, at the last print we can source (the June drop), is about \$3. The warrants are a 25-year call struck at \$13.80.

"Two questions," Ines says. "First: what does this desk do with a \$13.80 warrant on a \$3 stock, with a priming negotiation starting and the unsecured notes about to find out where they rank? You can buy it as cheap convexity, sell it as an overpriced lottery ticket, hedge it against the stock, or leave it alone. Pick one and tell me what you'd need to see to change your mind."

"And the second?"

"The second is you." She looks at Theo. "Your old firm is advising the company. You didn't work on Hertz. You know nobody on the deal team. Does that matter, and who decides?"

Theo knows the answer to the second question before he says it: not him. It goes to compliance first, as a memo, before anyone on the desk trades the name. The first question he takes home.

## 16.7 Case Study: Spirit Airlines, the Five-Month Zero

If Hertz shows how far the right tail of post-reorg equity can run, Spirit Airlines shows how fast the left tail arrives. Per press reports and the company's announcements, Spirit filed for Chapter 11 on 18 Nov 2024 in the Southern District of New York, emerged on 12 Mar 2025 after about four months, having converted roughly \$795mm of debt into equity, with old shareholders wiped out and the new equity issued to noteholders. On 29 Aug 2025, less than six months after emergence, it filed again, listing roughly \$8.1bn of debts against \$8.6bn of assets, after aircraft-lease terminations and card-processor holdbacks squeezed its liquidity; the company said its new shares would likely be cancelled and worthless *(dates and figures per press reports; verify on the docket)*.

Those are the facts. What follows is what they felt like from inside a book that owned the stock. *(The desk's position, its sizes, its risk committee and its internal numbers are fictional; every fact about Spirit is from the public record as cited.)*

Ines runs post-mortems the way she ran her poker hand histories: every decision replayed in order, with only the information available at the time, and nobody allowed to say "obviously." The document on the screen on a Friday afternoon is titled *Post-mortem: the airline position*, and it is four pages long, which Mags says is three pages longer than the trade deserved.

Kaz presents the first page, which is just a timeline of the desk's position. The desk had bought a small block of Spirit's new equity in the weeks after emergence, sized at 0.4% of the book, on the logic of §16.2: the noteholders who received the stock included constrained holders, the stock had little coverage, and the overhang model said there was a forced-flow drift to collect. For about six weeks the model was right in the small way models are right; the stock drifted and the position made a little money. Then it stopped making money, and the position's line on the weekly risk report began to do something odd.

"Page two," Ines says. "The committee."

Page two is a transcript, of sorts, of the weekly risk committee from a Tuesday in July 2025. The committee's chair, a former bank risk officer with a gift for short questions, had circled one number on the report and asked the question every risk committee eventually asks. *Why is this number different from last week?*

The number was the position's modeled upside: the gap between the desk's fair value for the stock and its market price. The week before it had been about 38%. That week it was 52% *(illustrative)*. Nothing on the desk had changed. No new trade, no new model.

Kaz, who had built the fair-value model, gave the answer, and the transcript records it faithfully. (Theo had not yet joined the desk; he is reading this page for the first time.) "Because the price fell and our fair value didn't. The fair value is a peer multiple, enterprise value over earnings before rent, applied to the plan's projections. The peers didn't move and the projections are the plan's, so the fair value is the same as it was in March. The price is lower. So the upside is bigger."

"So the worse it gets," the chair said, "the better it looks?"

"On that metric, yes."

"Then I'd like a different metric by next week."

That was the most useful sentence spoken on the desk that summer, and the post-mortem says so in bold. The position had been *marked on a multiple while the runway shrank*. A multiple is a statement about what a business is worth if it keeps operating. It has no term in it for the business running out of cash first. The plan's projections were the plan's projections; they did not get worse just because the company's bank account did.

The post-reorg equity of March 2025 was therefore not "a small-cap airline at a cheap multiple." It was a short-dated call on the business plan's **liquidity bridge**: the weeks between emergence and the point at which the plan's revenue assumptions either showed up in cash or did not. A multiple cannot see a card processor holding back receipts or a lessor taking back aircraft. A 13-week cash flow model can, because those are exactly the line items it is built from: receipts by week net of holdbacks, aircraft rent by week, minimum liquidity covenants by week.

Page three is Theo's, and it is the newest page in the document. Ines had asked the desk's newest hire to rebuild, more than a year after the fact, the model the desk should have had that July, and he had spent last weekend doing what he had done for a living at PJT, except that this time nobody had given him the company's data room. He built the model from public filings only: the plan's projections from the disclosure statement, the post-emergence quarterly report, the 8-Ks, and the trade press. The structure was the one on his USB stick. Receipts by week, starting from the plan's revenue line and then reduced by the share of ticket sales that card processors were reported to be withholding until flights were flown. Aircraft rent by week, with the lease schedule from the filings and the reported terminations removed as fleet. Fuel and labor as a fraction of capacity. And at the bottom, the line that mattered: unrestricted liquidity against the minimum the company's financing documents required it to hold.

"The plan's revenue is fine," he tells the desk. "That's the trap. If you take the plan's revenue line, the company works. But revenue isn't cash when somebody else is holding the cash. Every week the processors hold back more, the receipts line falls below the revenue line, and the gap comes straight out of liquidity." He scrolls to the week where the two lines cross: the week in which, on his reconstruction, the plan's own assumptions stopped being cash, with receipts net of holdbacks no longer covering rent plus the cushion the documents required. "That's not a forecast that the company fails. It's the week its own plan stops paying the bills without help."

*(Theo's reconstruction is the fictional desk's; the weekly figures are illustrative, not Spirit's reported numbers. The public record does show the direction: in August 2025 the company's quarterly report warned of substantial doubt about its ability to continue as a going concern, per press reports, and on 29 Aug 2025 it filed again.)*

Theo does not say "I told you so"; he was not here to say it. He takes out the USB stick, puts it on the desk, and lets the stick say it.

Kaz's page, page four, is the repair. The desk turns his model into a feature in the jump term of every post-reorg position: let the weekly default intensity be a decreasing function of liquidity runway,
$$\lambda_t = \lambda_0 \exp\!\left(-\beta\,\frac{L_t}{B_t}\right),$$
where $L_t$ is unrestricted liquidity (after holdbacks), $B_t$ is projected weekly cash burn, $L_t/B_t$ is runway in weeks, and $\lambda_0, \beta > 0$ are fitted on re-defaults (Chapter 14's re-default clock covers the fitting). A post-reorg position's size then shrinks automatically as runway shortens, which is what a restructuring banker would have done by instinct and what a systematic book has to be told explicitly. For the warrant pricer in §16.4, this $\lambda_t$ is the jump intensity; for the book in §16.8, it is the jump-to-zero probability.

> **Math, Gently.** *An intensity that depends on runway.* Earlier in this chapter a hazard rate was a constant: 2% a year, every year. Here it becomes a dial. The formula says the hazard is at its maximum, $\lambda_0$, when runway is zero, and that every extra week of runway multiplies it by the same factor $e^{-\beta}$. Pick $\beta$ so that ten weeks of runway halves the hazard, which means $\beta = \ln 2/10 \approx 0.069$ per week. Then twenty weeks cuts it to a quarter, thirty weeks to an eighth, and five weeks cuts it by only about 30% ($e^{-0.35} \approx 0.71$). Read the other way, the dial is what makes the formula useful: as runway shrinks from twenty weeks to ten to five, the hazard rises from a quarter of its maximum to a half to about seventy percent, and it rises fastest exactly where a restructuring banker's stomach would start to hurt. The formula is not a forecast of when a company fails. It is a rule that converts the one number a 13-week model produces, weeks of runway, into the one number a pricing model consumes, an intensity, with two parameters you fit on past re-defaults and argue about in committee. The full treatment of intensities is Chapter 6; the fitting is Chapter 14.

"So with the feature on," Ines says, "what would the book have done?"

Kaz shows the counterfactual. With the runway intensity in the jump term, the position's modeled value had started falling in July instead of rising, and the sizing rule of §16.8 would have cut it by more than half before the August quarterly report and to nearly nothing before the second filing *(illustrative backtest of a fictional position)*. Not a hero trade. A smaller loss, taken earlier, for a reason the committee could read.

"And the part you'd still have lost," Mags says, "is the part where the model was right about the forced seller and wrong about the company. That's not a bug. That's the business." She taps page one. "The overhang drift was real. It was just sitting on top of a liquidity problem that was bigger than it."

The coda is in the public record. The second case did not end in a reorganization. On 2 May 2026 Spirit announced that it was winding down and cancelling all flights, after a floated federal rescue package of about \$500mm fell through (press reports); *The Octus Download* (EP 29, 7 May 2026) recounted that the package evaporated when the DIP lenders declined to go along *(podcast account; we have not confirmed the lenders' decision in the filings)*. The equity that emerged in March 2025 had been a five-month instrument. The airline it owned kept flying for about eight months after the second filing, and about fourteen months after emergence.

**The counterweight.** It would be easy to take the wrong lesson from Spirit, which is that post-reorg equity is a trap. Wolfspeed, which opened this chapter, is the same instrument with the opposite tail. Its new stock closed its first day close to the plan's \$22 a share, ended 2025 at about \$17.41, closed at \$73.50 on 26 May 2026, more than three times plan value, and was trading around \$31 in early October 2026 after a conditional \$1.5bn government loan commitment (vendor closing prices and press reports; the October figure is a single-source close of 8 Oct 2026). Same kind of security, born the same way, a year of returns in the right tail where Spirit's was in the left. And a 13-week model would not have called either one. It would have flagged Spirit's runway, which is what it is for; it would have had nothing to say about a chipmaker's equity tripling on demand and policy, because the 13-week model is a tool for seeing death coming, not for seeing life. That asymmetry is the right lesson. The runway intensity belongs in the jump term, where it shrinks positions that are running out of cash. It does not belong in the expected return, where it would have talked the book out of Wolfspeed for no reason.

**When the runway intensity applies, and when it breaks.** The feature works for cash-burning emergers with lumpy obligations: airlines with aircraft rent and processor holdbacks, retailers with seasonal inventory builds, anything where the plan's projections assume revenue will turn into cash on schedule and a small number of counterparties can stop that from happening. It is weakest for companies whose liquidity is *covenant-gated* rather than cash-gated: a business with plenty of cash that will default the day a leverage test fails, or a borrower whose real deadline is a springing maturity or a lender consent, not an empty bank account. For those, runway in weeks is the wrong state variable; the right one is headroom to the covenant or days to the consent deadline, and Chapter 13's Priming Risk Score is the better tool. The practitioner's first check is simple: find the line in the financing documents that would actually stop the company, and ask whether it is denominated in dollars of cash or in a ratio.

Ines closes the document. "Last question, and then you can all go home. That July, with the feature on, the book would have been holding two post-reorg airline equities: this one, and a smaller carrier that emerged a few months later." *(The second carrier is fictional.)* "The second one's runway looked fine. Its multiple looked cheap. Its sector had just watched a peer go to zero. Do you cut both, because the sector is telling you something? Do you hedge the pair with the airline index and keep the idiosyncratic piece? Or do you keep the second one, buy the next one that comes along, and buy it smaller, because the strategy is a portfolio and one zero is the price of the right tail?"

Nobody answers right away, which Ines takes as a sign that the post-mortem worked. "§16.8," she says. "Monday."

## 16.8 Putting It Together: A Post-Reorg Book

### 16.8.1 The distribution you are actually trading

Monday, 8:10 a.m. Ines's Friday question is still on the whiteboard in her handwriting, under the word *airlines*: cut both, hedge the pair with the sector, or keep the second carrier and buy the next one smaller. Somebody has added, in Kaz's handwriting, "define 'smaller'," which Ines has decided to treat as a contribution.

Theo spent part of the weekend on the question and has come in with an answer he likes, which in his experience is the first sign that something is wrong with it. "Cut both," he says. "We just watched a post-reorg equity go to zero in five months. The second carrier is the same instrument, in the same sector, born out of the same kind of plan. If I were still the banker on the second one, I'd be worried."

"You'd be worried about the company," Mags says. "Is the company the trade?"

Theo opens his mouth and closes it again, because that is the question. On Friday the desk spent four pages on one position. A strategy is not one position. It is a bet that a population of positions, each of which looks a little like Spirit on the morning it is bought, will on average pay for the few that turn into Spirit. Whether to cut the second carrier depends less on the second carrier than on what that population looks like, and the population is something the desk can write down.

Kaz writes it down, on the half of the whiteboard nobody has claimed. They combine §16.1's Trend Watch with §16.7's left tail. Over a 200-day horizon a single post-reorg equity's return $R$ is roughly a lognormal with median zero and mean +40% (the Jiang–Wang–Yang shape) with a jump to $-100\%$ mixed in (the Spirit shape). Write $X \sim N(0, s^2)$ and a jump indicator $J$ with $\Pr(J=1) = p_J$:
$$R = (1 - J)\left(e^{X} - 1\right) - J, \qquad \mathbb{E}[e^X] = e^{s^2/2} = 1.4 \;\Rightarrow\; s = \sqrt{2\ln 1.4} \approx 0.82 .$$

"How does a median of zero go with a mean of forty?" Theo asks. "That's the line I'd have circled in a fairness opinion."

"Because the exponential bends," Kaz says, and for once the explanation is short.

> **Math, Gently.** *Why the half-variance appears (a callback).* Chapter 1 (§1.5) and Chapter 6 (§6.1.1) built Itô's $\tfrac12\sigma^2$ from first principles; this is the same idea wearing this section's numbers, which is the promise §16.2.2 made. Suppose a post-reorg stock's 200-day log return is a coin flip, $+0.82$ or $-0.82$, so the *log* return averages exactly zero and the median outcome is "flat." The price multiples are $e^{0.82} \approx 2.27$ and $e^{-0.82} \approx 0.44$, and their average is about 1.36, not 1. The exponential curves upward, so the good flip gains more than the bad flip loses. Replace the coin with a full bell curve of the same spread, which has tails beyond $\pm 0.82$, and the lift is exactly $e^{s^2/2} = e^{0.336} \approx 1.40$: the "+40% mean, zero median" of §16.1.2 in one line. In continuous time the curvature shows up as $\tfrac12\sigma^2$ per year, the term Itô's lemma adds. Run it backwards and you get the fact this chapter's lottery tickets live on: a stock whose *price* is a fair game must have a log return that drifts *down* by $\tfrac12\sigma^2$, so the more volatile the ticket, the more of its average sits in a few draws and the worse its typical draw looks.

Then $\mathbb{E}[R] = (1-p_J)(1.4) - 1$, which is +33% for $p_J = 5\%$ *(illustrative)*, while the median is slightly *negative*: solving $p_J + (1-p_J)\Phi(\ln(1+m)/s) = \tfrac12$ gives $m \approx -5\%$.

> **Math, Gently.** *A mixture: a coin that picks the deck.* Chapter 5 (§5.1.1) introduced mixtures with a deck of cards; here is the post-reorg version. Before each draw, flip a weighted coin. Five times in a hundred it says "Spirit," and you draw from a deck with a single card in it, $-100\%$. Ninety-five times it says "survivor," and you draw from the lognormal deck above, whose average multiple is 1.40. The mixture's mean is just the coin-weighted mean of the two decks, $0.05 \times 0 + 0.95 \times 1.40 = 1.33$, which is the +33%. The median is less polite. The five cards at $-100\%$ all sit at the far left of the line, so to find the middle draw you have to walk past them first, and the halfway mark lands a little *inside* the survivor deck's lower half: the survivor deck needs $(0.50 - 0.05)/0.95 \approx 47.4\%$ of its draws below the median, which is about $0.066$ standard deviations below its own middle, or a log return of about $-0.054$, roughly $-5\%$. A small lump at zero barely dents the mean and quietly drags the median below water. Chapter 12-C uses the same construction for plan values; Chapter 5 has the general version.

"So the typical name loses money," Theo says, "and the average name makes a third."

Mags writes that sentence on a sticky note: **the typical single name loses money; the average name makes a third.** She puts it on the bottom edge of Theo's second monitor, under the Wolfspeed chart, where anyone proposing a concentrated post-reorg book will have to read it on the way to proposing it. Figure 16.5 shows the single-name distribution beside a 20-name book.

![Figure 16.5: A single post-reorg name has a mean return of +33% but a median of −5%, while an equal-weighted book of 20 names has a median of +29%, close to its mean, so the premium is a portfolio property. Illustrative: 400,000 simulated draws per panel from the §16.8.1 jump-lognormal mixture (s = 0.82, jump probability 5%, seed 16); the right panel groups them into 20,000 books of 20 names.](assets/figures/ch16_post_reorg_return_distribution.png)

Diversification turns the mean into the median. For an equal-weighted book of $N$ independent names, the book return has the same mean, standard deviation reduced by $\sqrt N$, and skewness reduced by $\sqrt N$, so its median moves toward its mean. With $N = 20$ (about the size of Jiang, Wang & Yang's portfolio) the median book return is comfortably positive, as Block 5 confirms by simulation.

Theo looks at the right-hand panel for a long time. "So Spirit wasn't a mistake in the strategy. It was one of the five cards."

"It was one of the five cards," Ines says. "Now read the word you skipped."

He reads the paragraph again. *Independent.* The $\sqrt N$ only works if the twenty names fail for twenty different reasons. Two airline equities that emerged within months of each other share fuel prices, leisure demand, aircraft lessors and, as of this summer, a card-processing industry that has learned to hold back receipts from carriers in trouble. If their returns move together with a correlation of 0.5, two names diversify like about one and a third ($N_{\text{eff}} = N/(1 + (N-1)\rho) = 2/1.5$) *(illustrative correlation)*, and the book's median does not get the help the figure promises. That is the honest answer to the whiteboard, or most of it. Spirit going to zero says little about the second carrier's own coin. It says a great deal about whether the desk's "twenty names" are really twenty. The sector hedge is the tool for the part the two carriers share, and the "buy the next one smaller" option is the tool for the part they do not, which leaves Kaz's question, *define smaller*, as the only thing left on the board.

### 16.8.2 Sizing: Kelly with a trapdoor, then a liquidity ceiling

Kaz answers their own whiteboard question before lunch, which is how Kaz answers most questions: by computing something. "Smaller," they announce, "is the growth-optimal fraction. I ran Kelly on our post-reorg history. For a single name it says about half the book."

Nobody says anything for a moment. Mags puts her coffee down.

"Half," Ines says. "Of the book. In one OTC stock."

"That's what the sample says." Kaz turns their screen around. "It's the vendor's post-reorg universe, 2005 to 2024, two hundred-odd names, 200-day returns from the effective date."

"How did the vendor build the universe?"

Kaz scrolls to the methodology note, which they have not read, and reads it now, aloud, at a speed that slows as they go. The universe was assembled from tickers with a price history in the vendor's equity database. A company that emerged, filed again and was cancelled had its new ticker retired, and a retired ticker with no successor fell out of the history file the following year. "So the names that went to zero," they say, "are not in the names."

"So your sample has no Spirits in it."

"My sample has no Spirits in it." Kaz looks at the number again. "And with no Spirits in it, half the book is exactly what the model says, if you take the jumps out. I can show you why."

> **Math, Gently.** *Kelly in one coin flip.* Somebody offers you a coin that lands heads 60% of the time and pays even money: bet a dollar, win a dollar or lose it. You will play hundreds of times, and each bet is a fraction $f$ of whatever you have at that moment. What fraction makes your wealth grow fastest? Not 100%. Bet everything and you are wiped out the first time the coin lands tails, which it will, usually within the first few flips; one zero multiplies everything after it by zero. Not 0% either, since you have an edge. The answer, from maximizing the average *log* of each flip's wealth multiple, is $f = 0.6 - 0.4 = 20\%$, and it grows your wealth by about 2.0% per flip ($0.6\ln 1.2 + 0.4\ln 0.8 \approx 0.020$). Two neighbors of that number are worth knowing by heart. Half-Kelly, 10%, earns about 1.5% a flip, three-quarters of the growth for half the swings, which is why desks that are unsure of their odds use it. Double-Kelly, 40%, earns about $-0.2\%$ a flip: twice the optimal bet on a *winning* coin loses money over time. Kelly's formula is just "maximize the expected log of wealth," and the log is what makes the trapdoor visible, because $\ln 0 = -\infty$: any bet that can lose everything at a size where everything is the bet scores minus infinity, however good the odds. In this chapter the coin is §16.8.1's mixture, and the trapdoor is the five-in-a-hundred card at $-100\%$. Chapter 28 (§28.4) carries the full treatment, with jump-to-default for bonds.

For a single name held as a fraction $f$ of capital, growth-optimal sizing maximizes $g(f) = \mathbb{E}[\ln(1 + fR)]$. The jump to $-100\%$ makes $g(f) \to -\infty$ as $f \to 1$, so the Kelly fraction is always strictly less than one, and the first-order condition
$$g'(f) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{R}{1 + fR}\right] = 0$$
has a neat check point at $f = \tfrac12$, where the survivor half of $R$ contributes exactly nothing and the jump half contributes $-2p_J$ (the short calculation is in the Full Derivation below). So $g'(\tfrac12) = -2p_J$, and two things follow at once. With any jump risk at all, full-name Kelly is below one half. And with $p_J = 0$, which is what a sample with no Spirits in it estimates, Kelly is *exactly* one half, which is Kaz's number, reproduced by a formula that believed everything it was told.

"So the formula is fine," Theo says, "and the data is lying."

"The data is telling the truth about a different population," Ines says. "The population of companies that survived long enough to be in a database. That's not the population you buy on effective date."

The afternoon brings the second half of the lesson, and it arrives as a ticket. Theo, who has been convinced by the morning that the strategy is a portfolio and that the next post-reorg name is worth owning, puts in a request to buy \$5mm of a small-cap packaging company that emerged last week and trades over the counter. It is the kind of name §16.2 is built for: a CLO-heavy fulcrum, an overhang the model can schedule, a discount to the rights price. Ines declines the ticket from her phone and walks over.

"About two hundred thousand dollars a day."

"If the trucking company calls us tomorrow and says it needs the risk budget, or if the packaging company's first 10-Q has a going-concern paragraph in it, how fast can you get out?"

Theo does it on a legal pad, because some habits survive the move to a systematic desk. If a position must be exitable at participation rate $\pi$ of average daily dollar volume $\text{ADV}$ within $d$ days, its size is capped at $\pi \cdot \text{ADV} \cdot d$. At a 10% participation, which is about as much of an OTC stock's volume as a desk can be before it *is* the volume, and a 20-day exit, the cap is $0.10 \times \$200\text{k} \times 20 = \$400\text{k}$.

"Four hundred thousand," he says.

"That's your size. Kelly's a ceiling on a ceiling. We use half-Kelly (Chapter 28), and in this corner of the market we never get anywhere near it, because the constraint that actually binds is the door." She turns to go, then turns back. "This is why the surviving post-reorg alpha, the part Jiang, Wang and Yang found still lives in the OTC names, doesn't scale. It's real. And a \$500mm book can own about four hundred thousand dollars of it per name."

Theo does the second piece of arithmetic in his head and does not enjoy it: \$400k is 0.08% of a \$500mm book. Twenty such names are 1.6% of the book. Mags, passing behind him, reads the number over his shoulder. "Back in my day," she says, "we did this trade with the whole fund and three phone numbers. We also didn't have a risk committee. One of those was a mistake."

#### The Full Derivation

The check point at $f = \tfrac12$ takes two lines. On the survivor branch, $R = e^X - 1$, so
$$\frac{R}{1 + \tfrac12 R} = \frac{2(e^X - 1)}{e^X + 1} = 2\tanh(X/2),$$
and since $X \sim N(0, s^2)$ is symmetric about zero and $\tanh$ is an odd function, $\mathbb{E}[2\tanh(X/2)] = 0$. On the jump branch, $R = -1$ and the integrand is $(-1)/(1 - \tfrac12) = -2$. Weighting the branches by $1 - p_J$ and $p_J$, the non-jump term is $\mathbb{E}[2\tanh(X/2)] = 0$ by symmetry of $X$, and the jump term is $p_J \cdot (-1)/(1 - \tfrac12) = -2p_J$, so $g'(\tfrac12) = -2p_J < 0$: with any jump risk at all, full-name Kelly is below one half. Because $g''(f) = -\mathbb{E}[R^2/(1+fR)^2] < 0$, $g$ is concave, so $g'(\tfrac12) < 0$ puts the maximum to the left of one half, and $g'(\tfrac12) = 0$ when $p_J = 0$ puts it exactly there.

#### When Kelly applies, and when it breaks

Kelly is at its most useful as an upper bound that you hope never binds. It is the answer to "how much could this bet possibly deserve if I knew the odds," and its value on a post-reorg desk is mostly the confirmation that the true constraint lies somewhere below it. It breaks in three ways that this chapter has already shown, each of which makes the formula's answer too large. It breaks when $p_J$ comes from a sample that dropped the Spirits, because a jump probability estimated at zero lets Kelly climb all the way to one half, as Kaz's vendor file did. It breaks when the names are not independent, because the $\sqrt N$ of §16.8.1 assumed twenty separate coins and the two airlines were closer to one coin flipped twice. And it breaks whenever the mean is uncertain, because Kelly's growth curve is flat near the top and steep beyond it: overestimating the edge by a factor of two takes you from the optimum to double-Kelly, which, as the coin flip showed, grows nothing. The seasoned practitioner's order of operations is therefore the reverse of the textbook's. Size to the liquidity ceiling first, with $\pi \cdot \text{ADV} \cdot d$ and an honest $d$. Then compute half-Kelly with a jump probability taken from a universe that includes the names that died, and check that the ceiling binds first. In OTC post-reorg names it almost always does. On the rare day it does not, the formula is saying the edge is too thin for the door to matter, which is worth hearing before anyone argues about participation rates.

### 16.8.3 The pair trades

By Thursday the whiteboard has filled up with a different kind of list. Nobody planned it. It is the questions people asked that week, written down by whoever was nearest the marker, and on Thursday afternoon Ines notices that every one of them has the same kind of answer: two positions, one long and one short, chosen so that everything cancels except the thing the person was asking about. The instruments in this chapter come in natural pairs, each isolating one risk, and the desk had found five of them without trying.

Theo asked on Monday how to bet that the packaging company's claims pool will come in smaller than the register says without also betting on when the plan goes effective. The answer was to own the 503(b)(9) claims against the general unsecured ones, or the reverse, because the two legs share the effective date and only one of them shares the pool. Mags asked on Tuesday, of a vendor offering its trade claim at 30 because payroll is on Friday while the senior unsecured bonds that will share the same distribution are quoted at 38 *(illustrative)*, which of the two prices is wrong. Neither is wrong about enterprise value, since the two claims share it; the gap is the vendor's forced-seller discount, and owning the claim against a short in the bond collects it while leaving the valuation fight to somebody else (Moyer p.269). Kaz asked on Wednesday whether they could own the volatility of a reorganized company's thirty-year warrants without having to have an opinion about the company; the answer was the warrant against the stock at the dilution-adjusted delta of §16.4.2, which keeps volatility and survival and hedges direction. The operations analyst who books the desk's new-issue settlements asked, the same afternoon, why the overhang model insisted that a name emerging next week would be cheapest weeks after its shares were delivered rather than on the first day of when-issued trading; the answer was the delivery lag $\alpha$ of §16.2.2, and a when-issued position against regular-way stock is the cleanest way to own it. And Harlan came back with another printout to ask whether a seat in a backstop was worth more than simply exercising the rights; the difference between the two, it turned out, is exactly the put premium plus the exclusivity of §16.3.2. Ines copies the five answers into a table so that nobody has to stand at the whiteboard to read them:

> **Poker Table.** Ines replays the cold open's hand through the model, assuming most of the CLO's allocation had not yet settled (delivery rate $\alpha = 5$ per year, execution rate $\theta \approx 3.4$, total discount $\kappa Q_0/M = 15\%$, all *illustrative*). Buying the CLO's first offer at the open marks out at about −4.4% after one month and about −6.6% at the three-month trough, then climbs back to about −1.2% after a year: the open was the first card of a long deal, not the end of the hand. Buying at the trough instead earns about +5.4% to the one-year mark from the flow alone, before anyone has an opinion about trucking. "You didn't lose because the stock was bad," Ines says. "You lost because you called before the seller had finished getting chips." The hand history is in Block 5.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The dealer desk.* (A generic seat; no real firm is described.) The systematic desk sees the CLO's day-one offer as a supply schedule to wait out. A bank's distressed or special-situations trading desk sees the same offer as a client asking for an exit, and the dealer's question is not "what is this stock worth?" but "how long will I hold it, and who takes it off me?" On day one the dealer may bid for a block from the CLO manager at a discount and then work it out over days to hedge funds, crossover accounts and, quite possibly, the systematic desk. In the language of §16.2, the dealer's block bid is a moment when $\theta$ jumps: weeks of drain compressed into an afternoon, with the overhang the systematic desk was scheduling moved onto a balance sheet that has its own clock, set by inventory limits and the capital the bank must hold against an illiquid equity position. Months later, around day 130 *(illustrative)*, when the largest holders' lock-ups have rolled off and, if the plan granted them registration rights, a registered secondary becomes possible, the same bank may be on the cover of the prospectus, pricing a marketed offering at a discount to the last trade and allocating it to long-only funds that would not have touched the stock on effective date. From that seat the post-reorg discount is not an anomaly. It is the fee for warehousing a stock between the holders the waterfall chose and the holders who actually want it, and the dealer is the one participant in the story who can be paid at both ends. The twist for the systematic desk is that the dealer's flow is information. A block that clears a CLO's position early is §16.2.4's expiry date arriving ahead of schedule, and a secondary is a second overhang with a date printed on it.

## 16.9 Desk Debrief

Friday, 6:40 p.m. The trucking company's stock is still on Theo's first monitor, where it has been, in one window or another, since the 9:31 a.m. open that started all this. Beside it is a draft of eight sentences for the desk wiki, which is where the desk keeps the things it means to remember and then forgets to read. Ines has agreed to hear them before he posts them, on one condition: he reads them out loud, "because a sentence that sounds fine in your head is how ROBO ends up in cash."

He reads the first. "**The plan value is a negotiated number.**"

"Negotiated by whom?"

"The controlling creditors."

"Who wanted it where?"

"Low," Theo says. He knows this; he sat in the rooms. A low plan value means more of the new company for every dollar of claim and a cheaper rights price for the people buying the rights. "Low, because they were on both sides of it."

"Then say that. 'Negotiated' sounds like a handshake. It was a price set by the buyer." He rewrites the line on the spot, and it goes up on the wiki this way: **The plan value is a number the controlling creditors negotiated, and they negotiated it down.** The honest anchor for every post-reorg instrument is the price insiders actually pay in the same plan, the rights price or the direct-investment price, and not the disclosure statement's midpoint.

The second sentence is Mags's, and she says so from across the aisle without looking up. **Forced selling is a supply schedule, not a mood.** The man at the regional bank, calling every Friday at 3:55, becomes three parameters: an overhang $Q_0$, an execution rate $\theta$ (about 3.4 a year, a 2.4-month half-life, from the fallen-angel aggregates), and capital $M_t$ that arrives slowly. When delivery is staggered, as it was across the eleven CLO vehicles, the discount peaks *after* the event rather than on it, and the signal expires when the 13G says the constrained holders are out. "And when he stops calling," Mags adds. "Put that in a footnote."

The third is Monday's whiteboard. **The post-reorg premium is a portfolio property.** Mean +40%, median zero, and no alpha after 2010 for listed names. Only a diversified book harvests the right tail, and the surviving OTC alpha does not scale past a few hundred thousand dollars a name, which Theo learned from a declined ticket and a legal pad.

The fourth he reads for Harlan, who is not there. **A backstop is a short put, and the fee is its smallest leg.** At a 25% discount and 70% volatility the fair fee is about 6%; the discount, the fee paid in shares and the exclusivity option take the all-in transfer to about 20% of the raise. Since *ConvergeOne*, an exclusive backstop without a market test also carries legal reversal risk.

The fifth makes Kaz wince, which Theo takes as confirmation that it is right. **Price warrants with dilution, and never charge for the funeral twice.** With a market spot, jump-to-default enters through $r + \lambda$, not as an $e^{-\lambda T}$ haircut. And the plan's own warrant valuation tells you the volatility the other side assumed, which is the closest a post-reorg desk ever gets to reading the other player's cards.

The sixth is the claims desk in one line. **A claim is long a ratio and short a call at par.** Near par, pool uncertainty costs recovery (about 8 points at a 30% log-sd); deep out of the money, it helps. The whole trade depends on knowing which side of the kink you are on.

The seventh Theo is pleased with, and reads with a little flourish. "**The 503(b)(9)/GUC pair isolates pool risk.** The person who has counted the leases, applied the §502(b)(6) cap and struck the duplicate claims should be long the GUC against the 503(b)(9). That person used to work at PJT."

Ines lets the flourish land and then takes it apart. "What did that person bring from PJT?"

"The ability to count leases."

"And what didn't they bring?"

He thinks about the October memo to compliance, about his old colleagues on the other side of the Hertz table, about the wall that is exactly as thick as the manual says. "Anything they learned there about a specific company."

"So the sentence is half right. The skill travels. The files don't." She nods at the USB stick, which is on his desk, as it always is. "The template on that stick is yours. The data rooms it was filled from never were." The line goes up as: **That person used to work at PJT, and brought the method, not the files.** It is less funny. Ines says that is how you can tell it is better.

The eighth needs no correction, because it came out of a four-page post-mortem and has the scar tissue to prove it. **Check the 13-week model before the multiple.** Spirit's first post-reorg equity lasted under six months; Hertz's warrants run to 2051 and the company was back in the room in 2026. Runway drives the jump term.

"Now the screen," Ines says.

Theo looks at the trucking company. It is the question from the first morning, and he can answer it now in the order the chapter taught him. Part of that 11% discount was flow: eleven vehicles on eleven clocks, four of them amortizing and with nowhere to put a share, a supply schedule with a half-life of about two and a half months and a trough that the staggered deliveries pushed weeks past the open. That part did not need a view on trucking. It needed patience and a registry that tied, and the right trade was not to lift the 9:31 offer but to let the seller finish getting chips and buy near the trough, at a size the door allowed. The rest of the discount was value, and that part was the old job: what a trucking-and-warehousing business is worth with its debt cut, whether its own plan's projections turn into cash on schedule, and what the rights price said about what the creditors really thought. "The flow I can schedule," he says. "The value I have to underwrite. And the plan value was never the answer to either. It was the opening bid."

"Post it," Ines says, and goes home.
