## Cold Open: Effective Date, 9:31 a.m.

Tuesday, 9:31 a.m. A trucking-and-warehousing company that yesterday existed only as a first-lien term loan opens on the OTC market as common stock. The new ticker is five consonants long. At the plan's equity value the market cap is \$140mm. (The company and its numbers are fictional; the mechanics are not.)

"Eleven bids," Theo says. "One offer."

"Whose offer?" Ines doesn't look up.

Theo has three windows open: the plan's distribution schedule, the ad hoc group's Rule 2019 statement, and a CLO trustee report he still has from his old life. "A CLO manager. They held twenty-two percent of the term loan. Now they hold twenty-two percent of the equity, and their indenture carries equity at zero in the overcollateralization test."

He scrolls the distribution schedule while he talks, because three weeks on this desk have taught him that the first answer is usually the summary line and the second answer is in the rows underneath it. The rows underneath are not one holder. The manager's twenty-two percent was allocated across eleven separate CLO vehicles, each with its own trustee, its own indenture and its own reinvestment period. Theo pastes the vehicle names into a spreadsheet next to the dates he can find in the trustee reports. Four of the eleven are past the end of their reinvestment periods and are amortizing, which means every dollar they raise goes to paying down their own notes and none of it can be recycled into a loan that pays a coupon. Three end their reinvestment periods inside the next six months. Four have more than two years left. The smallest vehicle holds 0.6% of the new company; the largest holds 4.1%.

"It's not one seller," he says. "It's eleven sellers who share a phone number. And they're on eleven different clocks."

"So every share they keep costs them cushion and pays them nothing." Now Ines looks. "What's the pot, what are the odds, and who at this table is *required* to fold, and by when?"

"I've been reading the disclosure statement," Kaz says. "It says the first-lien lenders were the fulcrum. Is that a gymnastics term?"

"It's the class where the value runs out," Theo says. "The debt above it gets paid, the debt below it gets warrants and apologies, and the fulcrum gets the company."

"So it's the point where the beam balances." Kaz considers this. "Mechanically, that's correct. Why does the beam want to sell itself?"

Mags arrives with coffee and the air of someone who has seen this film. "In 2003 we called these back-end equities. We bought them by phone from people who were embarrassed to own them. The trick was never the valuation. The trick was knowing who *had* to sell and waiting for them to get bored."

She sets the coffee down on the one corner of Theo's desk that is not a keyboard, which tells him a story is coming.

"There was a man at a regional bank," she says. "Workout group, nice suits, terrible tie. The bank had been a lender to a manufacturer that went through Chapter 11 and came out the other side owned by its lenders, so one morning he woke up holding a block of stock in a company he had spent two years trying to foreclose on. His credit committee did not want it on the books. Every Friday at 3:55 he called me and offered fifty thousand shares. Every Friday. I thought he was a nervous seller. I thought he got to the end of the week, looked at the position, felt sick and picked up the phone. So I'd bid him a little cheap, he'd grumble, and we'd trade."

"A mood," Kaz says.

"That's what I thought. Then one Friday in March he called at 3:55 and I asked him, just making conversation, why he never called on a Tuesday. And he told me. His committee had given him a reduction target in shares per week, and his position report went to the committee at 4:15 on Friday. He wasn't sick. He was filing a report." Mags drinks her coffee. "That was the day I realised the Friday calls were a schedule, not a mood. A mood you have to guess. A schedule you can *read*. I stopped bidding on Thursdays, because why would I compete with the other desks for stock that was going to walk in my door in twenty-four hours? I started keeping a little card with how much he'd sold and how much the committee wanted gone. And in June he didn't call. Friday, 3:55, nothing. I phoned him at four and he said, 'We're done.' The stock went up eighteen percent in the next three weeks, and the only thing that had changed about the company was that a man at a bank had stopped filing a report." *(Mags's banker and his manufacturer are a fictional composite of several early-2000s workouts; the mechanism is the chapter.)*

"We don't wait for people to get bored anymore," Ines says. "We model how fast they get bored."

Kaz has been looking at the bid stack. "Here's what I don't understand. Eleven bids, and they're all within a quarter of each other. If there are eleven buyers who each think this company is worth something different, the bids should be spread out. Somebody should think it's worth plan value. Somebody should think it's worth half. Instead it looks like the bids were set by a committee."

"They were," Ines says. "Just not a committee that met." She turns her chair. "Eleven bids at the same level isn't eleven opinions about trucking. It's a table where everyone is waiting for the same player to fold. Every one of those desks has read the same distribution schedule Theo has open. They all know who has to sell. So nobody wants to be the one who bids up, because the person you'd be paying is a forced seller who was going to come to you anyway. The bid isn't a valuation of the company. It's a valuation of the seller's patience."

"So the price is a statement about the seller," Kaz says slowly, "not about the asset."

"For now. That's the whole trick of this chapter. For some window, the price is about the seller. After the window closes, it's about the asset again, and you'd better have a view of the asset by then."

"How do you know which one you're looking at?" Theo asks.

Ines leans over and opens a chart on his second monitor. "Pull up Wolfspeed. Twenty-ninth of September, last year."

Theo knows the name. Wolfspeed, the silicon-carbide chipmaker, had come out of a prepackaged Chapter 11 in Houston that morning, with the old stock cancelled and a new stock on the same ticker. Old stockholders had received about 0.008352 new shares for every old share they had held, rising to about 0.013920 once a regulatory clearance released another tranche months later (company 8-Ks; ratios calculated from the share counts). The new stock opened at about \$18, against a plan equity value of about \$22 a share. What happened next was a range so wide that trading was halted for volatility for part of the session, and the stock still closed the day within a dollar or so of plan value (vendors differ on the exact closing print). Reuters reported it up about a third the following day.

"That looks like a disaster," Theo says, "and then it looks like nothing happened."

"It looks like price discovery," Ines says. "Who got Wolfspeed's new stock?"

Theo scrolls. "Convertible noteholders, mostly. The converts were the fulcrum. About twenty-four and a half million of the roughly twenty-six million shares went to them."

"And who holds converts?"

"Hedge funds. Convert arb, distressed funds, crossover."

"Who has an OC test?"

"Nobody." Theo sees it. "There's no CLO in that stock. Nobody in that cap table is carrying the equity at zero against a ratio test. Nobody has a disposition clock."

"So that wild first morning was a few thousand people working out, in real time, what a silicon-carbide company is worth with the debt cut, and they got there by lunch. Violent, but it was about the asset from the first minute. There was no seller to schedule." Ines taps Theo's first monitor. "This one's fulcrum was a CLO-heavy term loan. Twenty-two percent of the stock is held by eleven vehicles that are paid to hold loans and penalized for holding stock. Whatever range this thing trades in for the next three months, a large part of it isn't price discovery. It's supply."

"Supply that runs out," Mags says. "Eventually the man at the bank stops calling."

Theo looks at the screen. The plan says \$140mm. The bids say less. The CLO's offer sits there like a confession. Somewhere in this stock's first ninety days there is a return. The desk's question is how much of it is a *flow we can schedule*, meaning a known seller working down a known position against slow-arriving capital, and how much is *value we have to underwrite* the old way, with a model of a trucking business and a strong stomach.

He does the arithmetic on the offer. It is 11% under plan value. The bids are a quarter of a point below that. In his old job he would have known exactly what to do with an 11% discount to a number he had helped negotiate: he would have known it was too high, or too low, and by roughly how much, and he would have known which exhibit of the disclosure statement it came from. Here the number is the same and the question is different.

"So," Ines says. "The offer is eleven percent under plan value. Do you lift it, do you wait, or do you short the open and buy the trough? And what would you need to know first?"

Theo opens a fourth window and starts typing a list. *Which of the eleven vehicles can sell today. How fast the other seven have to follow. Who else got stock and what they're allowed to hold. Whether there's a rights offering, and who backstopped it. Whether there are warrants, and who has them. Whether the trade creditors are about to sell their claims and buy the stock.* He stops. "And what the plan value is," he says. "Not the number. Where the number came from."

"Good," Ines says. "Now you know what the chapter is for."

What follows answers Ines's question one instrument at a time, and each answer has the same shape. First, find the holder the waterfall chose and the market would not have: the CLO, the bank, the insurer, the old stockholder, the vendor. Second, write down what forces their hand, whether an OC test, a disposition clock, a capital charge or a payroll. Third, price what they hold as the option it is. We will turn the forced seller into a supply schedule with a half-life and a signal that knows when it expires; we will see that a rights offering is a call, the backstop behind it is a put, and the warrants handed to old stockholders are a long-dated call on a call, that the backstop party's all-in take is several legs of which the advertised fee is the smallest, and that the plan's own numbers tell you what volatility the other side assumed; we will price the humblest instrument in the estate, a trade claim, whose value depends on a denominator nobody yet knows, and trade a 503(b)(9) claim against a general unsecured claim as a pure bet on that denominator. Hertz runs the whole circuit in one company, from a 2020 filing to a return to the restructuring room in 2026. Spirit Airlines shows how fast the left tail can arrive. And at the end we come back to this screen at 9:31 a.m., able to say out loud which part of the discount is flow and which part is value, and what to do about each.

A word on measures before we start. None of these instruments has a liquid hedge on the day it is born. Every expectation in this chapter is therefore under the physical measure $\mathbb{P}$ unless we say otherwise, and any $\mathbb{Q}$ that appears is the measure of some traded proxy (when-issued stock, peer equities, the high-yield index), not a true risk-neutral measure for the instrument itself. Kaz will try to write $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}$ out of habit. We will stop Kaz.

A word on notation, too. Arbitrage capital is $M_t$ (not $K$, which is a strike in §16.3); the warrant dilution ratio is $\omega$ (not $q$, which is inventory elsewhere in the book); distributable value and the allowed claim pool in §16.5 are $\mathcal{V}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ (calligraphic, so they cannot be confused with firm value $V_t$, a call price, or duration $D$).

## 16.1 The Back-End Equity Problem: What Emerges and Who Is Not Allowed to Hold It

### 16.1.1 Desk framing: a plan is a security factory with a distribution problem

At PJT you built the factory. You sat in the room where the waterfall was negotiated, you ran the recovery tables, and you watched the plan equity value move \$300mm in an afternoon because one side wanted more shares per dollar of claim and the other side wanted the juniors out of the money. You therefore already know the most important fact in this chapter: **the plan equity value is a negotiated number, not an estimate.** Moyer makes the same point from the buy side (Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.355 and p.369): investors who will control the reorganized company prefer conservative valuations, because a lower value means more shares for their claims and a cleaner path past the classes below them.

A restructuring partner would put the consequence in one sentence, and it is worth having it in your head before you look at any day-one tape: when the fulcrum class controls the process, confirmed plan values tend to sit below the values at which the same equity later trades or sells (Moyer p.355 and p.369 give the incentive; the regularity is the practitioner's, not a statistic we have measured). Envision Healthcare is a recent dated instance. Its AmSurg business emerged on 3 Nov 2023 with the second-lien lenders taking all of the equity, alongside a \$300mm rights offering priced at a 30% discount, and the plan projected a 73% recovery for that class. In June 2025 Ascension agreed to buy AmSurg for a reported \$3.9bn, which the trade press put above the top of the plan's valuation range (Octus, Jul 2025; Becker's; treat the exact gap as approximate). The second-lien lenders had not been wrong about the business. They had been on the side of the table that benefits from a low number, and the number had been low. Who negotiates the plan value, against which exhibit of the disclosure statement, and what a judge does when the two sides' bankers are \$300mm apart, is the subject of §12B.6 and Chapter 12-C. Here we only need the habit: treat the plan value as one side's opening bid that the other side accepted, not as a forecast.

> **Math, Gently.** *A share is a call.* You own a house worth \$500k with a \$400k mortgage, so your equity is \$100k. If the house rises to \$600k, your equity doubles to \$200k. If the house falls to \$350k, your equity does not become minus \$50k: you can hand the bank the keys and walk away, and your loss stops at the \$100k you had. A payoff that keeps all of the upside above a level and none of the downside below it is exactly a call option, and the level (here the mortgage) is the strike. A company's common stock is the same thing with a balance sheet instead of a house: limited liability means equity is worth the larger of "enterprise value minus debt" and zero. Tiny number: a reorganized company worth 100 with 80 of new debt has equity worth 20. If enterprise value later ends at 70, the equity is worth 0, not −10; if it ends at 130, the equity is worth 50. Now hand someone a warrant that pays only the equity value above 30. That warrant is worthless unless the equity more than half-again rises, which needs enterprise value above 110: it is a call whose underlying is itself a call. Everything in the table below is a version of this picture with a different strike, a different maturity, or a different person forced to hold it. The full version, with a volatility and a clock, is Merton's model in Chapter 6; the call-on-a-call is §16.4.

A plan converts claims into a basket of new instruments. The desk translates each one into option language, because that is the only language in which they can be compared.

To see the basket arrive, follow one fictional creditor through an effective date. Call it Ninefold Credit Partners, a mid-sized fund that owned a little of everything in the trucking company's capital structure. On the effective date its first-lien term loan disappears and is replaced by two things: a stack of new common stock, because the first lien was the fulcrum, and a smaller new term loan, because the plan handed part of the old claim back as take-back debt. As a member of the first-lien class Ninefold also received subscription rights, a short-lived right to buy more new stock at a fixed price, and because it sat in the ad hoc group and signed the backstop commitment it was obliged to buy whatever the rest of the class did not, in exchange for a fee. Its small second-lien position, which the waterfall left out of the money, came back as warrants. A trade claim it had bought from a tire supplier came back as an interest in a litigation trust, and a handful of tiny claims it had picked up in a bundle were paid in cash under the convenience class. One fund, seven instruments, and only some of them are things Ninefold would have chosen to buy:

| Instrument | In option language | Typical recipient | Who cannot comfortably hold it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| New common stock | A call on enterprise value struck at the new debt (Merton, Chapter 6) | Fulcrum class | CLOs (carried at zero in OC tests), banks (disposition clock), insurers (capital charges) |
| Warrants | A call on that call, usually long-dated and out of the money | Junior classes, old equity | Anyone who needs liquidity (see §16.4) |
| Subscription rights | A short-dated call struck at the rights price | Eligible members of a class | Holders with no cash, ineligible holders |
| Backstop commitment | A short put plus a fee | "The party" (§16.3) | Everyone not invited into the room |
| Take-back debt | Debt, with a recovery haircut baked in | Senior classes | Nobody, which is why CLOs ask for it |
| Litigation-trust interests | A non-transferable lottery ticket on lawsuits | General unsecured creditors | Anyone who wants to sell it |
| Cash | Cash | Administrative, 503(b)(9) and convenience claims | Nobody has ever objected |

If you read only one column of that table, read the last one; it is the chapter. The second column tells you how to price each instrument, and the third tells you who receives it, but the last column tells you who will be selling it to you and why they have no choice.

The last column is where the trading lives. Each instrument is born in the hands of a holder chosen by the legal waterfall, not by the market, and the waterfall does not care whether that holder is permitted to own what it receives. A CLO that held the fulcrum loan gets stock its documents treat as worthless. A bank that took equity in a debt-for-equity swap has a regulatory clock and an unpleasant capital charge while it waits (Moyer p.370). An insurer pays a far higher risk-based capital charge on common stock than on the bond it used to own. Index funds cannot own the stock until it lists and seasons. The old equity holders, who were told for a year that they would get nothing, get warrants they did not ask for and cannot value. The only natural holders on the first day are hedge funds, and they know they are the only natural holders.

So the first quantity the desk writes down is not a valuation. It is the **overhang share**: constrained holdings as a fraction of the stock that can actually trade.
$$\phi^{\text{OH}} = \frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{c}} n_j}{n^{\text{float}}}, \qquad n^{\text{float}} = n^{\text{out}} - n^{\text{ctrl}} - n^{\text{lock}},$$
where $\mathcal{J}^{c}$ is the set of constrained holders (CLOs, banks, insurers), $n_j$ is holder $j$'s share count from the plan's distribution schedule, $n^{\text{out}}$ is shares outstanding, $n^{\text{ctrl}}$ is shares held by a controlling holder who will not sell, and $n^{\text{lock}}$ is shares under a post-confirmation lock-up (Moyer p.372 discusses why controllers sometimes restrict trading early on). In the cold open, if a sponsor holds a locked 35% and the CLOs hold 22%, the investable float is 65% of the company and the overhang share is $22/65 \approx 34\%$ *(illustrative)*. A third of everything that can trade is in the hands of someone who has to sell it. Moyer's related point (p.369) is that the investable equity cap, not the headline cap, is the "technical factor" that determines who can even look at the stock: a controller holding half the shares halves the cap a fund can buy into.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.370:* "Banks and collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) are not natural holders of equity securities. The business model of these entities causes them to significantly prefer income earning assets as opposed to equities. ... Similarly, CDOs usually have contractual limitations on the absolute amount of and length of time they are allowed to hold equity."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer wrote this in 2004 about CDOs. In 2026 the relevant vehicle is the broadly syndicated loan CLO, and the sentence has become more true, not less. Moyer treats the forced seller as a qualitative hazard that a careful investor "will certainly consider" (same page) and that a careful plan designer avoids by offering the constrained class a non-equity recovery (p.371). The systematic desk reads the same paragraph as a *specification*: "absolute amount" is a position cap, "length of time" is a deadline, and together they define a supply schedule with a start date, a size, and an end date. That is something you can forecast, and anything you can forecast you can trade ahead of. The modern departure is in the docs: post-2020 CLO indentures let managers hold restructured *loans* more patiently (Trend Watch in §16.2), which means plans now hand CLOs take-back paper wherever the debt capacity exists. Where it does not, because the business can only support so much new debt, the equity overhang that remains is *more* concentrated in CLO hands and *more* predictable.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The CLO manager.* Sit for a moment in the chair of the portfolio manager who owns those eleven vehicles. From that seat the new stock is not an investment opportunity; it is a hole in a ratio. A CLO's overcollateralization test compares the par of its loans to the par of the notes it issued, and the public commentary on these documents is blunt about what happens to a restructured asset that comes back as stock: Weil's June 2024 note on CLOs as restructuring creditors observes that managers generally want to avoid that outcome because equity may be discounted entirely in the OC test, and S&P's *Par Wars* (23 Sep 2022) describes how assets far from the collateral-obligation definition are carried at zero. So a \$10mm loan position that becomes \$8mm of stock at plan value removes \$10mm from the numerator and puts back nothing, and the stock pays no coupon to cover the notes' interest. Sell it for \$7mm, an eighth below plan value, and buy a performing loan at 98, and the manager puts about \$7.1mm of par back into the test the same afternoon *(illustrative)*. If the test is close to its trigger, holding the stock risks diverting cash away from the CLO's own equity investors and the manager's subordinated fee; selling it at a discount repairs the ratio immediately. That is why, on the creditor committee months earlier, this manager argued for take-back paper instead of shares and lost only because the business could not carry more debt. The twist for the desk is that the manager is not a fool for selling cheap. The indenture makes holding cost more than the discount, and a rational seller with that cost will keep selling until the cost goes away. The discount is not a mistake to be exploited once; it is a price the seller is willing to pay, every week, until the position is gone.

### 16.1.2 What history says the stock does next

The academic record on post-reorg equity is a textbook example of an anomaly that was published, studied, and then eaten by the people who read the studies.

> **Trend Watch.** *The post-reorg premium: discovered 1999, gone by 2010.*
> - **1980–1993.** Eberhart, Altman & Aggarwal (1999), *The Equity Performance of Firms Emerging from Bankruptcy*, *Journal of Finance* 54(5): 131 emerging firms earned large positive excess returns over the first 200 trading days on every benchmark the authors tried (check the exact range in the paper). The authors read it as an expectational error, not a risk premium.
> - **2001–2003.** Moyer (p.374), citing Imperial Capital and Bloomberg data, reports that back-end equities from the 2001 and 2002 emergence vintages beat the S&P 500 handily in 2003, and cites a study of 117 post-reorg equities over 1986–2003 that also found outperformance.
> - **1981–2019.** Jiang, Wang & Yang, *The Disappearing Outperformance of Post-reorg Equity* (summarized on the Duke FinReg Blog, 17 Sep 2021), track every US public filer with at least \$50mm of assets that filed in 1981–2016 and emerged public. Mean 200-day abnormal returns are 40% to 50%, but **the median abnormal return is zero**: the gains live in the right tail. An equal-weighted, monthly rebalanced portfolio from 1992 (about 20 stocks at a time) beat risk-adjusted benchmarks by 7.2% a year, concentrated in OTC names. Five-factor alpha was 0.8% to 1.2% a month in 2000–2010 and **zero after 2010**, and exchange-listed post-reorg stocks earned *negative* alpha in the most recent decade. Institutional ownership of post-reorg equity rose from about 40% in the 1990s to about 80% in the 2000s.
> - **What it means for the desk.** Buy-and-hold "post-reorg beta" is dead. What survives is (a) right-tail skew, which only a diversified book harvests (§16.8), (b) the OTC and illiquid segment where institutions still cannot or will not go, and (c) the *timing* of forced flow, which is the subject of §16.2.

Read the third bullet twice. "Mean 40%, median zero" is not a footnote; it is the whole strategy. A single post-reorg stock is a lottery ticket with a positive expected value and a coin-flip chance of doing nothing. The premium is a property of a *portfolio* of such stocks, and it is only available to someone who can own twenty of them at once without moving the price. We come back to that arithmetic in §16.8, with a jump-to-zero added, because Spirit Airlines insists.

> **Math, Gently.** *Why the mean and the median disagree.* Buy three post-reorg stocks. One halves (−50%), one goes nowhere (0%), one goes up 170%. The mean return is $(-50 + 0 + 170)/3 = +40\%$. The median, the middle one when you line them up, is 0%. Both numbers are honest; they answer different questions. The mean is what a portfolio of many such stocks earns on average; the median is what happens to *your* stock if you own only one. They agree only when the distribution is symmetric, like the bell-shaped normal distribution, where a move of +10 is exactly as likely as a move of −10 and the middle of the bell is also its balance point. Returns on a thing that can lose at most 100% but can gain 1,000% cannot be symmetric: the left side of the picture hits a wall at −100% and the right side runs off the page. The standard shape for that is the lognormal, which is what you get when gains and losses compound multiplicatively, and its long right tail drags the mean above the median like one tall person dragging up a room's average height. Post-reorg equity is lognormal-ish with an extra lump at zero for the companies that file again, which makes the gap between mean and median even wider. Chapter 5 tours the distribution family; §16.8.1 builds the exact mixture this chapter trades.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.374:* "A 1998 study concluded that outperformance is most pronounced during the first 200 days after a stock emerges and in those situations where institutional investors demanded to receive only equity in the restructuring."
>
> **Annotation.** Two features in one sentence. The first is the 200-day window, which reappears in every later study and which §16.2 explains mechanically: 200 trading days is about four supply half-lives for a seller that executes at the rate the fallen-angel data imply, which is the point by which roughly 93% of the forced supply has cleared. The second is the more interesting one for a former restructuring banker: *who elected equity.* Many plans offer creditors a choice between a cash-out (or take-back paper) and stock, and the election results are disclosed in ballot certifications and distribution notices. A sophisticated holder that elects stock over cash at the plan value is revealing a private valuation, which is exactly Moyer's next sentence about insiders who thought the equity was cheap. The desk turns this into a feature, the equity-election share of informed holders. The modern departure is that Jiang, Wang & Yang's rise in institutional ownership means the informed electors are now usually the marginal holders on day one, so the signal is weaker than it was in 1998, and it is strongest precisely where the electors are few and the forced sellers are many.

## 16.2 Forced Selling as a Supply Process: From Fallen Angels to Orphan Equity

### 16.2.1 Desk framing: borrow the cleanest experiment on the shelf

We do not have a clean, large-sample study of CLO-driven selling in post-reorg stocks. We do have one of the cleanest natural experiments in all of credit: the fallen angel. When a bond is downgraded from investment grade to high yield, a large population of holders with investment-grade mandates is *required* to sell, regardless of what they think the bond is worth. That is the post-reorg CLO problem with a different label on the folder. Dynkin's account (Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.140, p.151, p.158) and Ben Dor's (Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.27, p.341) give us the shape: trading volume roughly quadruples around the event while bid-ask spreads *widen* (sellers paying for immediacy, not information), downgraded issuers underperform their peers by 15.09% into the event, and the price recovers over the following one to two years.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.27:* "We present strong empirical evidence of the transient price pressure experienced by these securities due to forced selling driven by investment constraints. A study of the time course of this effect shows that this price pressure tends to peak about three months after the downgrade. Over a period of one to two years following the downgrade, most securities recover."
>
> **Annotation.** Three facts a model must reproduce: the pressure is *transient* (it mean-reverts, so it is not information), it *peaks after the event* (so the sellers do not all hit the bid on day one), and it decays over *one to two years* (so there is a slow component, too). A model in which constrained holders dump everything at the open fails the second fact; a model with a single exponential decay will fail the third, as we show in §16.2.3. For post-reorg equity, the "event" is the effective date or the end of a lock-up, and the "investment constraint" is a CLO indenture rather than an investment-grade mandate. The departure from the bond setting is that equity has no coupon to pay you while you wait, and the post-reorg stock may have no analyst coverage at all (Moyer p.371), so the arbitrage capital that eventually absorbs the overhang arrives even more slowly.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.151:* "In the case of forced selling, upgraded issuers should not exhibit a reversal, since they are not subject to induced buying (or selling) by investors."
>
> **Annotation.** This is identification by asymmetry, and it is the best idea in this section. Overreaction would predict reversals in both directions; a constraint predicts a reversal only on the side where somebody is forced to trade. Dynkin finds upgraded issuers outperform by only about a third as much (5.66% vs. −15.09%, same page) and without the reversal. The post-reorg analogue gives us a cross-sectional test the desk can actually run: compare emergences where the fulcrum class was held mainly by CLOs (large forced supply) with emergences where it was held mainly by hedge funds (almost none). If the post-emergence drift is a constraint effect, it should scale with the CLO share of the fulcrum, and it should be absent where that share is near zero.

Theo's 13-week cash flow model makes its appearance here in an unexpected costume. In his old life the 13-week model was a schedule of cash *inflows and outflows by week*, with known dates and estimated sizes. The overhang model is the same object for shares: a schedule of forced *outflows by week*, with known start dates (the effective date, the lock-up expiry) and estimated sizes (the distribution schedule). Theo's USB stick, it turns out, contained the right data structure all along.

#### When the overhang model applies, and when it breaks

Borrowing a result from fallen angels is a loan, and loans have covenants. Before Theo builds anything, Mags makes him say out loud when the borrowed shape should hold for a post-reorg stock, and the answer comes in three conditions. The first is that the constrained holders must be *identifiable*: the plan's distribution schedule, the ad hoc group's Rule 2019 statements and the CLO trustee reports have to let you name who received the stock and roughly how much, because a supply schedule with an unknown size is just a mood with a spreadsheet attached. The second is that the stock must be small enough that the constrained holders' selling *is* the volume. In the cold open's \$140mm company, the eleven vehicles' 22% is a month or two of trading on its own; in a multibillion-dollar emergence with an index-eligible float, the same percentage is noise against the natural turnover. The third is that there must be no fundamental catalyst inside the window, because the model has one moving part, the seller, and an earnings release is a second moving part that does not care about the first.

The model breaks, in practice, in three corresponding ways. It breaks when the fulcrum was held by hedge funds, as at Hertz and Wolfspeed, where $Q_0 \approx 0$ and whatever the stock does in its first ninety days is price discovery about the business, not the decay of a forced position. It breaks when the first post-emergence earnings release lands inside the half-life, which happens more often than you would like because emergence dates cluster around quarter ends, and the release then dominates everything the overhang was going to do. And it breaks when the sellers are not as polite as the model assumes. Our supply equation says the constrained holders execute a constant fraction $\theta$ of what remains; real managers accelerate when a bid appears that repairs their test in one print, and slow down when the stock is falling and every sale crystallizes a loss in front of their own investors. A constant $\theta$ is a good average and a bad description of any particular Tuesday.

What does a seasoned practitioner check first? Not the half-life. Mags checks whether the CLOs *elected* take-back paper. Many plans let the fulcrum choose between more stock and more new debt, and CLO managers with any choice choose the debt; the election results, buried in the ballot certification or the distribution notice, tell you whether the overhang you are about to model exists at all. "Half the time," she says, "the forced seller you're excited about already sold you their problem in the form of a term loan, and the stock is owned by people who wanted it."

### 16.2.2 Derivation: overhang, slow capital, and the drift you can schedule

Mags's banker gives us the whole model in one anecdote: a fixed position, a seller working it down on a schedule, buyers who arrive when they notice, and a price that is depressed while the seller is still calling and recovers when he stops. What follows writes that story in three equations and then asks what return it leaves on the table for the patient buyer.

Let $v_t$ be the fundamental log value of the stock, which we treat as a $\mathbb{P}$-martingale over a 90-day horizon (we ignore the equity risk premium, which is small over that horizon compared with the effect we are measuring, and we will strip peer returns before fitting, as in Chapter 8). Let $Q_t \ge 0$ be the **overhang**: shares that constrained holders are required to sell and have not yet sold, measured in dollars at the plan value. Let $M_t > 0$ be the **arbitrage capital** willing to absorb that supply, in the same units. Three assumptions define the model.

> **Math, Gently.** *A random walk and a fair game.* Flip a coin every day; heads the stock goes up a step, tails it goes down a step. The path it traces is a random walk, and its most useful property is that your best forecast of where it will be next month is exactly where it is right now: the ups and downs you expect cancel. A process with that property is called a *martingale*, and that is all the word means: a fair game, in which no amount of staring at the past tells you which way the next step goes. Saying that the fundamental value $v_t$ is a martingale is therefore a modest assumption with a big consequence. It says the *business* gives you no predictable drift over the next 90 days, so any drift we can predict must come from somewhere else, here the seller and the buyers. Tiny number: if $v_t$ is 4.60 today (a log value, about \$100), our expectation for $v_t$ in three months is 4.60, even though it might end at 4.40 or 4.80. There is one wrinkle, about the difference between a fair game in log value and a fair game in dollars, involving a $\tfrac12\sigma^2$; Chapter 6 introduces it and §16.8.1 shows why it matters for this chapter's lottery tickets.

*Supply.* Constrained holders execute a fixed fraction of what remains per unit time:
$$dQ_t = -\theta\, Q_t\,dt, \qquad Q_{t_0} = Q_0,$$
where $\theta > 0$ is the execution rate (per year) and $t_0$ is the effective date or the lock-up expiry, whichever comes later.

> **Math, Gently.** *Exponential decay and half-life.* Picture a bathtub with the plug pulled, where the water drains faster when the tub is full and slower as it empties, because the flow is proportional to what is left. That is what $dQ = -\theta Q\,dt$ says: each instant, the sellers dispose of a fixed *fraction* $\theta$ of whatever they still hold, so big positions shrink quickly in shares and small ones slowly. The solution is $Q_t = Q_0 e^{-\theta(t-t_0)}$, and the natural way to read it is by its half-life, the time it takes to sell half of what remains: $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/\theta$. With $\theta = 3.4$ per year, the half-life is $0.693/3.4 \approx 0.20$ years, about 2.4 months. After one half-life half the overhang is gone; after two, three-quarters; after three, seven-eighths. The tub never quite empties in the equation, which is the mathematician's way of saying that the last few CLO vehicles always take longer than you planned. We fit $\theta$ to data in §16.2.3.

*Capital.* Arbitrage capital arrives slowly, in the spirit of Duffie's (2010) slow-moving capital:
$$dM_t = \rho\,(M^{*} - M_t)\,dt, \qquad M_{t_0} = M_0,$$
with $M^{*}$ the long-run capital that will eventually cover the name and $\rho$ its arrival speed (per year). The solution is $M_t = M^{*} + (M_0 - M^{*})e^{-\rho(t-t_0)}$.

*Price.* The observed log price carries a liquidity discount proportional to overhang per unit of capital:
$$p_t = v_t - \kappa\,\frac{Q_t}{M_t},$$
where $\kappa > 0$ is the price-impact coefficient (log points per unit of overhang-to-capital ratio).

Read the price equation as a sentence: the stock trades below what the business is worth by an amount that grows with how much the forced sellers still hold and shrinks with how much patient money has shown up. The tradeable object is the expected *excess* drift, the part of the return that is not fundamental. Because the business is a fair game, the only predictable motion in the price is the motion of that discount, and the discount shrinks for two reasons at once: the sellers are running out, and the buyers are arriving. The two-line calculation (quotient rule, then the martingale property) is in *The Full Derivation* below; the result is
$$\mu_t \equiv \frac{d}{dt}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}_{t_0}\!\left[p_t - v_t\right] = \kappa\left(\theta\,\frac{Q_t}{M_t} + \frac{Q_t\,\dot M_t}{M_t^{2}}\right).$$
Both terms are positive while capital is still arriving ($\dot M_t > 0$): the stock drifts up because the sellers are running out *and* because the buyers are showing up. With constant capital ($\rho = 0$, $M_t \equiv M$) everything is closed-form:
$$p_t - v_t = -\frac{\kappa Q_0}{M}\,e^{-\theta (t - t_0)}, \qquad \int_{t_0}^{\infty}\mu_t\,dt = \frac{\kappa Q_0}{M}, \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\theta}.$$
The total abnormal return available to a buyer at $t_0$ is the initial discount $\kappa Q_0/M$, and half of it is gone after one half-life. The boundary conditions are sensible: if $Q_0 = 0$ (hedge funds held the fulcrum) there is no drift at all, and as $M \to \infty$ (a large, well-covered name) the discount vanishes.

**Why the discount peaks late.** The constant-capital solution has its largest discount at $t_0$, which contradicts Ben Dor's "about three months after." The fix is to recognize that constrained holders do not receive all their shares on the effective date. Distributions settle over weeks, claims are allowed in batches, a when-issued market may trade before the shares are delivered (Moyer p.370), and lock-ups expire on a ladder. Think of the cold open's eleven vehicles: some got their shares on day one, some are waiting for the claims agent, and some cannot sell until a lock-up rolls off. So there are two taps on the bathtub. One pours shares *into* constrained hands at a rate that starts high and fades, with a mean lag of $1/\alpha$; the other drains them out at rate $\theta$. Early on, the inflow beats the drain and the overhang grows; later, the drain wins and it shrinks. The overhang, and with constant capital the discount, peaks at the moment the two taps run at the same speed, and that moment is always *after* the event, which is the second fact in Ben Dor's paragraph. For the cold open's parameters ($\theta = 3.4$ and $\alpha = 5$ per year) the peak lands at $s^{*} = \ln(\theta/\alpha)/(\theta-\alpha) \approx 0.24$ years, about 2.9 months. Its timing tells you the ratio of two speeds that you can estimate separately: how fast shares are delivered (from the claims agent's distribution notices) and how fast constrained holders sell (from volume and holdings filings). Figure 16.1 plots both cases for the cold open's parameters.

![Figure 16.1: When shares reach constrained holders over weeks, the liquidity discount troughs about 2.9 months after the event rather than on day zero, so buying the open means buying before the worst price. Illustrative: the §16.2.2 two-compartment model with θ = 3.4 per year, α = 5 per year and κQ₀/M = 15%.](assets/figures/ch16_overhang_delivery_lag.png)

Kaz, reading over Theo's shoulder, says this is "just a two-compartment pharmacokinetic model, the kind used for a drug dose moving from the gut into the bloodstream." Kaz is right, and we will let Kaz have this one, because the analogy is exact: the plan is the dose, the CLO is the gut, the market is the bloodstream, and the alpha is the side effect.

The practical consequence is the one in the figure's caption. A desk that buys the open is buying before the worst price, and a desk that waits for the trough needs to know roughly when the trough comes. That is a question about delivery mechanics, not valuation, and it is answerable from documents.

#### The Full Derivation

*The drift.* Differentiate the ratio with the quotient rule:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{Q_t}{M_t}\right) = \frac{\dot Q_t}{M_t} - \frac{Q_t\,\dot M_t}{M_t^{2}} = -\theta\,\frac{Q_t}{M_t} - \frac{Q_t\,\dot M_t}{M_t^{2}}.$$
Since $v_t$ is a martingale, $\tfrac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}_{t_0}[v_t] = 0$, and the deterministic discount is all that remains. Multiplying by $-\kappa$ gives the drift $\mu_t$ stated in the main text.

*The two-compartment solution.* Let shares flow *into* constrained hands at rate $Q_0\,\alpha e^{-\alpha (t-t_0)}$, so that a total of $Q_0$ arrives with mean lag $1/\alpha$, while those holders keep selling at rate $\theta$. Writing $s = t - t_0$ and starting from $Q = 0$:
$$\dot Q_s = Q_0\,\alpha\,e^{-\alpha s} - \theta\,Q_s \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Q_s = \frac{Q_0\,\alpha}{\theta - \alpha}\left(e^{-\alpha s} - e^{-\theta s}\right), \qquad \theta \neq \alpha .$$
(Verify by substitution: $\dot Q_s + \theta Q_s = \frac{Q_0\alpha}{\theta-\alpha}\left[(\theta - \alpha)e^{-\alpha s}\right] = Q_0\alpha e^{-\alpha s}$.)

*The peak.* The overhang, and with constant capital the discount, peaks where inflow equals outflow, $\dot Q_s = 0$:
$$-\alpha e^{-\alpha s^{*}} + \theta e^{-\theta s^{*}} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s^{*} = \frac{\ln(\theta/\alpha)}{\theta - \alpha},$$
with the limit $s^{*} = 1/\theta$ when $\theta = \alpha$. The peak is always *after* the event, which is the second fact in Ben Dor's paragraph, and its timing tells you the ratio of two speeds that you can estimate separately: how fast shares are delivered (from the claims agent's distribution notices) and how fast constrained holders sell (from volume and holdings filings).

### 16.2.3 Fitting the execution rate to published aggregates

We can calibrate $\theta$ without a single post-reorg trade, by borrowing the fallen-angel reversal. Dynkin p.158 reports the reversal for the worst-hit bucket of downgraded bonds over three successive six-month windows after formation: +5.75%, +1.04% and +0.86%. Under the single-exponential model with constant capital, the reversal earned in window $k$ (each of length $\Delta = 0.5$ years) is the drop in the discount over that window:
$$w_k = A\,e^{-\theta(k-1)\Delta}\left(1 - e^{-\theta\Delta}\right), \qquad A = \frac{\kappa Q_0}{M}.$$
The ratio of consecutive windows depends only on $\theta$:
$$\frac{w_2}{w_1} = e^{-\theta\Delta} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\theta = -\frac{1}{\Delta}\ln\frac{w_2}{w_1} = -2\ln\frac{1.04}{5.75} \approx 3.42 \text{ per year},$$
a half-life of $\ln 2/3.42 \approx 0.20$ years, or about 2.4 months, and an implied total reversal $\hat A = 5.75/(1 - e^{-1.71}) \approx 7.0\%$ *(illustrative fit to published aggregates)*. The model then predicts a third window of $w_3 = w_2\,e^{-\theta\Delta} \approx 0.19\%$. The data say 0.86%. The single exponential is wrong in the tail, and it is wrong in exactly the direction Ben Dor's "one to two years" warned about: there is a second, slower component. In our model that component already exists. It is the capital-arrival term $Q_t\dot M_t/M_t^2$, which decays at the speed of the slow money ($\rho$), not the speed of the sellers ($\theta$). Fitting $(\theta, \rho)$ jointly needs more than three numbers, which is why Exercise 2 hands you a panel instead.

> **Math, Gently.** *Logs turn ratios into differences.* The fit above looks like a trick, so here is why it works. We do not know $A$, the size of the total reversal, and we do not need to, because it appears in both windows and cancels when we divide one by the other. What is left, $e^{-\theta\Delta}$, has the unknown $\theta$ stuck in an exponent, and the logarithm is the tool that brings exponents down to earth: $\ln(e^{x}) = x$, and more generally $\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b$, so a ratio of two numbers becomes a difference of their logs. Tiny number: the second window over the first is $1.04/5.75 = 0.181$. Its natural log is $-1.71$. That $-1.71$ must equal $-\theta \times 0.5$, so $\theta = 1.71/0.5 = 3.42$ per year. Two windows pin the speed without knowing the size, the same way two photographs of a draining bathtub, taken a known time apart, tell you the drain rate without telling you how big the tub was. The catch is that the trick assumes one drain; the third window's 0.86% is the tub telling you there are two.

Ines, who has been listening with the expression of someone watching a stranger misplay a hand, translates: "The fast decay is the forced seller folding. The slow decay is the table filling up with players who could have sat down on day one but didn't know the game was running. You get paid for the first by being early. You get paid for the second by being *patient.* Different edges, different capital."

### 16.2.4 From model to signal: momentum with an expiry date

The signal the desk actually trades is the model's expected excess drift, estimated name by name:
$$\hat\mu_t = \hat\kappa\,\hat\theta\,\frac{\hat Q_t}{\hat M_t}, \qquad \hat Q_t = \hat Q_{t_0}\,\big(1 - \hat x_t\big),$$
where $\hat Q_{t_0}$ is the constrained holders' allocation from the plan's distribution schedule (cross-referenced against the ad hoc group's Rule 2019 statements and CLO trustee reports, which list loan positions by holder), $\hat x_t \in [0,1]$ is the fraction already executed (from 13D/13G amendments, Form 4 filings for 10% holders, quarterly 13F changes, and volume above a peer-implied baseline), and $\hat M_t$ is a capital proxy (13F holder count, average daily volume, borrow availability).

This is time-series momentum, the subject of Chapter 10, with two differences that make it better than ordinary momentum. First, the *sign* is known before the first return prints: as long as $\hat Q_t > 0$, the expected drift is positive. Second, the signal carries its own **expiry date**. When the constrained holders' filings show them out, $\hat Q_t \approx 0$ and the drift is zero by construction. At that point the desk is no longer trading a flow; it is long an uncovered small-cap, and it should either underwrite the business properly or leave. Ordinary trend-followers find out that a trend has ended by losing money. This one tells you in a 13G amendment.

> **Quant-shop life: the holder registry that did not tie.** *(A fictional composite of a systematic credit asset manager; no real firm or person is depicted.)* Monday, 8:40 a.m., a mid-sized systematic credit fund in a converted office park. Dana, a second-year data analyst, has one job this week: reconcile the "executed fraction" feature, the $\hat x_t$ above, for the forty post-reorg names in the research universe before the quarterly model review on Thursday. The feature is supposed to be simple. For each name, take the constrained holders' allocations from the distribution schedule, subtract what their filings say they still own, and divide.
>
> By ten she has thirty-one names that tie within a rounding error and nine that do not. By lunch she is down to one that matters. The fund's 13F parser says a CLO manager still holds 1.9mm shares of a reorganized freight broker. The CLO trustee reports, which the fund buys monthly, say the vehicles sold the last of their stock in June. One of these is wrong, and the feature for the name is either 0.31 or 1.00 depending on which.
>
> The afternoon is archaeology. The 13F is not wrong, exactly; it is old. Holdings reports are filed up to 45 days after quarter end, and the fund's vendor loaded this one late, so the "current" position is a snapshot of 31 March. Worse, the stock traded over the counter until May, and an OTC stock that is not on the SEC's list of 13(f) securities need not appear on a 13F at all, so the 1.9mm shares may be a manual entry the manager chose to include. The trustee reports have their own problem: they identify the position by the issuer's *pre-bankruptcy* name and an internal asset identifier, not the new CUSIP, because the trustee's system was set up when the asset was a term loan. Dana's join on CUSIP had silently dropped every row.
>
> Tuesday and Wednesday go to an entity-resolution table: old legal name, new legal name, old loan identifiers, new equity CUSIP, ticker, the dates each became valid. It is four hundred rows of typing and checking against the plan's distribution notices, the kind of work a model-review note will one day call "manual and error prone." She opens a ticket with the data vendor asking for an issuer-history mapping for reorganized companies. The ticket is acknowledged, assigned, and, as of the time of writing, still open.
>
> On Thursday the model review takes eleven minutes. The head of research asks why the freight broker's signal changed since last quarter. Dana explains, and then says the part that makes everyone in the room put down their coffee: the signal for that name was already zero. The trustee reports were right; the CLO vehicles had sold in June; $\hat Q_t$ had been near zero for two months, and the model had stopped trading the name on its own, because the volume had fallen back to baseline. The reconciliation changed nothing in the portfolio. It changed what the fund *knew* about why. That is the point, and it is not a small one. The expiry date in this section is only as good as the holder registry behind it, and the registry is a thing someone builds by hand, on a Tuesday, in a spreadsheet nobody will ever thank them for.

> **Ways This Blows Up.**
> 1. *Using the plan value as $v_t$.* Plan values are biased low by the controlling creditors (Moyer p.355, p.369), so the stock can open *above* the plan value with a large overhang, the measured "discount" is negative, and the fit explodes. Anchor $v_t$ to the when-issued price or a peer-multiple band, never to the disclosure statement.
> 2. *Stale OTC prints.* Equal-weighted portfolios of illiquid stocks inherit bid-ask bounce as fake alpha (Blume and Stambaugh's classic bias). Jiang, Wang & Yang's surviving alpha is concentrated in OTC names, partly for this reason. Use quote midpoints and skip-a-day returns.
> 3. *Survivorship.* Spirit Airlines' first post-reorg equity went from emergence to a second Chapter 11 in under six months (§16.7). A backtest that drops delisted names will report a beautiful drift.
> 4. *Fundamental news in the residual.* The first earnings release after emergence moves the stock more than the overhang does. Strip peer and factor returns first (Chapter 8's IDO step) and exclude event windows. IDO or it didn't happen.
> 5. *$\theta$ is not a constant.* Sellers accelerate into liquidity. Dynkin p.140 reports volumes about four times normal with *wider* spreads around the fallen-angel event; a seller who sees bids will hit them. Let $\theta$ depend on depth, or at least refit it after any volume spike.

> **Trend Watch.** *CLO documents got patient with debt, not with equity, 2020–2026.*
> - **Before 2020.** Most broadly syndicated CLO indentures gave managers little room to hold or fund anything that was not a conforming loan. Law-firm guidance from the 2020 downturn (O'Melveny, *CLO Issues in Workouts and Debt Restructurings*, 2020; Seward & Kissel client notes) describes severe restrictions on acquiring and holding equity, and general prohibitions on providing new money in exchange for equity or other non-conforming assets.
> - **2021–2022.** S&P Global Ratings described the spread of "loss mitigation" and workout-loan language (a video briefing of 10 Mar 2021, and *Par Wars: U.S. CLO Document Provisions Evolve To Provide Managers More Flexibility*, 23 Sep 2022): workout loans that broadly meet the collateral-obligation definition are typically carried as defaulted obligations, while restructured assets further from that definition are carried at zero. Industry tallies reported a sharp rise in the share of new European CLOs with such language between 2020 and 2021 *(approx.; secondary summaries, primary source not verified)*.
> - **2023–2024.** S&P published a Credit FAQ on uptier priming and drop-down provisions in US CLOs (26 Jul 2023), and Weil's June 2024 note on CLOs as restructuring creditors observed that managers generally want to avoid a restructured asset being treated as equity, because equity may be discounted entirely in the OC test or, if it qualifies as a loss-mitigation loan, receive punitive treatment.
> - **What it means for the desk.** The forced-seller channel is contractual, not regulatory, and the contracts now distinguish sharply between restructured debt (holdable) and equity (not). Plans respond by giving CLOs take-back term loans wherever the business can carry them. The equity overhang that survives is therefore concentrated in cases where the fulcrum was a CLO-heavy loan *and* the debt capacity was thin: precisely the cases you can identify from the plan's capital structure before the effective date.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**The Spec You Hand the Robot.** *Vague version:* "Buy post-reorg stocks where CLOs have to sell." *What ROBO built:* a strategy that buys at the open on the effective date, the moment the overhang is largest and before a single share has been delivered, and then holds forever, because nobody told it when "have to sell" stops. *Precise version:* build `ForcedSellerOverhang`, a deterministic model of overhang $Q_s$, capital $M_s$, discount $-\kappa Q_s/M_s$ and drift $\mu_s$ on $s = t - t_0 \ge 0$ in years; support both instantaneous release and exponential delivery at rate $\alpha$; fit $\theta$ from window reversals and from a panel of IDO-stripped residuals; emit a signal that is exactly zero once the constrained holders have executed.

*Input schema (holder registry, one row per holder per instrument per as-of date):* `instrument_id` (internal string; never a guessed CUSIP), `holder_id`, `holder_type` in {`CLO`, `bank`, `insurer`, `hedge_fund`, `sponsor`, `retail`, `other`}, `shares` (float, millions), `source` in {`plan_schedule`, `rule_2019`, `13D`, `13G`, `form_4`, `13F`, `trustee_report`}, `as_of` (date), `lockup_end` (date or null). *Panel shapes:* time grid `s` of shape `(n_days,)` on a 252-day year; residual panel of shape `(n_names, n_days)`. *Edge cases:* $\theta = \alpha$ (use the limit formula), $M_0 \le 0$ (reject), zero overhang (signal zero, no division), negative mean residuals near zero in the log fit (mask them).

The spec above reads like a finished thought, which is exactly when it is most dangerous. The model has seven parameters, a closed form that changes shape when two speeds happen to coincide, and a fitting step that must recover an execution rate from a panel of noisy residuals nobody has seen yet. Each of those is a place where a sign error can sit quietly for a quarter. A desk that rebuilds this in a spreadsheet for every new emergence will find the bug in the P&L, which is the most expensive place to find anything. So the model goes into code, and the code arrives with its own proof obligations.

The listing has five layers. First, `ForcedSellerOverhang` holds the parameters of §16.2.2 and refuses nonsense at construction: negative overhang, price impact or capital speed, or a non-positive execution rate, capital or delivery rate, raises an error rather than drawing a plausible curve. Its methods return the overhang (single exponential when `alpha` is `None`, the two-compartment formula otherwise, with the $\theta = \alpha$ limit handled separately), the capital path, the log-price discount $-\kappa Q_s/M_s$, and the drift by the quotient rule, so slow capital is included. `half_life` and `peak_time` are the closed forms. Second, `theta_from_windows` is the §16.2.3 calculation, and it raises unless the first reversal exceeds the second and both are positive. Third, `simulate_residual_panel` builds what an IDO-stripped panel would look like (the model's discount plus independent daily noise for 200 names), and `fit_theta_loglinear` recovers $\theta$ by regressing the log of the negative cross-sectional mean on time over the first half-year. Fourth, `overhang_signal` computes $\hat\kappa\hat\theta\hat Q_{\text{rem}}/\hat M$ with remaining overhang clipped at zero, so a fully executed holder base gives a signal of exactly zero. Fifth, the assertions.

The assertions are the reason to prefer this to a spreadsheet, which will cheerfully compute a half-life from the wrong sign and say nothing. The calibration must reproduce 3.42 per year and the 2.4-month half-life, and must show the single exponential undershooting the observed third window, which is the model admitting it lacks a slow component. The drift, integrated with the listing's own `trap`, must equal the change in the discount. The numerically located trough must land on `peak_time`. Slow capital must add drift, and the simulated panel must return $\theta$ and the starting discount within ten percent.

Use it when you have a defensible allocation and a capital proxy and want an expiry-dated drift per name. Do not read it as a price forecast: it is a deterministic expected discount under $\mathbb{P}$ with fundamentals held as a martingale, and `overhang_signal` drops the delivery schedule and the capital-arrival term. The edge case that bites is the fit's `floor`. When the true discount in the window is close to the noise, the mean residual flips sign at random and its log is undefined, so the code keeps only days whose mean deviation is below the negative floor. That is a selection on the outcome, and it biases the slope flatter, meaning a slower $\theta$, if the window is long or the discount is small. Keep `max_years` short.

## 16.3 Rights Offerings and Backstops: Pricing the Put You Sold

### 16.3.1 Desk framing: the backstop is the player who agrees to call any bet

Start with an insurance company that almost never pays a claim. Three scholars, Vincent Buccola of the University of Chicago Law School with Adi Marcovich Gross and Matthew McBrady, went through 49 Chapter 11 cases since 2016 in which a group of creditors had agreed in advance to buy whatever part of a rights offering the rest of their class did not take. That promise is the backstop, and its whole justification is risk: the backstop party is said to be paid for standing ready to buy shares that might turn out to be worth less than the price. So the authors asked the obvious question. How often did that risk actually bite? In 19 of the deals there was an arm's-length estimate of what the securities were really worth, a market price or a later transaction, against which to check the exercise price. In 18 of those 19, the securities sold were worth more than the price paid for them, on average about twice as much (Buccola, Marcovich Gross & McBrady, *The Backstop Party*, *American Bankruptcy Law Journal* 100(1), 2026). The insurance policy paid out once in nineteen tries, and the people selling it were paid as if it paid out often. The rest of this section is about why that happens, how to price the policy, and how to tell when the premium is a fair price for risk and when it is a toll for being in the room.

A rights offering is a capital raise in which a class of creditors is offered new shares at a price set off the plan equity value, which (see §16.1) is a number the people setting it had every reason to set low. A backstop is a commitment, signed weeks or months before the offering funds, to buy every share the class does not take. The backstop party is paid a fee for this, usually in shares.

"Translate," says Ines, when Theo explains this. Then she does it herself. "The backstop is the player who agrees in advance to call any bet. Nobody does that for free. You do it if you're paid a fee, if you get to buy chips at a discount, and if you get to see everybody's cards before they decide whether to play. In a rights offering you get all three. That's why the seat is so crowded and the door is so narrow."

The reason the instrument exists at all is legal, and Moyer states it precisely.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.362:* "Thus, an unsecured creditor is extremely vulnerable when the debtor's circumstances are such that exit financing cannot be obtained in an amount sufficient to pay off all administrative claims; frequently this is a reason why a rights offering to raise new cash from creditors is included in plans of reorganization."
>
> **Annotation.** Administrative claims must be paid in full in cash on the effective date unless the holder agrees otherwise, so a plan that cannot raise enough exit cash cannot be confirmed. The rights offering is the plan's way of making its own creditors the lender of last resort. Moyer frames it as protection for unsecured creditors; the modern data (Trend Watch below) show that in practice it has become the dominant form of exit equity and a large transfer *within* a class, from the members who are not in the backstop group to the members who are. The desk's departure from Moyer is to stop asking "does the plan need the cash?" and to start asking "what is the put worth, and who got to sell it?"

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Houlihan_Lokey_Distressed_MA p.24:* "A backstopped rights offering maintains fairness for all existing shareholders and assures that the needed capital is raised, but, in turn, requires extra consideration for the "backstop" investor."
>
> **Annotation.** "Extra consideration" is the polite phrase for an option premium. Houlihan's example is a family-owned company with a well-capitalized insider as backstop, and its "fairness" claim holds when every holder can participate on equal terms. The 2025 *ConvergeOne* decision (Trend Watch below) is the legal system discovering what the derivation below shows mathematically: when only *some* members of a class are allowed to sell the put and collect the premium, the offering is not fair to the members who are left out, however equal the rights themselves look on paper.

#### When the put view applies, and when it breaks

The model below treats the backstop as a short put and asks what fee would compensate for it. That is the right lens when the commitment window is short, a few months from signing to funding, and when the reorganized equity has some tradable proxy, a when-issued market, listed peers, or the company's own bonds, from which a volatility can be borrowed with a straight face. Under those conditions the put has a meaningful value and the fee can be compared with it.

The lens breaks when the discount is so deep that the put is nearly worthless. If shares are offered at half of plan value, the equity has to lose half its value before the backstop party loses a dollar, and the fair fee for the put rounds to zero, yet the backstop party is still paid. In that case the economics are not in the put at all. They are in the reserved allocation (a slice of the offering set aside for the backstop group at the discounted price) and in exclusivity (the right to take up whatever others leave unexercised). Pricing the fee as insurance in such a deal is like pricing a lottery syndicate by the cost of the ticket. The money is in who gets to join.

So the first thing a seasoned practitioner checks is not the volatility. It is whether *the whole class was offered the seat*. Since 25 Sep 2025 that question has carried legal as well as economic weight. In *ConvergeOne*, Judge Hanen of the Southern District of Texas reversed confirmation of a prepackaged plan insofar as it gave a subset of first-lien lenders the exclusive right to backstop a \$245mm rights offering for a 10% premium, holding on §1123(a)(4) grounds that similarly situated members of the class had been treated unequally; a year earlier the same court had refused to dismiss the excluded lenders' appeal as equitably moot. The debtors noticed an appeal to the Fifth Circuit on 22 Oct 2025, and we have not been able to confirm a ruling as of this writing, so treat the appellate outcome as open. For the desk the point is narrower and more durable: in an exclusive backstop with no market test, the fee is no longer just an option premium. It is an option premium with a reversal risk attached, and the reversal risk is priced by forum, not by volatility.

### 16.3.2 Derivation: the right is a call, the backstop is a put

**Setup.** Let $n_0$ be the plan shares distributed before the offering (to creditors and direct investors, excluding fee shares), $m$ the number of rights-offering shares, and $K$ the subscription price, so the offering raises $mK$. Let $E$ be the pre-money value of the reorganized equity at the funding date, random as of the commitment date, and write the per-share pre-money value as $x \equiv E/n_0$. After the offering the company holds $E + mK$ of value across $n_0 + m$ shares:
$$S^{\text{post}} = \frac{E + mK}{n_0 + m} = \frac{n_0 x + mK}{n_0 + m}.$$
Subtracting $K$ from both sides gives the identity that drives everything:
$$S^{\text{post}} - K = \frac{n_0\,(x - K)}{n_0 + m}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad S^{\text{post}} > K \iff x > K.$$

**The right.** A rational holder subscribes if and only if $x > K$. Each eligible plan share carries $m/n_0$ rights, so the value of the rights attached to one plan share at funding is
$$\text{Right}(x) = \frac{m}{n_0}\left(S^{\text{post}} - K\right)^{+} = \frac{m}{n_0 + m}\,(x - K)^{+},$$
a call on the *pre-money* per-share value, struck at the subscription price, scaled by a single dilution factor. (The "theoretical ex-rights price" of equity-capital-markets folklore is just $S^{\text{post}}$ evaluated at today's $x$.)

**The backstop.** If $x < K$, nobody subscribes, and the backstop party must buy all $m$ shares at $K$, each worth $S^{\text{post}}$. Its loss is $m(K - S^{\text{post}}) = \frac{m n_0}{n_0+m}(K - x)$. If $x > K$, everybody subscribes and the backstop buys nothing. So the backstop's payoff is a short put:
$$\Pi^{\text{BS}}(x) = -N_{\text{eff}}\,(K - x)^{+}, \qquad N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{m\,n_0}{n_0 + m}.$$
Let $\text{Put}(x_0, K; \sigma, T)$ be the value of a put on $x$ struck at $K$, with $\sigma$ the volatility of the reorganized equity and $T$ the time from commitment to funding. The fair fee, expressed as a fraction of the capital raised, sets the fee equal to the put:
$$f^{*}\,mK = N_{\text{eff}}\,\text{Put}(x_0, K;\sigma,T) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f^{*} = \frac{n_0}{n_0+m}\cdot\frac{\text{Put}(x_0, K;\sigma,T)}{K}.$$
Because no liquid option exists on a company that has not emerged yet, we evaluate the put with the Black–Scholes formula under the assumption that $x$ is a driftless lognormal under $\mathbb{P}$ over the short commitment window (so the "$\mathbb{Q}$" here is nominal: zero rate, zero drift). Kaz asks why we don't just use the risk-neutral measure. Theo asks Kaz to name the traded instrument that spans it. Kaz goes quiet.

> **Math, Gently.** *What a put is, and what "risk-neutral" means.* Ines, who played cash games for a living, describes a put as the insurance some card rooms used to sell on an all-in hand: you pay a premium now, and if your hand loses, the insurer pays you the shortfall. A put on $x$ struck at $K$ pays $K - x$ if $x$ ends below $K$ and nothing otherwise, so the backstop party, which must buy shares at $K$ when they are worth only $x$, has *written* that insurance. Two things make the premium larger: more time before the cards are turned (a longer window $T$), and a wilder deck (a higher volatility $\sigma$), because both widen the range of places $x$ can land. Tiny number, using the worked example below: with $x_0 = 1$, $K = 0.75$, $\sigma = 0.70$ and $T = 0.5$, the model says $x$ finishes below 0.75 a bit more than a third of the time (about 37%), and when it does, the average shortfall is about 0.20. Multiply the two and you get the put's value, about 0.0734. Now the honest part. In textbooks a put is priced "risk-neutrally," which sounds like an assumption about attitudes and is really a statement about hedging: if you can trade the underlying stock continuously, you can build the put's payoff out of stock and cash, and the price of the put is simply the cost of that recipe, whatever anyone's appetite for risk. Nobody can hedge with a stock that has not emerged yet. So the zero-drift lognormal we use here is a *convention*, a reasonable way to turn a volatility guess into a number, and not a measure that any trade enforces. That is why Theo stops Kaz: the formula is the same, but the authority behind it is not.

**Worked number.** Put $x_0 = 1$, a rights price at a 25% discount to fair value, $K = 0.75$ (the average plan-value discount Buccola, Marcovich Gross & McBrady report; Trend Watch below), $\sigma = 0.70$ and $T = 0.5$. The Black–Scholes put is then worth about 0.0734 per unit of pre-money value, which is $\text{Put}/K \approx 9.8\%$ of the subscription price (the $d_1$, $d_2$ arithmetic is in *The Full Derivation* at the end of this subsection). Before reading the next sentence, notice what that 9.8% is: the price of insuring a buyer against a fall of more than a quarter in six months, for a stock as volatile as a newly reorganized company. With Hertz's dilution factor, $n_0/(n_0 + m) = 291.25/454.75 \approx 0.64$ (share counts in §16.3.4), the fair fee is $f^{*} \approx 0.64 \times 9.8\% \approx 6.3\%$ *(illustrative)*. That is almost exactly the 6% average fee that Seth reports for hedge-fund backstops. On this metric the market prices the backstop like a Black–Scholes put at 70% volatility.

Now vary the two inputs nobody negotiates in public:

| Discount to fair value | Fair fee, $T = 0.25$ y | Fair fee, $T = 0.50$ y |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0% ($K = x_0$) | ≈ 8.9% | ≈ 12.5% |
| 25% ($K = 0.75\,x_0$) | ≈ 3.0% | ≈ 6.3% |
| 50% ($K = 0.50\,x_0$) | ≈ 0.3% | ≈ 1.6% |

*(Illustrative: $\sigma = 0.70$, dilution factor 0.64, zero rate; the code below reproduces the table.)* The table makes the chapter's central point about backstops. **The fee and the discount are substitutes.** A deep discount makes the put nearly worthless, because the equity would have to fall by half before the backstop lost a dollar. A backstop party that receives a 50% discount *and* a 10% fee is being paid twice for one risk, and the second payment is the larger one. Figure 16.2 draws the two payoffs and the fee curve behind the table.

![Figure 16.2: The rights are a call and the backstop is a short put (left), and the fair fee for selling that put falls from 12.5% of the raise at no discount to 1.6% at a 50% discount for a six-month window (right). **The all-in transfer.** The backstop party's take has four legs, not one:
$$\Pi^{\text{BP}} = \underbrace{f\,m\,S^{\text{post}}}_{\text{fee, paid in shares}} \;+\; \underbrace{a\,m\,(S^{\text{post}} - K)}_{\text{reserved allocation at a discount}} \;+\; \underbrace{(1-a)\,\eta\,m\,(S^{\text{post}} - K)^{+}}_{\text{exclusivity option}} \;-\; \underbrace{N_{\text{eff}}\,(K - x)^{+}}_{\text{the put}},$$
where $a$ is the fraction of the offering reserved for the backstop parties outright and $\eta$ is the fraction of in-the-money rights that other holders fail to exercise (ineligible holders, holders without cash, retail holders who never read the notice). Three things in this expression are routinely mispriced. The fee is paid in *shares*, so it is worth $f\,m\,S^{\text{post}}$, not $f\,m\,K$: when the discount is real, the fee is worth more than its headline. The reserved allocation is a pure transfer whenever $x_0 > K$. And the exclusivity option, the right to absorb whatever others leave on the table, is a free call. (We have ignored the small extra dilution from the fee shares themselves, which the code handles by valuing fee shares at the post-money price.)

#### The Full Derivation

*The Black–Scholes put in the worked number.* With zero rate and zero drift, the put on $x$ struck at $K$ is $\text{Put} = K\,\Phi(-d_2) - x_0\,\Phi(-d_1)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function. For $x_0 = 1$, $K = 0.75$, $\sigma = 0.70$ and $T = 0.5$:
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(1/0.75) + \tfrac12(0.49)(0.5)}{0.70\sqrt{0.5}} = \frac{0.2877 + 0.1225}{0.4950} \approx 0.829, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - 0.4950 \approx 0.334,$$
$$\text{Put} = K\,\Phi(-d_2) - x_0\,\Phi(-d_1) \approx 0.75(0.3693) - 0.2036 \approx 0.0734, \qquad \frac{\text{Put}}{K} \approx 9.8\%.$$
The term $\Phi(-d_2) \approx 0.3693$ is the model's probability that $x$ finishes below $K$, which is where the Math, Gently box's "about 37%" comes from, and dividing the put by it gives the average shortfall of about 0.20. Multiplying $\text{Put}/K$ by the dilution factor $n_0/(n_0+m) \approx 0.64$ gives the 6.3% fair fee in the main text.

> **Trend Watch.** *Rights offerings took over exit financing, and the courts noticed, 2003–2026.*
> - **Prevalence.** Gunjan Seth (USC Marshall), *Do Rights Offerings Reduce Bargaining Complexity in Chapter 11?* (CLS Blue Sky Blog, 11 Mar 2025): rights offerings financed about 35% of large Chapter 11 cases over 2003–2021 on an asset-weighted basis, rising to about 87% by 2019, with roughly \$46bn raised; hedge-fund backstoppers charge about 6% of the amount raised, buy about 43% of the equity, and appoint about 40% of the board; participants earn about 50% within three months; instrumental-variable estimates suggest rights offerings raise total creditor recoveries by about 40% and shorten cases by about seven months. (The paper's NBER abstract, February 2024, gives 47% of bankruptcies on a different basis.)
> - **The transfer.** Buccola, Marcovich Gross & McBrady, *The Backstop Party*, *American Bankruptcy Law Journal* 100(1) (2026): 49 confirmed plans with backstopped rights offerings, January 2016 to June 2024. Average discount to plan value 25%, about 50% effective; all-in backstop compensation 20.1% of the amount raised (25.1% in cases with market-priced values), against 3–7% underwriting spreads for IPOs and seasoned offerings. Per dollar of *identical* claim, backstop parties came away with about 21 cents of value against about 8 cents for other holders measured at plan values, and about 35 cents against 19 cents measured at market values.
> - **The legal regime shift.** The Fifth Circuit's *Serta* decision (31 Dec 2024) held that an uptier indemnity for some lenders violated the equal-treatment rule of §1123(a)(4), looking at a plan's actual effect rather than its form. ### 16.3.3 Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality

> **Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality.** *Myth:* "The backstop fee is the price of the backstop." *Reality:* The fee is the smallest of four legs. In a 25%-discount offering at 70% volatility the put is worth about 6% of the raise; the discount on the backstop party's reserved allocation and the exclusivity option are worth more, and the fee itself is paid in shares that are worth more than their face whenever the discount is real. If you are not in the backstop group, your rights are worth exactly what the derivation says. If you are, your seat is worth several times that.

### 16.3.4 Case Study: the Hertz rights offering, June 2021

The Hertz plan (§16.6 has the whole docket) raised \$1.635bn in a rights offering at \$10.00 per share, priced off an equity value of about \$4.7bn (Hertz 8-K/A, 14 May 2021). The offering was made first to Hertz's old stockholders, who could take either the rights *or* a package of 30-year warrants, but not both, and unexercised rights flowed to eligible holders of unsecured funded debt. The plan sponsors and other commitment parties backstopped it for a 10% premium paid in common stock at the same valuation. The rights expired on 15 Jun 2021. The share counts in the 8-K filed after the effective date (7 Jul 2021) are worth writing down exactly, because they let us audit every number:

| Item | Shares | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Rights shares to participants | 127,362,114 | Includes 7,858,805 to commitment parties |
| Unsubscribed shares to commitment parties | 36,137,887 | The put "exercised" against the backstop: the shares nobody else took |
| Backstop fee shares | 16,350,000 | $16.35/163.5 = 10.0\%$ of the offering, paid in stock at \$10 |
| Plan sponsors' direct purchase | 277,119,438 | \$2.781bn at the rights price |
| Old stockholders' 3% | 14,133,075 | |
| Total new common outstanding | 471,102,514 | The five rows sum exactly |

So $m = 127.36 + 36.14 = 163.50$mm shares (\$1.635bn at \$10), $n_0 = 277.12 + 14.13 = 291.25$mm, and the dilution factor in the derivation is $291.25/454.75 \approx 0.64$. Three observations a systematic desk would have made in real time:

1. **The put was short-dated.** The commitment-to-funding window ran from the May 2021 auction result to the 30 Jun 2021 effective date, about $T \approx 0.1$ to $0.15$ years. At $T = 0.1$ and 70% volatility, even an at-the-money put is worth only about 5.6% of the raise after dilution *(illustrative)*. A 10% fee was rich by the Black–Scholes yardstick before counting any discount.
2. **The unsubscribed block was large and in the money.** About 22% of the offering went unsubscribed, largely because old stockholders who preferred warrants could not also take rights. That is the exclusivity option in our formula, with a large $\eta$ supplied by the plan's own either-or election.
3. **Ex post, the seat was worth a fortune.** At the \$29 re-IPO price of 8 Nov 2021, the 36.14mm unsubscribed shares bought at \$10 carried a gain of about \$687mm, and the 16.35mm fee shares were worth about \$474mm, together about \$1.16bn on a \$1.635bn commitment *(approx.; derived from sourced share counts and prices, before any hedging or financing cost)*. Most of that is the stock's re-rating, not the put premium, which is exactly the point of the Buccola findings: the backstop seat is a levered long position in the plan's own discount.

Mags, who backstopped a rights offering in 2009 "with a fax machine and a prayer," reads the table twice. "Back in my day the backstop was the guy nobody else would lend to. Now it's the guy everybody wants to be. That's how you know the price is wrong."

Look at what we just did by hand. We audited a share table row by row, evaluated a Black–Scholes put with a pocket calculator, scaled it by a dilution factor, and compared the answer with a fee someone else negotiated. That is fine once, in a chapter. It is not fine on a Tuesday in the commitment window of an offering that closes in three weeks, when the fee, the discount and the funding date are all moving, the share count in the latest 8-K has a different footnote than the last one, and a portfolio manager wants to know whether the backstop is cheap before the rights expire. The desk automates this step because the arithmetic is simple and the ways to get it subtly wrong are many: the wrong share count in $n_0$, a price where a pre-money value belongs, a fee valued at cash when it is paid in stock.

The listing has three parts. First, `bs_call` and `bs_put` are a bare-bones Black–Scholes pair that fall back to intrinsic value when time or volatility is zero, so the same function works on the day the offering funds. Second, `RightsOffering` holds $n_0$, $m$ and $K$ and rejects any that are not positive. It exposes the dilution factor $n_0/(n_0+m)$, the effective share count $N_{\text{eff}}$, the post-money price, the value of the rights attached to one plan share, the backstop's payoff $-N_{\text{eff}}(K-x)^{+}$, and `fair_fee`, which is the put scaled by dilution and divided by $K$, expressed as a fraction of the raise. Third, a block of checks. The Hertz share table must sum exactly to 471.1mm, the fee must equal 10% of the offering in shares, put-call parity must hold, and the dilution factor must land near 0.64. A Monte Carlo of 400,000 driftless lognormal paths must match the closed-form short put within two percent. The fee table must show fee falling as the discount deepens, and the ex post Hertz arithmetic at \$29 must come to about \$1.16bn.

Use it to screen announced backstops against a model fee, and to see how much of a headline fee the discount has already paid. Do not use it to value the whole seat: it prices only the put, not the reserved allocation or the exclusivity option, and it assumes a driftless lognormal over a window that is short precisely because nothing trades on the reorganized equity yet. The edge case that bites is units. The argument `x` is the pre-money value per *plan* share, $E/n_0$, not the quoted post-money price, and `n0` must exclude the fee shares. Feed it the \$29 print as `x0` with a \$10 strike, or add the fee shares to $n_0$, and the fee looks absurdly cheap or absurdly rich with no error to warn you.

## 16.4 Warrants: Long-Dated Calls Handed to People Who Were Told They'd Get Nothing

### 16.4.1 Desk framing: the consolation prize is the most convex thing in the plan

Plans hand out warrants for the same reason game shows hand out consolation prizes: the losing contestant has to leave the stage quietly. The class that is out of the money at the plan value, often old equity and sometimes the unsecured creditors, receives a long-dated call on the reorganized company, struck at or above the plan value, in exchange for not objecting. The plan describes this as a premium to the firm. Moyer agrees, with an important qualifier.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.373:* "Warrants give the holder an economic interest in a firm, but also require the holder to pay cash upon exercise (i.e. the strike price of the warrant) to purchase a share of its stock. Investors presumably are better off because they generally would not exercise warrants unless the stock was higher than the exercise price. On the other hand, where the strike price is above the plan of reorganization's projected value, the firm is effectively, relative to the plan, receiving a premium."
>
> **Annotation.** The load-bearing words are "relative to the plan." A strike set above a plan value that was negotiated *down* (§16.1) may be at or below fair value. The desk therefore never measures warrant moneyness against the disclosure statement; it measures it against the price at which informed insiders are buying stock in the same plan, which in a rights-offering case is printed in the plan itself. Moyer adds on the same page that warrants are freely tradable when issued but generally less liquid than the stock, so a warrant quote is not a stock quote with a strike subtracted. The departure for a 2026 desk is that we can price the warrant properly (dilution, jump-to-default, a long-dated volatility) and use the plan's *own* warrant valuation to back out the volatility the plan's advisors assumed, which is a free read on how the other side of the table modeled the company.

### 16.4.2 Derivation: dilution, the fixed point, and the jump that Kaz gets backwards

**Dilution.** Let $n_0$ be shares outstanding, $n_w$ the number of warrants, each exercisable into one new share at strike $K$ at expiry $T$, and $\omega = n_w/n_0$ the dilution ratio. Let $S_t$ be the share price and $W_t$ the warrant price. The total market value of the equity claims, shares plus warrants, is $n_0 S_t + n_w W_t$. If the warrants are exercised at $T$, the company receives $n_w K$ in cash and has $n_0 + n_w$ shares, so each warrant holder receives
$$\frac{n_0 S_T + n_w W_T + n_w K}{n_0 + n_w} - K = \frac{n_0}{n_0 + n_w}\left(S_T + \omega W_T - K\right) = \frac{1}{1+\omega}\left(\hat S_T - K\right),$$
where $\hat S_t \equiv S_t + \omega W_t$ is the equity value per *existing* share including the warrants' claim. Exercise is rational iff $\hat S_T > K$. If $\hat S$ follows a lognormal with volatility $\sigma$, the warrant is $1/(1+\omega)$ calls on $\hat S$:
$$W = \frac{1}{1+\omega}\,C_{\text{BS}}\!\left(S + \omega W,\;K,\;T,\;\sigma,\;r\right).$$
This is a fixed point in $W$, the classic Galai–Schneller formulation. It is a contraction, since the derivative of the right-hand side in $W$ is $\omega\,\Delta/(1+\omega) < 1$ with $\Delta = \partial C_{\text{BS}}/\partial S \in (0,1)$, so iterating from $W^{(0)} = C_{\text{BS}}(S, K)$ converges. One more bound we will assert in code: because $C_{\text{BS}}$ is convex with slope below one, $C_{\text{BS}}(S + \omega W) \le C_{\text{BS}}(S) + \omega W$, which rearranges to $W \le C_{\text{BS}}(S)$. Dilution never makes a warrant worth more than the equivalent call, so backing out volatility while ignoring dilution always *understates* the volatility.

**The compound-option view.** In the Merton model of Chapter 6 the share is itself a call on firm value $V_t$ struck at debt face $F$. A plan warrant is therefore a call on a call, and it inherits the leverage-driven skew: as $V_t$ falls toward $F$, equity volatility $\sigma_E$ rises, and an out-of-the-money warrant is mostly a bet on the right tail of $V$. For a 30-year tenor, the warrant price is dominated by two things that are not delta: vega, and the probability that the company does not need a second Chapter 11 before expiry.

**The jump to zero.** Add a default event, at intensity $\lambda$ per year, at which the stock goes to zero (Chapter 6 notation; the warrant then expires worthless). To keep the stock price itself fairly priced in a nominal pricing measure that earns $r$, the pre-default stock must drift at $r + \lambda$: the survivors are compensated for the jump. Writing $\tilde S_T = S\,e^{(r+\lambda-\sigma^2/2)T + \sigma W_T}$ for the pre-default path (here $W_T$ is the Brownian motion, not the warrant):
$$C^{\text{JD}} = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf 1_{\{\tau > T\}}(\tilde S_T - K)^{+}\right] = e^{-rT}e^{-\lambda T}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\tilde S_T - K)^{+}\right] = C_{\text{BS}}(S, K, T, \sigma, r + \lambda),$$
because $e^{-(r+\lambda)T}\mathbb{E}[(\tilde S_T - K)^+]$ is exactly the Black–Scholes call at rate $r + \lambda$. Holding the observed share price and the diffusive volatility fixed, **adding a jump to zero makes the call worth more, not less**: the strike is paid only in survival states, and the stock must outperform while it survives.

Kaz's first draft multiplied the Black–Scholes warrant by $e^{-\lambda T}$. At $\lambda = 2\%$ for 30 years that is $e^{-0.6} \approx 0.55$, a 45% haircut, and Kaz presented it with the confidence of someone who has never been wrong in front of a restructuring banker. Ines put it gently: "The share price already knows the company can die. You charged the warrant holder for the funeral twice." The haircut is correct only if your spot is a *going-concern* value, such as a plan DCF that assumes survival; then the survival factor belongs. If your spot is a market price, it does not.

**What the plan assumed.** Hertz's plan valued the warrants issued to old stockholders at \$769mm (Third Circuit opinion, footnote 5), and the effective-date 8-K reports 89,049,029 warrants with a \$13.80 strike expiring 30 Jun 2051. That is \$8.64 per warrant. The rights offering priced the stock at \$10.00, and the plan valued the old holders' 3% at \$141mm, which is \$10 a share on 14.13mm shares. Taking $S = 10$, $K = 13.80$, $T = 30$, $r \approx 2\%$ *(assumption)* and $\omega = 89.05/471.10 \approx 0.19$, the volatility that reproduces \$8.64 is in the low 50s, both with and without dilution (the code below solves it; dilution adds a little). That is a perfectly reasonable long-run volatility for a levered rental-car company. Had the advisors instead used the \$13.80 implied by the \$6.5bn "warrant basis" as spot, the same \$8.64 would imply a volatility in the mid-20s, which no one who has watched a rental-car equity would sign. The plan's numbers are internally consistent only at the lower spot, which is the honest one. The \$769mm was not a gift. The gift was the \$29 print four months later.

### 16.4.3 Case Study: the Hertz warrants, from "worthless" to \$1.35bn of intrinsic value

In June 2020, while selling stock into a retail rally during its bankruptcy, Hertz's prospectus supplement warned of "a significant risk that the [Stockholders] will receive no recovery under the Chapter 11 [c]ases and that our common stock will be worthless" (quoted in the Third Circuit opinion, 10 Sep 2024). Twelve months later the plan sponsor auction turned on a different question: how much old equity would get, not whether. On 8 Nov 2021, selling stockholders priced 44.52mm shares at \$29.00 in a re-IPO, and the stock and warrants began trading on Nasdaq as HTZ and HTZWW the next day (Hertz press release, 8 Nov 2021).

At \$29 a \$13.80-strike warrant had \$15.20 of intrinsic value. Across 89.05mm warrants that is about \$1.35bn, roughly 1.76 times the plan's \$769mm estimate *(derived from sourced counts and prices)*. Averaged over all ≈156mm old shares, the package an old stockholder received was:

| Component | Per old share | Value at the \$29 re-IPO |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cash | \$1.53 | \$1.53 |
| New common (14.13mm ÷ 156mm ≈ 0.091 shares) | 0.091 × \$29 | ≈ \$2.63 |
| Warrants (89.05mm ÷ 156mm ≈ 0.571 warrants) | 0.571 × \$15.20 intrinsic | ≈ \$8.68 |
| **Total** | | **≈ \$12.8** |

*(Approx.; averaged across all old shares, warrants at intrinsic value only, before time value. Holders who elected rights instead of warrants received a different mix.)* The stock that its own prospectus said might be worthless delivered roughly \$12.8 of value per share 4.5 months after emergence. The systematic desk's lesson is not "buy meme stocks in bankruptcy." It is that the warrant's value was almost entirely *timing and volatility*: priced at a plan volatility in the low 50s with a spot the sponsors chose, and then marked at a spot the public market chose. Anyone long the warrant at the plan's mark was long a 30-year option at a reasonable volatility on a spot that was about to be re-rated by a factor of nearly three.

The warrants expire on 30 Jun 2051. As §16.6 shows, by October 2026 Hertz was back in the restructuring room, which makes "a 30-year warrant is mostly a bet on not needing Chapter 11 again before 2051" sound less like a modeling assumption and more like a weather forecast.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**The Spec You Hand the Robot.** *Vague version:* "Price the Hertz warrants." *What ROBO built:* a Black–Scholes call with the stock's 30-day realized volatility from the meme week of June 2020, which priced a 30-year option at roughly the value of the stock and confidently recommended selling the stock to buy the warrant. *Precise version:* implement `warrant_price` as the dilution fixed point with an optional jump-to-zero intensity entering as $r + \lambda$, raise if the iteration does not converge, and implement `implied_vol_warrant` by bracketing root-finding on $\sigma \in [0.01, 3.0]$. Inputs: `S` (USD per share, the spot you believe, labeled market or going-concern), `K` (USD), `T` (years), `r` (continuously compounded, stated), `omega` = warrants / shares outstanding (dimensionless, $\ge 0$), `lam` (per year, $\ge 0$). Guards: reject negative $\omega$; never apply an $e^{-\lambda T}$ haircut to a market spot.

A warrant whose value appears on both sides of its own pricing equation is the kind of object that a spreadsheet handles with a circular-reference warning and a prayer. Then the question that actually matters, what volatility did the plan's advisors assume, requires running that equation backwards, inside a root-finder, across four variants of the spot and the jump. This is the moment to stop doing it by hand, because the answer is only useful if you can re-run it on the next plan's warrants before the other side has finished its second coffee.

The listing leans on `bs_call` from the previous listing and does two jobs. `warrant_price` starts from the plain Black–Scholes call, feeds the answer back into the spot as $S + \omega W$, divides by $1 + \omega$, and repeats until two passes agree to $10^{-12}$. The contraction argument in the derivation guarantees this works, but the loop still raises an error after a thousand passes rather than returning a half-converged number. The jump intensity enters only by raising the discount rate to $r + \lambda$, which is the correct treatment of a market spot. `implied_vol_warrant` wraps that function in `brentq` over volatilities from 1% to 300%, and refuses to run unless the target price is bracketed.

The checks then replay Hertz. The plan's \$769mm over 89.05mm warrants gives about \$8.64 each, and the fixed point must satisfy its own equation and never exceed the plain call. Implied volatility must be at least as high with dilution as without, the jump variant must come in below the diluted one, and the \$13.80-basis spot must give an implausibly low volatility. Kaz's funeral is encoded as an ordering: jump price above no-jump price above haircut price. The last block reproduces the \$29 intrinsic value and the roughly \$12.8 per old share package.

Use it for any long-dated plan warrant where dilution is more than a few percent of the share count. Skip it for short-dated warrants on a deep-pocketed issuer, where the fixed point and the plain call agree to the cent. The edge case that bites is the bracket. At a 30-year tenor the price has a floor even at near-zero volatility, about \$2.43 in the Hertz setup, and a ceiling at the spot. A target below the floor, such as a quote carrying a heavy illiquidity discount, cannot be matched by any volatility, and the function correctly raises. Treat that error as information about the quote, not as a bug to widen away.

## 16.5 Claims: 503(b)(9) vs. General Unsecured, and the Denominator Problem

### 16.5.1 Desk framing: a claim is a zero-coupon bond with a random face, a random maturity, and a random number of siblings

A trade claim is the least glamorous instrument in this chapter and the one a former RSSG banker understands best. It pays no coupon (Moyer p.413, n.26, notes that trade claims do not accrue interest, which is also why hedging one with a short bond position costs carry), it matures on an effective date nobody can promise, and its recovery is a fraction whose *denominator*, the total pool of allowed claims in the class, is unknown for most of the case. Sellers are usually not investors at all.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.310:* "1. The claim is significantly less liquid and thus purchasers often insist on a discount to compensate for this illiquidity. 2. The claim holder may not have the financial wherewithal to wait until the completion of a bankruptcy case to realize full value of the claim and thus is a forced seller."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer lists five reasons a trade creditor sells below the trading level of similarly ranked bonds; the first two are illiquidity and the forced seller, and the others (preferring cash to stock, being a business rather than an investor, unfamiliarity with the process) are variations on the same theme. Notice that this is §16.2's overhang again, one level down the capital structure: the vendor who shipped goods on credit is the CLO of the claims market, constrained not by an indenture but by payroll. The modern desk departs from Moyer in two ways. First, it prices the discount as a sum of separable premia (allowance, timing, pool size, illiquidity) rather than as a single haircut. Second, the claims market now includes customer claims in crypto and fintech bankruptcies, where the seller is a retail depositor and the forced-selling motive is acute (Trend Watch below).

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.311:* "This "nonallowance" risk can be managed through a variety of mechanisms. The first is to only purchase claims that have been deemed allowed by the bankruptcy court or at least have been admitted by the debtor."
>
> **Annotation.** The parameter $p_A$ in the pricing formula below. Moyer immediately notes the cost of this safety: claims are often allowed late, sometimes after confirmation, so buying only allowed claims means buying after most of the discount has gone. The desk's version is a classifier: given the claim's documentation, its match to the debtor's schedules, its type, and the objection history of similar claims in the same case, estimate $p_A$. Transfers are noticed on the docket under Bankruptcy Rule 3001(e) (Moyer p.311), which makes the claims register and the transfer notices a public, machine-readable record of who is buying what.

Moyer also describes (p.269) the classic hedge: a trade claim and a senior unsecured bond of the same debtor are *pari passu*, so long claim / short bond isolates the claim's discount while neutralizing the enterprise-value risk they share. The desk keeps that trade and adds a sharper one, the 503(b)(9) pair.

### 16.5.2 The statute block: what decides the face value

The legal rules below are the entire source of the pool-size randomness. Each one is a truncation, a priority switch, or an estimation procedure.

- **§503(b)(9)** (added by the 2005 bankruptcy amendments, after Moyer was written) grants administrative-expense priority to "the value of any goods received by the debtor within 20 days before the date of commencement of a case under this title in which the goods have been sold to the debtor in the ordinary course of such debtor's business" (11 U.S.C. §503, Cornell LII).
- **§1129(a)(9)(A)** requires administrative claims, including 503(b)(9) claims, to be paid in full in cash on the effective date unless the holder agrees otherwise. That is what makes a 503(b)(9) claim a near-par instrument.
- **"Received" means physical possession.** *In re World Imports, Ltd.*, 862 F.3d 338 (3d Cir. 2017): goods are received when the debtor or its agent takes physical possession; delivery to a common carrier with risk of loss passing at the shipping point does not count. Bankruptcy courts in Delaware have since denied 503(b)(9) status to drop-shipped goods (*In re SRC Liquidation*). The vendor who shipped on day 22 by boat and the vendor who delivered on day 19 by truck hold different instruments.
- **§502(b)(2)** disallows claims "for unmatured interest": no post-petition coupon accrues on a general unsecured claim, with the solvent-debtor exception that §16.6 shows Hertz paying for.
- **§502(b)(6)** caps a landlord's lease-rejection damages at "the rent reserved by such lease, without acceleration, for the greater of one year, or 15 percent, not to exceed three years, of the remaining term," plus unpaid rent (11 U.S.C. §502, Cornell LII).
- **§502(b)(7)** caps employment-contract termination claims at one year's compensation plus unpaid compensation.
- **§502(c)** requires estimation of contingent or unliquidated claims whose liquidation would unduly delay the case.

Kaz is delighted by §502(c). "The statute requires estimation! Which estimator? Maximum likelihood? Bayesian with a conjugate prior?" Theo explains that the estimator is a bankruptcy judge. Kaz writes this down as "an M-estimator with a robe," which is not wrong: a §502(c) estimate is a court's point estimate for the purpose of voting or reserves, not the mean of anything, and the desk should treat it as one more noisy observation of the pool, not as the pool.

### 16.5.3 Derivation: the random denominator

**Setup.** Let $\mathcal{V}$ be the value distributable to the class and $\mathcal{C}$ the total allowed claims in the class. A claim's recovery per dollar of allowed face is
$$R = \min\!\left(1, \frac{\mathcal{V}}{\mathcal{C}}\right).$$
Let $Y \equiv \ln(\mathcal{V}/\mathcal{C})$ and assume $Y \sim N(\mu_Y, s_Y^2)$ under $\mathbb{P}$, which holds if $\mathcal{V}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ are jointly lognormal; their correlation enters through $s_Y^2 = s_{\mathcal{V}}^2 + s_{\mathcal{C}}^2 - 2\varrho\, s_{\mathcal{V}} s_{\mathcal{C}}$. Split the expectation at $Y = 0$:
$$\mathbb{E}[R] = \Pr(Y \ge 0) + \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{Y}\mathbf 1_{\{Y<0\}}\right].$$
The first term is $\Phi(\mu_Y/s_Y)$. For the second, complete the square in the exponent of the normal density:
$$y - \frac{(y - \mu_Y)^2}{2 s_Y^2} = -\frac{(y - \mu_Y - s_Y^2)^2}{2 s_Y^2} + \mu_Y + \frac{s_Y^2}{2},$$
so the exponential tilts the normal's mean by $s_Y^2$, and
$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{Y}\mathbf 1_{\{Y<0\}}\right] = e^{\mu_Y + s_Y^2/2}\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{0 - \mu_Y - s_Y^2}{s_Y}\right).$$
Putting the pieces together:
$$\mathbb{E}[R] = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\mu_Y}{s_Y}\right) + e^{\mu_Y + s_Y^2/2}\,\Phi\!\left(-\frac{\mu_Y}{s_Y} - s_Y\right).$$
This is Black–Scholes bookkeeping with $\mathcal{V}/\mathcal{C}$ in the role of $S/K$: a claim is a long position in the ratio with a short call at 1 on top (the cap at par). Boundary checks: as $s_Y \to 0$ the formula collapses to $\min(1, e^{\mu_Y})$, and as $\mu_Y \to +\infty$ it tends to 1.

**The special case that matters most.** Fix $\mathcal{V}$ and let the pool be lognormal with mean $m_{\mathcal{C}}$ and log-standard deviation $s$, so $\ln\mathcal{C} \sim N(\ln m_{\mathcal{C}} - s^2/2,\, s^2)$ and $\mu_Y = \ln(\mathcal{V}/m_{\mathcal{C}}) + s^2/2$. With $a \equiv \mu_Y/s$:
$$\mathbb{E}[R] = \Phi(a) + \frac{\mathcal{V}}{m_{\mathcal{C}}}\,e^{s^2}\,\Phi(-a - s).$$
At $\mathcal{V} = m_{\mathcal{C}}$ (the class is covered *on average*) and $s = 0.30$: $a = 0.15$ and $\mathbb{E}[R] \approx 0.5596 + 1.0942 \times 0.3264 \approx 0.917$ *(illustrative)*. More than eight points of recovery disappear purely because the denominator is uncertain, even though the expected pool exactly equals the value available. That is the cap at work: when the pool comes in small you cannot recover more than par, and when it comes in large you lose.

**Jensen cuts both ways.** Now take a deeply impaired class, $\mathcal{V}/m_{\mathcal{C}} = 0.30$, with the same $s$. The cap almost never binds, and the reciprocal $1/\mathcal{C}$ is *convex*, so variance helps: the formula gives $\mathbb{E}[R] \approx 0.328 > 0.30$. Near par, pool uncertainty is a cost; deep out of the money, it is a (small) gift. The desk must know which side of the kink a class sits on before it decides whether it wants to own the uncertainty or sell it. Figure 16.3 draws the kink.

![Figure 16.3: With a random claims pool, expected recovery sits below the no-uncertainty line when the class is covered on average (0.917 at V/m = 1) and above it when the class is deeply impaired (0.328 at V/m = 0.30), and a wider pool widens both effects. Illustrative: the closed form of §16.5.3 with a lognormal pool, s = 0.30 and s = 0.60.](assets/figures/ch16_claim_recovery_pool_risk.png)

**Pricing the claim.** With allowance probability $p_A$, an effective date $T$ that arrives at hazard rate $h$ per year (so $T$ is exponential with mean $1/h$ and independent of $R$), a discount rate $r$ and an illiquidity premium $\psi$, the expected discount factor is
$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-(r+\psi)T}\right] = \int_0^\infty h\,e^{-ht}\,e^{-(r+\psi)t}\,dt = \frac{h}{h + r + \psi},$$
and the price per dollar of face of a general unsecured claim is
$$\pi^{\text{GUC}} = p_A\,\frac{h}{h + r + \psi}\,\mathbb{E}[R] \;+\; \underbrace{p_A\,\Pr(\text{solvent})\,\mathbb{E}[\text{post-petition interest} \mid \text{solvent}]}_{\text{the Hertz option, usually} \;\approx\; 0}.$$
No coupon appears anywhere, because §502(b)(2) bars one. A **503(b)(9) claim** is the same bond with the face problem removed: it is paid in full in cash on the effective date unless the estate is administratively insolvent (probability $p_{AI}$, loss given that event $L_{AI}$):
$$\pi^{(9)} = p_A^{(9)}\,\frac{h}{h + r + \psi}\,\left(1 - p_{AI}L_{AI}\right), \qquad p_A^{(9)} = \Pr(\text{goods, ordinary course, physical receipt within 20 days}).$$

**The pair.** Because both claims share the same timing factor, the log price spread cancels it exactly:
$$\ln \pi^{(9)} - \ln \pi^{\text{GUC}} = \ln\frac{p_A^{(9)}}{p_A} + \ln\!\left(1 - p_{AI}L_{AI}\right) - \ln \mathbb{E}[R].$$
Duration is held fixed and what remains is allowance risk, administrative-insolvency risk, and pool risk. For a near-par class, where more pool uncertainty lowers $\mathbb{E}[R]$, *long 503(b)(9) / short GUC is long pool uncertainty*: it pays if the denominator surprises the market by coming in large or ragged. The reverse trade, long the GUC and short the 503(b)(9), is the trade for the person who has *shrunk* $s$ by doing the work: counting rejected leases and applying the §502(b)(6) cap lease by lease, matching filed claims to the schedules, striking duplicates and superseded amendments, sizing WARN Act and litigation claims. That person is the former RSSG associate. This is the chapter's cleanest example of being paid for a variance everyone else is afraid of.

**Denomination risk.** In customer bankruptcies the claim is often fixed in petition-date dollars. If a claim on an asset worth $A_{t_0}$ at the petition date recovers $R$ in cash at $T$, the recovery *in kind*, in units of the asset the customer actually had, is
$$R^{\text{in-kind}} = R\,\frac{A_{t_0}}{A_T}.$$
A 120% cash recovery on a bitcoin claim dollarized at about \$17k on 11 Nov 2022 is roughly a 20% in-kind recovery if bitcoin trades at \$100k on the distribution date *(illustrative)*. The claimant is short a call on the asset, struck at the petition-date price, and was paid nothing for it.

> **Trend Watch.** *Claims trading went crypto, 2023–2026.*
> - **Price path.** Some lower-ranked FTX claims traded at roughly 12 to 15% of face at the start of 2023 and about 35% by September 2023, according to Claims Market pricing (Bloomberg, 21 Sep 2023; Decrypt, 22 Sep 2023), which also reported more than \$250mm of claims bought by Silver Point Capital, Diameter Capital Partners and Attestor Capital, a \$23mm claim bought by Hudson Bay, and possibly more than \$100mm of trades not yet reported because of transfer-paperwork lags.

FTX was the Lehman of this cycle: a right-tail buyer who read the dollarization clause correctly went from about 15 cents to more than 100 cents in cash in roughly two years, roughly seven to nine times the money.

The pricing edge is in $p_A$ and $s$, and the risk the market most often misprices is denomination.

A wider pool distribution must widen that spread. The in-kind example turns 120% in cash into 20.4%.

Use it to bid on claims in classes whose pool you can bound, and to size the 503(b)(9)-versus-GUC pair. Do not use it to replace the work of shrinking $s$: the model is only as good as your pool estimate. The edge case that bites is the omitted solvency option. `price_claim` carries no post-petition interest, as its docstring warns, so on a solvent debtor, where §502(b)(2) yields to the Hertz-style exception, it prices the general unsecured claim too low.

Hertz is the only case we know of that runs every instrument in this chapter through one company: equity trading during bankruptcy, a competitive plan auction, a rights offering with a backstop, 30-year warrants, a re-IPO, a solvent-debtor fight over post-petition interest, and then, five years later, a return to the liability-management zone. The Third Circuit's opening paragraph is the cleanest statement of the absolute priority rule a systematic desk will ever read: "The debtors here, able to pay their creditors in full, believe they have the leverage to deny their unsecured noteholders more than a quarter billion dollars of interest they promised to pay pre-bankruptcy, all while giving lower priority equityholders four times that amount" (*In re The Hertz Corp.*, 3d Cir., 10 Sep 2024). Footnote 5 of the same opinion gives us the plan's own marks for what equity received: about \$240mm of cash, a 3% stake valued at \$141mm, and warrants valued at \$769mm, which is the roughly \$1.1bn the court describes the stockholders receiving.

**How a systematic desk would have traded each leg.**

1. **June 2020, equity in bankruptcy.** Old equity in a Chapter 11 case is a deep out-of-the-money call on enterprise value struck at *all* claims, including post-petition interest. The useful number was the enterprise value implied by the equity market cap plus every claim ahead of it, compared with any plausible range for the business. In June 2020 that comparison said "sell," and the debtor itself tried to sell. It also turned out that the call finished in the money, helped by the recovery in travel and the 2021 surge in used-car prices, which raised the value of the fleet. The desk lesson is humility about deep out-of-the-money calls on a company whose main asset is a commodity in a supply shock, and discipline about borrow cost and locates if you are short them.
2. **May 2021, the auction.** Two competing bids are a free market test of the equity. Pricing each bid's package for old holders with the warrant and rights machinery of §16.3–16.4 tells you which bid the equity should prefer and how much the auction moved old-equity value. It is also the moment to read the warrant strike basis (\$6.5bn) against the rights valuation (\$4.7bn): a 38% gap that the plan described as a premium and that the derivation in §16.4 shows was only a premium relative to the sponsors' own lowball.
3. **June 2021, the rights and the backstop.** §16.3.4: a short-dated put sold for a 10% fee paid in shares, plus a 22% unsubscribed block created by the plan's own warrant-or-rights election.
4. **July to November 2021, emergence to re-IPO.** Hertz is an unusual post-reorg equity because its unsecured creditors were paid in cash, so there was little CLO-driven overhang. The "supply" was the sponsors' and backstop parties' wish to monetize, released on a schedule through an underwritten secondary. In the language of §16.2, $Q_0$ was large but its release date was known, and the buyback of \$300mm at the offering price was the company absorbing part of the release itself.
5. **2021 to 2024, the make-whole fight.** This is the "Hertz option" term in the GUC pricing formula of §16.5.3: post-petition interest that exists only in the solvent branch. Usually it is worth approximately zero and we drop it. In Hertz it was worth more than \$270mm, and the noteholders were paid for having written the option into their indentures in the first place.
6. **2026, round two.** At a \$3 stock the \$13.80-strike warrants expiring in 2051 are a 25-year call roughly 4.6 times out of the money, which is to say a pure bet on volatility and survival. The new exchangeable first-lien PIK notes rank ahead of existing unsecured debt, and the creditor groups that organized in October 2026 put Hertz squarely in the territory of the Priming Risk Score of Chapter 13. Theo, who did not work on Hertz at PJT, notes that his former colleagues are now on the other side of a trade his current desk might want to make, and that the wall between the two is exactly as thick as the compliance manual says.

## 16.7 Case Study: Spirit Airlines, the Five-Month Zero

If Hertz shows how far the right tail of post-reorg equity can run, Spirit Airlines shows how fast the left tail arrives. Per press reports and the company's announcements, Spirit filed for Chapter 11 on 18 Nov 2024 in the Southern District of New York, emerged on 12 Mar 2025 after about four months, having converted roughly \$795mm of debt into equity, with old shareholders wiped out and the new equity issued to noteholders. On 29 Aug 2025, less than six months after emergence, it filed again, listing roughly \$8.1bn of debts against \$8.6bn of assets, after aircraft-lease terminations and card-processor holdbacks squeezed its liquidity; the company said its new shares would likely be cancelled and worthless *(dates and figures per press reports; verify on the docket)*.

The post-reorg equity of March 2025 was therefore not "a small-cap airline at a cheap multiple." It was a short-dated call on the business plan's **liquidity bridge**: the weeks between emergence and the point at which the plan's revenue assumptions either showed up in cash or did not. A multiple cannot see a card processor holding back receipts or a lessor taking back aircraft. A 13-week cash flow model can, because those are exactly the line items it is built from: receipts by week net of holdbacks, aircraft rent by week, minimum liquidity covenants by week.

Theo does not say "I told you so." He takes out the USB stick, puts it on the desk, and lets the stick say it. The desk turns his model into a feature in the jump term of every post-reorg position: let the weekly default intensity be a decreasing function of liquidity runway,
$$\lambda_t = \lambda_0 \exp\!\left(-\beta\,\frac{L_t}{B_t}\right),$$
where $L_t$ is unrestricted liquidity (after holdbacks), $B_t$ is projected weekly cash burn, $L_t/B_t$ is runway in weeks, and $\lambda_0, \beta > 0$ are fitted on re-defaults (Chapter 14's re-default clock covers the fitting). A post-reorg position's size then shrinks automatically as runway shortens, which is what a restructuring banker would have done by instinct and what a systematic book has to be told explicitly. For the warrant pricer in §16.4, this $\lambda_t$ is the jump intensity; for the book in §16.8, it is the jump-to-zero probability.

## 16.8 Putting It Together: A Post-Reorg Book

### 16.8.1 The distribution you are actually trading

Combine §16.1's Trend Watch with §16.7's left tail. Over a 200-day horizon a single post-reorg equity's return $R$ is roughly a lognormal with median zero and mean +40% (the Jiang–Wang–Yang shape) with a jump to $-100\%$ mixed in (the Spirit shape). Write $X \sim N(0, s^2)$ and a jump indicator $J$ with $\Pr(J=1) = p_J$:
$$R = (1 - J)\left(e^{X} - 1\right) - J, \qquad \mathbb{E}[e^X] = e^{s^2/2} = 1.4 \;\Rightarrow\; s = \sqrt{2\ln 1.4} \approx 0.82 .$$
Then $\mathbb{E}[R] = (1-p_J)(1.4) - 1$, which is +33% for $p_J = 5\%$ *(illustrative)*, while the median is slightly *negative*: solving $p_J + (1-p_J)\Phi(\ln(1+m)/s) = \tfrac12$ gives $m \approx -5\%$. **The typical single name loses money; the average name makes a third.** That is the sentence to tape to the monitor before anyone proposes a concentrated post-reorg book. Figure 16.5 shows the single-name distribution beside a 20-name book.

![Figure 16.5: A single post-reorg name has a mean return of +33% but a median of −5%, while an equal-weighted book of 20 names has a median of +29%, close to its mean, so the premium is a portfolio property. Illustrative: 400,000 simulated draws per panel from the §16.8.1 jump-lognormal mixture (s = 0.82, jump probability 5%, seed 16); the right panel groups them into 20,000 books of 20 names.](assets/figures/ch16_post_reorg_return_distribution.png)

Diversification turns the mean into the median. For an equal-weighted book of $N$ independent names, the book return has the same mean, standard deviation reduced by $\sqrt N$, and skewness reduced by $\sqrt N$, so its median moves toward its mean. With $N = 20$ (about the size of Jiang, Wang & Yang's portfolio) the median book return is comfortably positive, as Block 5 confirms by simulation.

### 16.8.2 Sizing: Kelly with a trapdoor, then a liquidity ceiling

For a single name held as a fraction $f$ of capital, growth-optimal sizing maximizes $g(f) = \mathbb{E}[\ln(1 + fR)]$. The jump to $-100\%$ makes $g(f) \to -\infty$ as $f \to 1$, so the Kelly fraction is always strictly less than one, and the first-order condition
$$g'(f) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{R}{1 + fR}\right] = 0$$
has a neat check point. At $f = \tfrac12$, the non-jump term is $\mathbb{E}[2\tanh(X/2)] = 0$ by symmetry of $X$, and the jump term is $p_J \cdot (-1)/(1 - \tfrac12) = -2p_J$, so $g'(\tfrac12) = -2p_J < 0$: with any jump risk at all, full-name Kelly is below one half. In practice we use half-Kelly (Chapter 28) and then hit the constraint that actually binds, which is liquidity. If a position must be exitable at participation rate $\pi$ of average daily dollar volume $\text{ADV}$ within $d$ days, its size is capped at $\pi \cdot \text{ADV} \cdot d$. For an OTC post-reorg stock trading \$200k a day, a 10% participation and a 20-day exit give a \$400k position. This is why the surviving post-reorg alpha, which lives in the OTC segment, does not scale: it is real, and a \$500mm book can own about four hundred thousand dollars of it per name.

## 16.9 Desk Debrief

- **The plan value is a negotiated number.** Anchor every post-reorg instrument to the price insiders actually pay in the same plan (the rights price, the direct-investment price), never to the disclosure statement's midpoint.
- **Forced selling is a supply schedule, not a mood.** Overhang $Q_0$, execution rate $\theta$ (about 3.4 a year, a 2.4-month half-life, from the fallen-angel aggregates), capital $M_t$ arriving slowly. The discount peaks *after* the event when delivery is staggered, and the signal expires when the 13G says the constrained holders are out.
- **The post-reorg premium is a portfolio property.** Mean +40%, median zero, and no alpha after 2010 for listed names. Only a diversified book harvests the right tail, and the surviving OTC alpha does not scale past a few hundred thousand dollars a name.
- **A backstop is a short put, and the fee is its smallest leg.** At a 25% discount and 70% volatility the fair fee is about 6%; the discount, the fee paid in shares and the exclusivity option take the all-in transfer to about 20% of the raise. Since *ConvergeOne*, an exclusive backstop without a market test also carries legal reversal risk.
- **Price warrants with dilution, and never charge for the funeral twice.** With a market spot, jump-to-default enters through $r + \lambda$, not as an $e^{-\lambda T}$ haircut. The plan's own warrant valuation tells you the volatility the other side assumed.
- **A claim is long a ratio and short a call at par.** Near par, pool uncertainty costs recovery (about 8 points at a 30% log-sd); deep out of the money, it helps. Know which side of the kink you are on.
- **The 503(b)(9)/GUC pair isolates pool risk.** The person who has counted the leases, applied the §502(b)(6) cap and struck the duplicate claims should be long the GUC against the 503(b)(9). That person used to work at PJT.
- **Check the 13-week model before the multiple.** Spirit's first post-reorg equity lasted under six months; Hertz's warrants run to 2051 and the company was back in the room in 2026. Runway drives the jump term.

**The Spec You Hand the Robot.** Build `post_reorg_engine.py` with five modules, each under $\mathbb{P}$ unless labeled, each carrying the asserts in Blocks 1–5 as unit tests. (1) `overhang`: `ForcedSellerOverhang(Q0, theta, M0, M_star, rho, kappa, alpha=None)` with `Q`, `discount`, `drift`, `half_life`, `peak_time`; inputs from a holder registry (plan schedule, Rule 2019, 13D/13G, Form 4, 13F, trustee reports); output a daily expected-excess-drift signal per name that is exactly zero once executed fraction reaches one.
