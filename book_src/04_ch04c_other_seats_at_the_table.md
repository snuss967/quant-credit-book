## Cold Open: Seven People Bought the Same Bond

*Tuesday, 7:40 a.m. Day 3.* (Halvorsen is fictional, and so is everyone in this chapter's seven seats; every price on Halvorsen's tape is illustrative. Day numbers count trading days from the Friday of Chapter 1's RFQ. One market holiday falls inside the window, so day 30 lands on a Friday.)

The morning explain for day 2 (Chapter 8-C builds the machine that prints it) has one line that matters, and it is not subtle: *HALVORSEN 7.25% 2029, \$12mm face, 92.00 to 78.00, −\$1,680,000.* Next to it the carry line reads +\$2,417, three days of coupon on \$12mm under 30/360, which this morning looks like a tip left on the table after the restaurant has burned down.

Theo has read the line four times. He reads it a fifth time in case it has changed.

"Somewhere," Mags says from behind her printout, "there is a man who owns this bond at 99 and has not lost a cent. He is right. And so are you."

"He's lost fourteen points," Theo says. "He just hasn't written it down."

"He isn't required to write it down. That's the whole difference between him and you, and it's worth more than anything in that explain." She turns a page. "He bought it at issue for a book that carries it at amortized cost. Unless somebody decides it's impaired, his number this morning is 99 and change. Yours is 78. Neither of you is lying."

Kaz looks up from a notebook full of Greek. "Both can't be true. A bond has one price."

"A bond has one price," Ines says, "and seven owners, and each owner has a different thing they're forced to do when the price moves. Some of them are forced to sell by Thursday. Some owe a regulator a number by December. Some can sit on their hands until a judge tells them otherwise." She sets her coffee down on Theo's desk like a chip. "So who owns this thing?"

Theo knows roughly. In his old job, sitting across the table from all of them was the job. The term loan sits in a few dozen CLOs; the trustee reports say which. The notes sit in high-yield mutual funds and at least one systematic manager, according to the last N-PORT filings. A life insurer owns a slice of a CLO that owns the term loan. A business development company holds some of the loan in what it calls its liquid sleeve. And Friday's \$15mm went to the dealer who won the RFQ that Theo passed on (Chapter 1).

He types a request to ROBO before he can think better of it: *Model the other holders of Halvorsen.*

ROBO returns in eleven seconds with seven portfolios. Each one is the desk. Each has the desk's twenty-day horizon, the desk's limit sheet from Chapter 8-B and the desk's five-point stop, and each has been given a name: HOLDER_1 through HOLDER_7. HOLDER_4 is labeled CLO_MANAGER.

"That's not a CLO," Kaz says. "That's us in a hat."

ROBO did exactly what it was asked. Theo had asked it to model the holders, and the only holder it had a model for was the one in the room. The precise version of the request, which this chapter builds toward and §4C.9 writes down, is that each holder is not a utility function at all. Each one is a short list of constraints, a set of dates on which those constraints are checked, and a price at which each one bites.

"Seven chairs," Ines says, "and nobody has pulled them together yet. When they pull them together it's called an ad hoc group, and it's a different chapter." She taps the explain. "For now, go and find out who has to do something about this number, and when. That's the only thing about Halvorsen you can know that the credit agreement can't tell you."

So Theo spends the next six weeks doing what he used to do for a living, with a different purpose. He calls people. A dealer he used to see at closing dinners. A systematic portfolio manager who once asked him what a springing lien was. A pod PM he went to business school with. A CLO manager, an insurer's investment committee, a direct lender's analyst who was an associate at the next desk over at his old firm, and the head of a dislocation fund who never returns calls until she wants something. The bond falls from 92 to 70 over thirty trading days. Every one of them sees the same seven prints. This chapter is about what each of them books, and why.

By the end you will be able to take any bond on the desk's screen and draw the table that Theo draws in §4C.9: for each kind of owner, its horizon, its capital, the constraint that binds it, the date that constraint is checked, and the price at which it fires. You will see why the same tape produces opposite trades from two coherent seats, why a rule-bound buyer is a predictable seller, and why accounting is a filter laid over a price rather than a disagreement about it. You will meet six small pieces of mathematics on the way, each in a Math, Gently box at the moment it first matters. And on day 30, when the coupon on the 6% notes is not paid, you will watch the seven seats turn into the classes, lenders and plan sponsors of Chapter 12-B, because the seat map is also the coalition map.

## 4C.1 Friday, 2:58 p.m.: The Dealer Desk

*This seat is a fictional composite of a mid-sized dealer's high-yield desk. Dana Rourke and Sunil are invented; the public statements of real dealers quoted below are dated and sourced.*

Rewind to the Friday. The RFQ that arrived on Theo's screen in a grey box arrived on Dana Rourke's screen in a grey box too, and for her the fourteen seconds held more information, not less.

Dana runs high yield at a dealer large enough to matter in single-B paper and small enough that one bad week shows up in the stock. Her book is one book: Sunil's auto-quoter answers the small electronic inquiries, her voice traders answer the large ones, and both draw on the same inventory and the same risk limits. Fred Orlan, Jefferies' global head of fixed income, described the model at his firm's investor day on 16 October 2025: "seamless integration between our low touch electronic trading and our high touch voice desks is essential to making this happen." Dana's firm has the same org chart and a smaller balance sheet.

Three things about the Halvorsen RFQ were visible to Dana and not to Theo. Her firm had been a bookrunner on Halvorsen's 2021 bond deal, so the issuer was a banking client and the coverage banker would be calling on Monday whatever happened. The seller was a fund that ranks in the desk's top twenty accounts and would want allocations in the next new issue the desk led. And the desk's client ranking for that account, the one the sales team reviews every quarter, gave credit for showing a bid when other dealers would not. All three were reasons to win.

Sunil's quoter priced the notes at 91.40, the same number Kaz's engine produced across town. Dana overrode it to 91.25 because the lender call had ended at two and she did not like what silence from the company's advisors usually means. That was the whole of her adjustment for the possibility that the seller knew something: fifteen cents. She won. At 4:40 p.m. the 8-K landed.

The rest of Friday evening was the dealer's version of a restructuring all-nighter, conducted mostly on chat. The first thing Dana's desk reaches for when it buys a block is a way to lay it off or hedge it. The Federal Reserve's June 2025 Senior Credit Officer Opinion Survey asked dealers how they manage the baskets they take on in portfolio trades, and the answers describe Dana's toolbox for any block: about four-fifths of active dealers rated trading out of the individual bonds by other protocols "very important," ETF creation and redemption mattered to about three-quarters, and three-fifths "frequently hedge" with credit ETFs when the bonds are not in the creation basket. Halvorsen was in the index, but this week it was in nobody's creation basket, so the cleanest exit, delivering the bonds into an ETF, was closed. The ETF short was still available, and it covered exactly the part of Friday's move that was not about Halvorsen. Monday's high-yield ETF fell a few tenths of a point. The notes fell thirteen.

So the second tool, finding the other side, was the only one left, and over the weekend the other side did not exist. Real buyers of a single-B bond after an 8-K about new-money financing at a subsidiary that holds the patents are people who need to read the 8-K, call counsel and size the position, and none of them do that on a Saturday. On Monday the notes opened at 78. Dana's desk was down \$1,987,500 on the position, thirteen and a quarter points on \$15mm.

Here the credit stopped mattering. Dana's head of credit trading has a standing instruction that any position in a name that has gapped more than ten points, and that is still on the book after two trading days, goes to the Wednesday risk committee with a plan. Dana has attended that committee as the person with the plan. It is not a forum that rewards the sentence "I think the market is overreacting." On Tuesday, day 3, she sold \$10mm at 79 to a relative-value pod (§4C.3) and \$5mm to a distressed fund at the same price, and realized a loss of \$1,837,500. By Wednesday the bonds were 81, so for one afternoon she was wrong. By day 30 they were 70, so she was right. Neither outcome was the reason. The limit was the reason.

Dana is not unusual, and the public record says so in plainer words than a dealer usually uses. Orlan, profiled by The DESK on 26 October 2023: "It's hard for investment banks to run large balance sheets in this environment because it isn't making a lot of money." A bank-owned dealer feels the same pressure through its regulatory capital. JPMorgan's chief financial officer, Jeremy Barnum, on 14 April 2026, called the G-SIB surcharge "a significant disincentive to a particular type of business, in particular some markets business." And Bank of America's CFO Alastair Borthwick, on 14 October 2026, described what happens to the whole franchise when markets gap: "It just happens when rates go up 85 basis points in a quarter, that tends not to be good for those businesses. Clients tend to slow their activity." A dealer's edge is the client franchise. Its horizon is days. Its capital belongs to a balance sheet that charges rent, and so its number for a bond, when the bond goes wrong, is not a view of what Halvorsen is worth. It is the price at which the desk can be rid of it.

There is a second way a dealer's credit exposure arrives, and it does not come through the trading desk at all. In October 2025, Global Trade Review reported that Point Bonita Capital, a trade-finance fund run by Jefferies' Leucadia Asset Management unit, had US\$715mn of exposure to First Brands receivables, about a quarter of the fund's US\$3bn of trade-finance assets (GTR, 8 Oct 2025). Jefferies' chief executive, Rich Handler, said: "we believe we were defrauded." Whatever one makes of the episode, its shape is the point for this chapter. Reading a dealer seat from its trading line alone misses the affiliates, and an affiliate's loss changes how much risk the trading line is allowed to carry the following week.

> **Math, Gently.** *Horizon can change the sign of a trade.* Suppose Dana and the pod in §4C.3 agree exactly on Halvorsen. Both think the notes, at 79, are worth 88 in a year once the forced selling fades: an expected drift of 9 points over 252 trading days, or about 0.036 points a day. Both think the daily noise in a name that has just gapped is about 1.5 points. Expected gain grows in proportion to time, but the typical swing grows only with the square root of time, because independent daily moves partly cancel (Chapter 3's random walk; §3.1.3). Over 20 days the expected gain is $0.036 \times 20 \approx 0.7$ points and the typical swing is $1.5\sqrt{20} \approx 6.7$ points. Over a year the gain is 9 points and the swing $1.5\sqrt{252} \approx 24$ points. The ratio of gain to swing rises from about 0.1 to about 0.4, slowly, as $\sqrt{T}$.
>
> Now give Dana's seat what it actually has: a 5-point loss trigger and a 20-day horizon. A driftless walk touches a level before time $T$ about twice as often as it finishes beyond that level (the reflection principle), so with a swing of 6.7 points the chance of touching −5 inside 20 days is roughly $2 \times \mathbb{P}(Z < -5/6.7) = 2 \times 0.23 \approx 0.45$, a little less with the small positive drift. Each forced exit costs about 3 points of bid–ask in a stressed name. Expected cost: about $0.43 \times 3 \approx 1.3$ points. Expected drift captured: at most 0.7. Dana's expected P&L from holding is negative. The pod, with a one-year horizon and a 15-point trigger, is stopped out about as often over its longer life, roughly four times in ten, but the drift it collects is measured in points rather than tenths: something like 6 to 7 points against about 1.2 points of expected exit cost. Same bond, same opinion, opposite sign. The full version, with drift in the hitting probability and the cost of the stop as an option, is §28.6.

Dana's desk did nothing irrational. It ran the wrong model of its own seat on Friday, a fifteen-cent adjustment for the possibility of an informed seller, when the seat's real exposure was a fourteen-point gap followed by a two-day clock. The quote that would have priced both is a five-term decomposition: the mid, the half-spread, the inventory charge, the adverse-selection charge and the cost of the exit the desk's own limits would force. Chapter 18 derives its pieces and Chapter 18-B assembles them into the quote Dana's desk ought to have run at 2:58.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The venue.* The RFQ went out all-to-all, on a platform, and the platform is the one participant in this chapter that is paid when everyone else is paying. MarketAxess's 2022 shareholder letter, written after a year in which the Fed's hikes "drove an unprecedented 14% decline in investment-grade bond indices," credits its share gains to the dealers' retreat: Open Trading was "a critical driver of our strong estimated market share gains in 2022, as spreads widened, and dealer liquidity was less prevalent in the market," and total credit Open Trading penetration reached 36% (MarketAxess, FY2022 annual report letter, Rick McVey). The venue sees Halvorsen's day 2 as volume. It also sees what the dealers see, from the other side: when dealers step back, buyers and sellers cross with each other, and the price they cross at is set by whoever is forced. The venue earns a fee per million on that trade whether the seller is Dana, the systematic fund in §4C.2 or a CLO in §4C.4; the same letter notes that rapid hikes cut its average fee per million in high grade. It is paid in volume, while every other seat is paid in pain.
