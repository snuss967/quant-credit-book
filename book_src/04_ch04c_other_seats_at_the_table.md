## Cold Open: Seven People Bought the Same Bond

*Tuesday, 7:40 a.m. Day 3.* (Halvorsen is fictional, and so is everyone in this chapter's seven seats; every price on Halvorsen's tape is illustrative. Day numbers count trading days from the Friday of Chapter 1's RFQ. One market holiday falls inside the window, so day 30 lands on a Friday.)

The morning explain for day 2 (Chapter 8-C builds the machine that prints it) has one line that matters, and it is not subtle: *HALVORSEN 7.25% 2029, \$12mm face, 92.00 to 78.00, −\$1,680,000.* Next to it the carry line reads +\$2,417, three days of coupon on \$12mm under 30/360, which this morning looks like a tip left on the table after the restaurant has burned down.

Theo has read the line four times. He reads it a fifth time in case it has changed.

"Somewhere," Mags says from behind her printout, "there is a man who owns this bond at 99 and has not lost a cent. He is right. And so are you."

"He's lost fourteen points," Theo says. "He just hasn't written it down."

"He isn't required to write it down. That's the whole difference between him and you, and it's worth more than anything in that explain." She turns a page. "He bought it at issue for a book that carries it at amortized cost. Unless somebody decides it's impaired, his number this morning is 99 and change. Yours is 78. Neither of you is lying."

Kaz looks up from a notebook full of Greek. "Both can't be true. A bond has one price."

"A bond has one price," Ines says, "and seven owners, and each owner has a different thing they're forced to do when the price moves. Some of them are forced to sell by Thursday. Some owe a regulator a number by December. Some can sit on their hands until a judge tells them otherwise." She sets her coffee down on Theo's desk like a chip. "So who owns this thing?"

Theo knows roughly. In his old job, sitting across the table from all of them was the job. The term loan sits in a few dozen CLOs; the trustee reports say which. The notes sit in high-yield mutual funds and at least one systematic manager, according to the last N-PORT filings. A life insurer owns a slice of a CLO that owns the term loan. A business development company holds some of the loan in what it calls its liquid sleeve. And Friday's \$15mm went to the dealer who won the RFQ that Theo passed on (Chapter 1).

He types a request to ROBO before he can think better of it: *Model the other holders of Halvorsen.*

ROBO returns in eleven seconds with seven portfolios. Each one is the desk. Each has the desk's twenty-day horizon, the desk's limit sheet from Chapter 8-B and the desk's five-point stop, and each has been given a name: HOLDER_1 through HOLDER_7. HOLDER_4 is labeled CLO_MANAGER.

"That's not a CLO," Kaz says. "That's us in a hat."

ROBO did exactly what it was asked. Theo had asked it to model the holders, and the only holder it had a model for was the one in the room. The precise version of the request, which this chapter builds toward and §4C.9 writes down, is that each holder is not a utility function at all. Each one is a short list of constraints, a set of dates on which those constraints are checked, and a price at which each one bites.

"Seven chairs," Ines says, "and nobody has pulled them together yet. When they pull them together it's called an ad hoc group, and it's a different chapter." She taps the explain. "For now, go and find out who has to do something about this number, and when. That's the only thing about Halvorsen you can know that the credit agreement can't tell you."

So Theo spends the next six weeks doing what he used to do for a living, with a different purpose. He calls people. A dealer he used to see at closing dinners. A systematic portfolio manager who once asked him what a springing lien was. A pod PM he went to business school with. A CLO manager, an insurer's investment committee, a direct lender's analyst who was an associate at the next desk over at his old firm, and the head of a dislocation fund who never returns calls until she wants something. The bond falls from 92 to 70 over thirty trading days. Every one of them sees the same seven prints. This chapter is about what each of them books, and why.

By the end you will be able to take any bond on the desk's screen and draw the table that Theo draws in §4C.9: for each kind of owner, its horizon, its capital, the constraint that binds it, the date that constraint is checked, and the price at which it fires. You will see why the same tape produces opposite trades from two coherent seats, why a rule-bound buyer is a predictable seller, and why accounting is a filter laid over a price rather than a disagreement about it. You will meet six small pieces of mathematics on the way, each in a Math, Gently box at the moment it first matters. And on day 30, when the coupon on the 6% notes is not paid, you will watch the seven seats turn into the classes, lenders and plan sponsors of Chapter 12-B, because the seat map is also the coalition map.

## 4C.1 Friday, 2:58 p.m.: The Dealer Desk

*This seat is a fictional composite of a mid-sized dealer's high-yield desk. Dana Rourke and Sunil are invented; the public statements of real dealers quoted below are dated and sourced.*

Rewind to the Friday. The RFQ that arrived on Theo's screen in a grey box arrived on Dana Rourke's screen in a grey box too, and for her the fourteen seconds held more information, not less.

Dana runs high yield at a dealer large enough to matter in single-B paper and small enough that one bad week shows up in the stock. Her book is one book: Sunil's auto-quoter answers the small electronic inquiries, her voice traders answer the large ones, and both draw on the same inventory and the same risk limits. Fred Orlan, Jefferies' global head of fixed income, described the model at his firm's investor day on 16 October 2025: "seamless integration between our low touch electronic trading and our high touch voice desks is essential to making this happen." Dana's firm has the same org chart and a smaller balance sheet.

Three things about the Halvorsen RFQ were visible to Dana and not to Theo. Her firm had been a bookrunner on Halvorsen's 2021 bond deal, so the issuer was a banking client and the coverage banker would be calling on Monday whatever happened. The seller was a fund that ranks in the desk's top twenty accounts and would want allocations in the next new issue the desk led. And the desk's client ranking for that account, the one the sales team reviews every quarter, gave credit for showing a bid when other dealers would not. All three were reasons to win.

Sunil's quoter priced the notes at 91.40, the same number Kaz's engine produced across town. Dana overrode it to 91.25 because the lender call had ended at two and she did not like what silence from the company's advisors usually means. That was the whole of her adjustment for the possibility that the seller knew something: fifteen cents. She won. At 4:40 p.m. the 8-K landed.

The rest of Friday evening was the dealer's version of a restructuring all-nighter, conducted mostly on chat. The first thing Dana's desk reaches for when it buys a block is a way to lay it off or hedge it. The Federal Reserve's June 2025 Senior Credit Officer Opinion Survey asked dealers how they manage the baskets they take on in portfolio trades, and the answers describe Dana's toolbox for any block: about four-fifths of active dealers rated trading out of the individual bonds by other protocols "very important," ETF creation and redemption mattered to about three-quarters, and three-fifths "frequently hedge" with credit ETFs when the bonds are not in the creation basket. Halvorsen was in the index, but this week it was in nobody's creation basket, so the cleanest exit, delivering the bonds into an ETF, was closed. The ETF short was still available, and it covered exactly the part of Friday's move that was not about Halvorsen. Monday's high-yield ETF fell a few tenths of a point. The notes fell thirteen.

So the second tool, finding the other side, was the only one left, and over the weekend the other side did not exist. Real buyers of a single-B bond after an 8-K about new-money financing at a subsidiary that holds the patents are people who need to read the 8-K, call counsel and size the position, and none of them do that on a Saturday. On Monday the notes opened at 78. Dana's desk was down \$1,987,500 on the position, thirteen and a quarter points on \$15mm.

Here the credit stopped mattering. Dana's head of credit trading has a standing instruction that any position in a name that has gapped more than ten points, and that is still on the book after two trading days, goes to the Wednesday risk committee with a plan. Dana has attended that committee as the person with the plan. It is not a forum that rewards the sentence "I think the market is overreacting." On Tuesday, day 3, she sold \$10mm at 79 to a relative-value pod (§4C.3) and \$5mm to a distressed fund at the same price, and realized a loss of \$1,837,500. By Wednesday the bonds were 81, so for one afternoon she was wrong. By day 30 they were 70, so she was right. Neither outcome was the reason. The limit was the reason.

Dana is not unusual, and the public record says so in plainer words than a dealer usually uses. Orlan, profiled by The DESK on 26 October 2023: "It's hard for investment banks to run large balance sheets in this environment because it isn't making a lot of money." A bank-owned dealer feels the same pressure through its regulatory capital. JPMorgan's chief financial officer, Jeremy Barnum, on 14 April 2026, called the G-SIB surcharge "a significant disincentive to a particular type of business, in particular some markets business." And Bank of America's CFO Alastair Borthwick, on 14 October 2026, described what happens to the whole franchise when markets gap: "It just happens when rates go up 85 basis points in a quarter, that tends not to be good for those businesses. Clients tend to slow their activity." A dealer's edge is the client franchise. Its horizon is days. Its capital belongs to a balance sheet that charges rent, and so its number for a bond, when the bond goes wrong, is not a view of what Halvorsen is worth. It is the price at which the desk can be rid of it.

There is a second way a dealer's credit exposure arrives, and it does not come through the trading desk at all. In October 2025, Global Trade Review reported that Point Bonita Capital, a trade-finance fund run by Jefferies' Leucadia Asset Management unit, had US\$715mn of exposure to First Brands receivables, about a quarter of the fund's US\$3bn of trade-finance assets (GTR, 8 Oct 2025). Jefferies' chief executive, Rich Handler, said: "we believe we were defrauded." Whatever one makes of the episode, its shape is the point for this chapter. Reading a dealer seat from its trading line alone misses the affiliates, and an affiliate's loss changes how much risk the trading line is allowed to carry the following week.

> **Math, Gently.** *Horizon can change the sign of a trade.* Suppose Dana and the pod in §4C.3 agree exactly on Halvorsen. Both think the notes, at 79, are worth 88 in a year once the forced selling fades: an expected drift of 9 points over 252 trading days, or about 0.036 points a day. Both think the daily noise in a name that has just gapped is about 1.5 points. Expected gain grows in proportion to time, but the typical swing grows only with the square root of time, because independent daily moves partly cancel (Chapter 3's random walk; §3.1.3). Over 20 days the expected gain is $0.036 \times 20 \approx 0.7$ points and the typical swing is $1.5\sqrt{20} \approx 6.7$ points. Over a year the gain is 9 points and the swing $1.5\sqrt{252} \approx 24$ points. The ratio of gain to swing rises from about 0.1 to about 0.4, slowly, as $\sqrt{T}$.
>
> Now give Dana's seat what it actually has: a 5-point loss trigger and a 20-day horizon. A driftless walk touches a level before time $T$ about twice as often as it finishes beyond that level (the reflection principle), so with a swing of 6.7 points the chance of touching −5 inside 20 days is roughly $2 \times \mathbb{P}(Z < -5/6.7) = 2 \times 0.23 \approx 0.45$, a little less with the small positive drift. Each forced exit costs about 3 points of bid–ask in a stressed name. Expected cost: about $0.43 \times 3 \approx 1.3$ points. Expected drift captured: at most 0.7. Dana's expected P&L from holding is negative. The pod, with a one-year horizon and a 15-point trigger, is stopped out about as often over its longer life, roughly four times in ten, but the drift it collects is measured in points rather than tenths: something like 6 to 7 points against about 1.2 points of expected exit cost. Same bond, same opinion, opposite sign. The full version, with drift in the hitting probability and the cost of the stop as an option, is §28.6.

Figure 4-C.2 draws the box: gain against swing on the left, and on the right what each seat's stop and clock do to the same opinion.

Dana's desk did nothing irrational. It ran the wrong model of its own seat on Friday, a fifteen-cent adjustment for the possibility of an informed seller, when the seat's real exposure was a fourteen-point gap followed by a two-day clock. The quote that would have priced both is a five-term decomposition: the mid, the half-spread, the inventory charge, the adverse-selection charge and the cost of the exit the desk's own limits would force. Chapter 18 derives its pieces and Chapter 18-B assembles them into the quote Dana's desk ought to have run at 2:58.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The venue.* The RFQ went out all-to-all, on a platform, and the platform is the one participant in this chapter that is paid when everyone else is paying. MarketAxess's 2022 shareholder letter, written after a year in which the Fed's hikes "drove an unprecedented 14% decline in investment-grade bond indices," credits its share gains to the dealers' retreat: Open Trading was "a critical driver of our strong estimated market share gains in 2022, as spreads widened, and dealer liquidity was less prevalent in the market," and total credit Open Trading penetration reached 36% (MarketAxess, FY2022 annual report letter, Rick McVey). The venue sees Halvorsen's day 2 as volume. It also sees what the dealers see, from the other side: when dealers step back, buyers and sellers cross with each other, and the price they cross at is set by whoever is forced. The venue earns a fee per million on that trade whether the seller is Dana, the systematic fund in §4C.2 or a CLO in §4C.4; the same letter notes that rapid hikes cut its average fee per million in high grade. It is paid in volume, while every other seat is paid in pain.

## 4C.2 Monday, 6:45 a.m.: The Systematic Fund

*This seat is a fictional composite of a long-only systematic high-yield manager. Annika Holm, Jules and Ola are invented. Robeco, AQR and Man Group appear only through their published words.*

> **Quant-shop life (a fictional composite).** At 5:10 a.m. on day 2 the overnight rebalance fails, which is how most mornings at a systematic credit manager begin and almost no articles about systematic credit mention. The failure is not interesting. The vendor's evaluated-price file arrived with Friday's timestamp on 41 bonds, and the pipeline's freshness check, written two years ago after a worse morning, refuses to run an optimizer on prices it believes are stale. Halvorsen is one of the 41. Ola, the data engineer on the early rota, opens a ticket with the vendor, gets an automated reply promising a response within four business hours, and calls the vendor's support line, where a person in another time zone confirms that the file is "being regenerated." The regenerated file arrives at 6:15. Ola reruns the job. The custodian's holdings file then fails to tie to the internal book by \$3mm face in a different bond, because a Friday trade booked for T+1 settlement is sitting in the custodian's "pending" column and in the book's "settled" column at the same time. Ola writes a one-line exception, flags it for the operations team, and moves on, because the rebalance cannot wait for operations to finish their coffee. By 6:45 the run is complete and Annika Holm, the portfolio manager, has a list of 212 trades and a cup of something she will not finish. None of this will appear in the fund's quarterly letter. All of it is the job.

Annika runs a benchmark-relative high-yield strategy of about \$1.2bn, and her process is the one the large systematic houses describe in public. Robeco's quant credit team, in a September 2026 note by Lodewijk van der Linden and Patrick Houweling, describes a selection model spanning about 1,600 issuers and 5,000 bonds in global high yield, a "ranking engine" and a "portfolio construction engine," and an aim "to keep first-order risks, such as rates and credit beta, close to the benchmark, so that relative returns are driven primarily by credit returns from bottom-up issuer and bond selection." Annika's universe is smaller and her optimizer is home-grown, but the shape is the same: scores per issuer, a covariance from the risk model (Chapter 8-B), a cost model per bond, and a set of constraints that the optimizer is not allowed to trade against.

The overnight scores for Halvorsen disagree with each other in a way that would start an argument on a fundamental desk and starts nothing here. Value says the notes are cheap: the spread widened by roughly 400 basis points against peers with similar leverage, which is exactly what a value signal is built to notice. Momentum says sell: a fourteen-point fall in one session is the strongest negative reading the signal has produced for any bond this year. And the default-probability guard, which turns the equity's collapse and the widening into a one-year probability of default, says the name has crossed the threshold above which the fund may not hold it at all. The guard is not a score that gets averaged with the others. It is a constraint. Once it binds, value and momentum stop mattering, and the optimizer's target weight for Halvorsen is zero. The fund holds \$8mm face. The trade list says sell \$8mm.

That kind of guard is a public stance and not an eccentricity. AQR's published framework for credit counts defensive and quality among the premia it harvests, and Diogo Palhares and Scott Richardson's paper *(Il)liquidity Premium in Credit Markets: A Myth?* (Journal of Fixed Income, 2019) finds "only limited evidence of a liquidity premium in the cross section of corporate bonds." A seat that believes that is not interested in being paid to hold an illiquid, gapped single-B while it decides whether the market has overreacted. It exits, and it accepts the cost of exiting as the price of not having an opinion about the 8-K.

Nobody at Annika's fund read the 8-K on Monday. Nobody was supposed to. Robeco's note is unusually candid about this blind spot: it describes a bond that looks cheap because spreads widened while "a major lawsuit, regulatory issue, large acquisition or other event sits outside the model's scope," and says that its "portfolio managers and fundamental credit analysts can challenge the model where downside risk is not adequately reflected." Robeco has more than twenty analysts doing that challenge. Annika has none. For her fund, the documents behind Halvorsen are a residual: a source of idiosyncratic risk the fund diversifies across 600 issuers rather than reads.

The sell order went out as an RFQ at 8:02 to five dealers, because about nine in ten of the fund's orders now go out electronically. Man Group reported in 2023 that Man Numeric had moved from roughly 30% of its high-yield order flow on electronic venues to over 90% (*Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023 p.3*; Chapter 1 has the full quotation). The best bid came back at 77. The vendor composite at 8:02 said 80. Jules, the execution trader, hit the 77, and Monday's first prints were 77 and 78. The 77 was theirs.

The optimizer had assumed, for a B3 bond, a cost of three-quarters of a point. That number is not wrong on an ordinary day; it is an average over ordinary days, and the optimizer's trade list is a list of trades "if filled" at modelled cost. The transaction-cost line on Tuesday's report showed three points against the composite on \$8mm face, \$240,000, about a quarter of the fund's monthly alpha budget on a strategy targeting 100 basis points a year.

> **Math, Gently.** *A composite is an average of quotes, not a price.* A vendor composite is built from whatever indications the vendor collected, weighted by recency and by how much it trusts each contributor. At 8:02 on day 2, suppose Halvorsen's five inputs were 82 (a Friday quote nobody had refreshed), 81, 80, 79 and 78. Their average is 80. The live mid, the price at which a buyer and a seller who both knew the morning's news would meet, was nearer 79: so one point of the gap between 80 and the 77 fill is pure staleness. A second point is the dealer's half-spread, the fee for standing in the middle of a stressed market at all. The third is the dealer's charge for the chance that the seller knows something and for the days the bond will sit in inventory (the toxicity and inventory terms of Chapter 1's bid). So: 80 composite, minus 1 of staleness, minus 1 of half-spread, minus 1 of inventory and toxicity, gives 77. Annika's value signal, by contrast, had expected about 1.5 points of excess return from the name over the next quarter. Costs measured against a composite can easily be twice the alpha they are meant to protect, and the size of that ratio depends on the morning. Odd Lots' 2021 episode with Morningstar's Maciej Kowara and Eric Jacobson (listed in the Resource Graph) is a good forty minutes on why two honest pricing services can disagree about the same bond. The full version, with the half-spread derived and the toxicity priced, is Chapter 2-B and §18.3.

Theo calls Annika on day 4, after the bonds have bounced to 81. They had met at a conference panel where she asked him what a springing lien was, and he had explained it with a diagram she still has.

"We sold it Monday at 77," she says. "I didn't read the 8-K until Wednesday. It didn't matter."

"It was at 81 this morning."

"It was at 81 this morning for you. For me it's a bond the guard says I can't own, at any price, until the default probability comes back under the line. If it comes back, the model buys it again, probably higher. That's not a mistake. That's the cost of having a guard." She pauses. "You know what the expensive part was? Not the four points. The three points against the composite. That's the number my risk committee is going to ask me about on Thursday, and the question will be 'why is this number different from last month?'"

The systematic seat, seen from Theo's chair, is a price taker. Its horizon is months, the length of a factor's half-life. Its cost of trading is the dealer's spread, which on a bad morning is set by the Danas of §4C.1, and the constraint that actually moves it is its own risk budget, written in advance and enforced without discussion. That last property is the source of the seat's value and its predictability. Annika's fund will sell any name the guard flags, on the morning after the guard flags it, without reading the documents. A desk that knows the guard exists knows something about Monday morning's supply. The limit sheet that formalizes the same kind of constraint for Theo's own desk is §8B.9, and §11.6 explains why a defensive high-yield strategy's guard rail excludes exactly the CCC-bound names that Halvorsen was becoming.

> **Trend Watch.** *Systematic credit is now bought, not built (2020 to 2026).* Blackstone bought the systematic credit manager DCI in 2020. Ares took a minority stake in BlueCove in 2023, agreed in October 2025 to buy the whole firm, about \$5.5bn of assets and some 60 staff at 30 September 2025, and completed the acquisition in early February 2026, forming Ares Systematic Credit within its credit group (Business Wire, 3 Feb 2026; fi-desk.com and Alternative Credit Investor, 30 Oct 2025). Ares cited insurance distribution and "increased interest in systematic credit." For the desk this changes who sits in Annika's chair. The systematic seat increasingly lives inside an alternative-credit platform that also owns a direct lender (§4C.6) and runs CLOs (§4C.4), so the same logo can sell Halvorsen on Monday from its systematic sleeve and be asked to finance Halvorsen's subsidiary by Thursday from its private one. Chapter 4-B's scorecard tracks the deals.

## 4C.3 Monday, 10:00 a.m.: The Multi-Strategy RV Pod

*This seat is a fictional composite of a capital-structure pod inside a multi-manager fund. Owen Achterberg and his platform, Vantress Capital Management, are invented. D. E. Shaw appears only through its own published research and press releases, and its public credit record is structured and private credit, not flow high yield; the pod below is generic.*

Chapter 1's View From Another Seat box left the pod on Friday afternoon, choosing its hand. By Monday at ten Owen Achterberg had three hands on the whiteboard in his pod's corner of a floor that holds forty pods and one coffee machine everybody complains about.

The first was long the term loan and short the 7.25% notes. The two rank pari passu under the intercreditor, so in a clean bankruptcy they recover the same. The trade is a bet that the restructuring will not be clean: that whoever provides the new money will invite term lenders and leave noteholders outside, so that "pari passu" becomes a description of the past. The second was long the notes and long CDS protection: a basis trade, with the CDS paying if Halvorsen defaults and the bond earning its cheapness if it does not (Chapter 24 prices it). The third was long the 7.25% notes against a short in the unsecured 6% notes due 2031: a bet that the secured paper recovers far more than the unsecured in any outcome, and that the market had priced the two as if the patents were already gone.

Owen's analysts had spent the weekend in the documents, which a pod can do because nobody is waiting for it to quote. Their reading was that the market was over-extrapolating from the last three drop-downs it had seen. The 8-K described new money at the subsidiary that held the patents. But moving the patents beyond the reach of the term lenders and noteholders would need investment-basket capacity that, on their reading of the 2021 agreement, was smaller than the headlines assumed, and the pari passu intercreditor gave the term lenders and the noteholders the same reasons to fight it. Theo had cut his recovery for the notes from 40 to 15 on Sunday night (Chapter 8-B); Owen's team thought 15 was the price of a drop-down that was more likely to stall than close.

So Owen took the third hand. On day 3 he bought \$10mm of the 7.25s from Dana at 79 and sized a short in the 6% 2031s so that the pair would lose almost nothing on the day of a default. That is Chapter 3-B's jump-to-default hedge, built from two bonds of one issuer instead of a bond and its CDS, and it left Owen long the gap between secured and unsecured recoveries. The box at the end of this section does the arithmetic and enters the trade in the Pair Book as PB-02.

By day 12 the notes were 74, and the pair was losing money on a day when nothing in Owen's documents had changed. The selling he had modelled was the CLO selling that follows a downgrade to Caa1: he expected it at month-end, after the agencies, and planned to add to the long into it. The agencies moved on day 9, the CLO managers moved on day 11 (§4C.4), and the selling arrived a week before his model said it would. Owen did not add. He did not sell either.

He did not have to, and the reason is the architecture of his seat rather than the strength of his conviction. A multi-manager allocates capital to pods with drawdown limits measured against the pod's whole allocation, not against a single position, and it matches the capital to the expected horizon of what the pod does. A loss of a couple of hundred thousand dollars on a pod allocated several hundred million is a bad week, not a stop. Dana's desk, with a two-day clock on gapped inventory, could not wait to find out whether Owen was right. Owen's pod could wait a quarter.

The cleanest public example of this seat's logic comes from a firm whose credit business looks quite different from flow high yield. D. E. Shaw's research note *Assessing U.S. Mortgage-Backed Credit Stress in 2020* describes how, over March and April 2020, "we purchased in excess of \$400 million in CAS and STACR bonds at a significant discount to par," the credit-risk-transfer bonds issued by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. The sellers were asset managers and REITs that, in the note's words, "were put under stress by redemption requests and margin calls." The firm's judgment was about the analogue: it treated the 2001 recession as a better guide than the 2008 crisis and concluded that market participants "were over-relying on experiences from the GFC." The structure is Owen's: a seat with patient capital, a loan-level loss model and no obligation to quote, buying from seats whose own constraints had fired. The same firm's private-credit funds turn the idea into a product. D. E. Shaw's May 2025 press release on its \$1.3 billion Diopter Fund II describes "capital optimization investments, which are designed to help financial institutions enhance balance-sheet and business-model efficiency." Banks' capital constraints are, for that seat, the supply of risk. The dealer's leash becomes the pod's edge.

Same tape, opposite trades. On the morning of day 2, Annika sold Halvorsen because her guard said she could not own it. > **The Pair.** *Pair Book entry PB-02, Owen Achterberg's pod at Vantress Capital Management (fictional composites; Halvorsen is the book's fictional issuer; positions, prices, fees and P&L are illustrative).* **Thesis.** The secured 7.25s recover far more than the unsecured 6% 2031s in any outcome, because the drop-down needs basket capacity the 2021 agreement lacks; at 79 and 55 the market prices the patents as already gone. **Horizon.** To the plan vote, inside the pod's one-year clock (§4C.1), or to the day the drop-down closes. **Legs.** Long \$10mm face of the secured notes at 79. ## 4C.4 Day 9 to Day 12: The CLO Manager

*This seat is a fictional composite of a mid-sized loan manager running eleven CLOs. Rachel Imbert and her team are invented. Kundu's paper, Eagle Point's shareholder letter, Loomis Sayles' quarterly note and the Fifth Circuit's Serta opinion are real and cited.*

> **Quant-shop life (a fictional composite).** Nobody at Rachel Imbert's firm describes month-end as exciting. On the 22nd of each month the compliance team runs the firm's own version of every test in every indenture, and on the 25th the trustee sends its version, and for most of the following two days the job is making the two agree. This month they disagree about one CLO's junior OC ratio by 0.07 percentage points. The cause, found at 4:15 p.m. on the second day by an analyst named Wes who has been at the firm for eleven months and has already found three of these, is a loan rated B3 by Moody's and CCC+ by S&P. The indenture classifies a loan as CCC if *either* agency says so; the internal system was coded, years ago, to use Moody's. Wes writes a ticket for the system vendor. The ticket joins a queue that also contains a request, filed in 2024, to make the WARF calculator handle withdrawn ratings. Rachel signs the monthly report at 6 p.m. and goes home. The report is forty-one pages long and will be read in full by the trustee, two rating agencies and, on a slow afternoon, one equity investor.

Mags, hearing Theo describe this on the phone, puts down her coffee. "Back in my day the CLO manager was a man named Gary with a fax machine. He computed his WARF by hand on the 25th, from a binder, and if the number was bad he computed it again until it wasn't." She thinks about it. "The trustee report is better. It's the only document in this market where a buyer tells everyone, every month, exactly how close it is to being forced to sell. People pay a lot of money to learn less than that about a hedge fund."

The agencies cut Halvorsen's term loan to Caa1 on day 9. Rachel's flagship CLO, \$500mm of loans, holds \$3mm of it. Her CCC bucket had been at 7.3%, which is high for her and slightly above the market's average, but inside the 7.5% limit that Kundu (ESRB Working Paper No. 141, 2023) describes as "typically set to 7.5%." The downgrade moves \$3mm of par into the bucket, which now holds \$39.5mm, or 7.9%. The limit allows \$37.5mm. There is \$2mm of excess.

An excess does not fail anything by itself. What it does is change how the pool is counted. "The loans in excess of this percentage are marked at the lowest market value of the loans in the CCC/Caa1 bucket," Kundu writes, and the cheapest CCC in Rachel's pool is not Halvorsen. It is a loan to a dental-practice roll-up, quoted at 58. So the arithmetic that decides Rachel's next move has nothing to do with Halvorsen's credit. It has to do with a dentist.

> **Math, Gently.** *The OC test, and why the haircut picks the wrong loan.* An overcollateralization test is a ratio: what the loans are worth, counted the indenture's way, divided by the debt that ranks at and above a given tranche. If the ratio falls below its trigger, cash that would have gone to the equity is diverted to pay down the senior notes, so the equity holders, who often include the manager, care a great deal. The numerator, in Kundu's statement of it, is par, plus the recovery value of defaulted loans, plus the purchase price of discount obligations, minus the CCC excess adjustment. The last term is the one at work here. Rachel's \$2mm of excess is counted at 58 instead of 100, which takes $2 \times (1 - 0.58) = \$0.84$mm out of the numerator. She has three ways to make the excess disappear. Selling \$2mm of the dentist at 58 turns \$2mm of par into \$1.16mm of cash, a loss of \$0.84mm: no better than doing nothing. Selling \$2mm of Halvorsen at 77 loses \$0.46mm. Selling \$2mm of her best CCC, a software loan downgraded last spring that has since recovered to 86, loses only \$0.28mm. The cheapest way out is to sell the *best* loan in the bucket, because the rule penalizes the excess at the *worst* price. Kundu calls this behaviour contractual arbitrage. Put in proportion, \$0.84mm against debt of about \$465mm is 0.18 percentage points of OC, out of a cushion of roughly 4 points; Kundu finds that "the median CLO operates within 4% of the most stringent covenant threshold." One name never breaks the test on its own. A downgrade wave in one sector, landing on many names in the same month, can. The full version, with the tests written as an option book, is §21.4.

Kaz, who has been listening on speaker, asks whether "OC" stands for over-collateralized or over-caffeinated. Nobody answers, which is an answer.

Rachel sells the software loan at 86 on day 11 and keeps Halvorsen. Her own analyst likes Halvorsen's term loan at 77: it is secured, the 2021 credit agreement is tighter than most of the vintage, and the drop-down needs consents that the pari passu intercreditor makes hard to buy. From the outside her trade looks perverse, a manager selling a recovering credit and keeping a collapsing one. From inside the indenture it is the only trade that minimizes the haircut, and it happens to agree with her credit view.

Figure 4-C.3 shows why that trade is the cheap one, and why other managers cure the same kind of excess by selling Halvorsen.

Three other managers who own Halvorsen's loan face the same arithmetic with different inventories. In their pools, Halvorsen is the *highest*-priced CCC, so for them the cheapest cure is to sell Halvorsen. They sell on days 11 and 12, into the month-end determination date, and the loan trades down to the low 70s. The notes, pari passu with the loan and arbitraged against it by every pod that can hold both, follow it to 74. Ines's desk shows bids in the loan on day 12 and buys some. It is competing with sellers who are not trying to get the best price. They are trying to get out at the cheapest approved moment, and the moment is set by a calendar the whole market can read.

That is the seat's paradox, and it is the most useful idea in this chapter for a liquidity provider. The CLO is the market's biggest and most rule-bound buyer of leveraged loans, and therefore its most predictable seller. Chapter 1's box on the same seat gave Kundu's sample averages (CCC loans at 85, the cheapest CCC at 58) and the S&P finding that a CCC bucket above 7.5% rarely means a failing test; the bucket is a soft wall. In early 2026 the wall was well back from most managers: Thomas Majewski's Eagle Point letter (18 Feb 2026) put market-wide CCC exposure in CLOs at an average 4.3% and junior OC cushions at 3.9%. Averages hide the vintages, though, and Halvorsen's CLO holders are the 2021 vintage that bought the loan at issue. The desk's version of Mags's insight is a number: for each loan, the face held by CLOs weighted by how close each of those CLOs is to its CCC limit and its junior OC trigger, read from last month's trustee reports. §4C.9 builds it. On day 8, before the downgrade, Halvorsen's version of that number was already high.

Managers also vote, which puts the seat on both sides of Part IV's fights. Loomis Sayles' loan team expected the risk of liability management exercises to "remain elevated as not all these maturities are refinanceable" (Q4 2025 letter, January 2026). In Serta's 2020 uptier, which the Fifth Circuit held on 31 December 2024 "was not a permissible open market purchase," the prevailing lenders included Barings, Eaton Vance and Invesco's senior loan arm, and the objectors included the "LCM Lenders" (that LCM's vehicles were CLOs is background knowledge, not something the opinion states). Part of the reason is structural: CLO indentures make equity from an exchange awkward to hold, so managers lean toward deals that hand them first-out loans. Rachel already knows which kind of offer she can accept. §21.8 follows CLOs into the LMT.

## 4C.5 Day 12: The Life Insurer

*This seat is a fictional composite of a mid-sized life insurer's general account. Harriet Lomax and her committee are invented. The Federal Reserve note, the Sidley summary of the NAIC's 2026 rules, and the public remarks of Apollo's and UBS's leaders are real and cited.*

The quarterly portfolio review of a life insurer's general account has a printed agenda, and the agenda has eleven items, and Halvorsen is not one of them. It appears as a sub-bullet of item 7, "Structured credit watch list," because the insurer does not own the 7.25% notes. It owns \$25mm of the BBB− tranche of Rachel Imbert's flagship CLO, the one with \$3mm of Halvorsen's term loan inside it, and a \$40mm private placement issued by one of Halvorsen's competitors, which, if anything, is having a good month.

Harriet Lomax, the chief investment officer, reads item 7 aloud. The CLO's junior OC cushion is down 0.2 points on the month; its CCC bucket is above the limit and being managed; the tranche's rating is unchanged; the trustee's next report is due on the 25th. The committee asks two questions, both about the capital charge. Then it moves to item 8. Nothing happens to Halvorsen in this room on day 12, and that is the point of the scene.

Theo, who sat through a version of this meeting as an adviser once and remembers the biscuits, asks Harriet afterwards why nobody mentioned the price.

"Which price?" she says. "We hold the tranche at amortized cost. It's designated by the NAIC, it's in our risk-based capital at a factor for that designation, and our liabilities are annuities that pay out over twenty years. If the tranche fell ten points tomorrow and stayed BBB−, nothing in my world would move except a footnote. If it went to BB+ and stayed at par, a great deal would move." She taps the agenda. "I don't sell when a price changes. I sell when a designation changes."

> **Math, Gently.** *Accounting is a filter laid over a price.* Take the same 92-to-78 move and run it through three ledgers. Mark-to-market (Theo's desk, the systematic fund, the dealer) books −14 on day 2. Amortized cost (Harriet's general account, and the man in the cold open who "owns this bond at 99") books 0, and keeps booking 0 until somebody decides the holding is impaired, at which point it books most of the loss in one step. A fund that marks quarterly (a BDC, §4C.6) books nothing until the quarter-end valuation, and then often books only part of the move, because valuers anchor to the last mark. Suppose the quarterly mark moves halfway toward the market each quarter: a true price that falls from 92 to 70 and stays there is reported as 92, then 81, then 75.5, then 72.75. The measured quarterly changes are −11, −5.5 and −2.75, and their volatility is much lower than the single −22 that actually happened. The total, eventually, is the same −22. Smoothing changes when a loss is recognized and how volatile a series looks. It does not change how much was lost. The full version, with the smoothing written as a moving-average filter and unsmoothed by Geltner's method, is §17.2.

The question Harriet's committee asked twice was capital per unit of spread, and it is the question that explains why her seat exists at all.

> **Math, Gently.** *Spread per unit of capital.* An insurer does not compare two bonds by their spreads. It compares spread divided by the capital each bond consumes. Suppose a BBB private placement pays Treasuries plus 250 basis points and carries a risk-based capital factor of about 1.3% of its value, while a BB public bond pays Treasuries plus 300 and carries about 3.5% (illustrative numbers in roughly the shape of the NAIC's factor table). Per point of capital charge, the private pays $250 / 1.3 \approx 190$ basis points and the public $300 / 3.5 \approx 86$. The bond with the lower spread is more than twice as good a use of the insurer's capital. Now apply the same lens to Harriet's tranche. A Federal Reserve note (Carlino, Foley-Fisher, Heinrich and Verani, 21 Mar 2025) found that "insurers holding a portfolio of B-rated loans can cut their risk-based capital charges by two-thirds if they package those loans into a CLO and purchase the entire CLO capital stack." Same loans, same spread, one-third of the capital: three times the ratio. That is not a loophole anyone hid. It is a property of a rulebook that charges each tranche by its rating, and it is why life insurers' exposure to CLOs reached, in the note's words, "a record \$212 billion by the end of 2023." The full version, with the factor table and the 2026 changes, is in Chapter 22 and §17.5.

The firms that built the largest version of this seat describe it in these terms. Marc Rowan, on Apollo's fourth-quarter 2025 call (9 Feb 2026): "Spread is created by having an asset origination machine, by having a liability origination machine at a low cost, and by having a low OpEx." Jim Zelter, on the same call, put Athene's net invested assets at \$292 billion at year-end 2025, with a net spread of about 120 basis points. Critics describe the same machine with a different adjective. UBS chairman Colm Kelleher, in November 2025: "If we look at the insurance business, to me, there is a looming systemic risk coming through." Rowan's reply, reported by Bloomberg on 24 November: "People have really just lost their minds, and the headlines get more and more hysterical and have almost nothing to do with the substance." The book takes no side. Both men are describing a seat whose behaviour is set by a capital rule, and capital rules have dates.

Harriet's date is 31 December. Her tranche is BBB−, one notch above the line, and it is about 3% thick. Under the factors the NAIC adopted in June 2026, effective for year-end 2026 reporting, a below-investment-grade broadly syndicated CLO tranche 4% thick or thinner carries an 11.77% pretax surcharge, with thickness measured on the latest trustee report (Sidley, 9 Jul 2026). One notch on Rachel's CLO, in December, would change the capital cost of Harriet's position by far more than any price move in Halvorsen could. The seat does not watch Halvorsen. It watches Rachel's CCC bucket, through the trustee report, and the agencies' calendar. When it sells, it sells all at once, into a market that has been reading the same calendar.

> **Trend Watch.** *Insurers' capital rules for CLOs (2023 to 2027).* Life insurers' BSL and middle-market CLO exposure reached a record \$212bn at end-2023 (Federal Reserve note, 21 Mar 2025). On 23 June 2026 the NAIC's risk-based-capital working group adopted new C-1 factors for CLOs based on rating and tranche thickness, effective for year-end 2026, keeping a 45% charge on residual tranches and adding the 11.77% surcharge on thin below-IG BSL tranches; from year-end 2027 an overcollateralization-based framework replaces the uniform charge on collateral loans (Sidley, 9 Jul 2026; Mayer Brown, Jul 2026). For the desk, each change is a date on which one seat's demand curve shifts, and the regulatory calendar is as tradable as an earnings calendar, provided one knows who holds what.

So the insurer is the slowest money in the chapter: a horizon of decades, a trigger that is a letter grade checked at year-end, and selling that is rare and discontinuous, nothing for years and then everything, on a date anyone could have circled.

Danny Ostrowski was an associate at the next desk over at Theo's old firm, and he still builds slides as if a partner will check the footnotes. Halvorsen's sponsor has asked his platform to provide \$200mm of new money at the subsidiary that holds the patents, which is the financing Theo read about in the 8-K on the first Friday. The platform already knows the neighbourhood: it holds a \$35mm piece of a unitranche to one of the sponsor's other companies, and its liquid-credit sleeve owns \$15mm of Halvorsen's term loan.

The marks on the private book move by a different clock. In December 2023 Ares Capital carried its first-lien loan to Pluralsight at 89.0 while Blue Owl carried the same loan at 96.8; six months later Ares's mark was 48.0 (ARCC 10-Q filings; §17.3 tells the rest). Danny's seat has no daily price at all for its private loans, which is what makes it patient, and a quarterly queue, which is what makes it, now and then, impatient in public markets. §17.6 turns Theo's thirteen-week model into the runway that measures how many quarters a queue like this can last.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The agent bank, and the sponsor.* Two parties shape this month without owning the risk the other seats own. The administrative agent keeps the register, sends the notices and moves the money; in a drop-down it is the agent's instructions that decide who is paid and when. The public record shows how much rides on that plumbing. In August 2020 Citibank, as agent on Revlon's 2016 term loan, mistakenly wired principal to lenders; the recipients in the litigation received "approximately \$500 million" while the loan's participations "were trading at 20% to 30% of the face amount," and on 8 September 2022 the Second Circuit vacated the judgment that had let them keep it (Citibank v. Brigade Capital Management, No. 21-487). The sponsor is the one owner who wrote the documents. Its equity is a call option on Halvorsen's value, and the 2021 credit agreement's baskets are flexibility it bought and paid for in the coupon. A drop-down is the sponsor exercising that flexibility before the option expires worthless. Chapter 6-B prices the call; Chapter 13 prices the baskets.

> **Trend Watch.** *Semi-liquid private credit meets its first net outflows (2025 to 2026).* The Federal Reserve's Financial Stability Report of 8 May 2026 put private credit at about \$1.4tn and recorded that accepted redemptions at perpetual BDCs "somewhat exceeded new inflows in the first quarter of 2026, the first time this has occurred since these vehicles were created," with requests "much larger than 5 percent." For the desk the change is in one seat's rule: a lender that used to be a pure holder now has a queue, and the queue's release valve is the syndicated loan and bond market where the desk trades.

## 4C.7 Day 16: The Opportunistic Credit Fund

*This seat is a fictional composite of a dislocation fund inside a large alternative-asset manager with an affiliated insurer. Celeste Varga is invented. Apollo appears only through its own published words and filings.*

On day 16 Dana Rourke, the dealer from §4C.1, has a problem she did not want: a \$40mm block of the 7.25% notes, assembled that morning from two sellers whose rules fired after the day-9 downgrade. One is a separately managed account whose guidelines bar anything rated Caa. The other is a CLO whose indenture, like many, allows a small bucket of senior secured bonds, and whose manager is curing its CCC excess the cheapest way available. Neither seller has a view on Halvorsen. Dana has called four accounts and found \$6mm of interest at 73. She calls Celeste Varga, who never returns calls until she wants something, and who picks up on the second ring.

Celeste bids 72 for all forty.

Her fund's money is committed capital with a five-year life, and its sister vehicles sit on the balance sheet of an affiliated annuity writer. She does not mark to the screen and she does not need to sell, which is the "principal's mindset" Marc Rowan described on Apollo's 9 February 2026 call (Chapter 1 quotes it in full). Her first question to her analysts is the one her platform asks of everything: can a structure turn this single-B into something an insurer can own? Apollo's president Jim Zelter described his firm's 2025 debt origination as "approximately 80% IG with an average rating of single A," and Rowan put it at "350 basis points over treasuries with an average rating of BBB." That is the business when it works: find a borrower's single-B risk, carve out a senior, collateralized piece, and sell the insurer a BBB at a spread a public BBB does not pay.

The analysts come back in an hour with the answer Theo would have given in his old job. The pari passu intercreditor means no new senior piece can be carved out of the first-lien collateral without the consent of the class, and the class is about to become a negotiating party. The structuring option is a Chapter 12-B problem, not a trade. Celeste buys anyway. At 72, on her firm's model of what the first lien recovers in a restructuring the first-lien group controls, she is paid for the wait.

> **Math, Gently.** *The LTV ladder is a set of call spreads.* Apollo's public strategy pages slice a capital structure by loan-to-value: credit at 0–50%, hybrid value at 50–80%, private equity at 80–100%. A claim that sits between an attachment point $A$ and a detachment point $D$ in the capital structure is paid nothing until the firm's value $V$ passes $A$, is paid in full once $V$ passes $D$, and is paid dollar for dollar in between. Its payoff is $\max(V - A, 0) - \max(V - D, 0)$: long a call struck at $A$, short a call struck at $D$. For Halvorsen's first-lien class (\$1,400mm of term loan and notes behind a \$150mm ABL, ignoring the collateral split and the DIP that §12B.9 adds back), $A = 150$ and $D = 1{,}550$. At issue, with an enterprise value near \$2.8bn, the class ran from about 5% to 55% LTV, almost all of it in the credit bucket. At a market-implied value of \$1.3bn on day 16, it runs from about 12% to 119%: the same claim now spans all three of Apollo's buckets. A downgrade is a claim climbing the ladder. A tiny check: if $V$ lands at 1,000, 1,300 or 1,700 with probabilities 0.3, 0.5 and 0.2, the class receives 850, 1,150 or 1,400, which is 61, 82 or 100 cents, and about 79 cents on average, before time, fees and priming. The "jump" in a drop-down is a moved strike: new money ahead of the class on the patents raises $A$. The full version is Chapter 6-B, and §12C.2 writes the fulcrum as the probability that $V$ lands between a class's strikes.

Ines watches the block print at 72 on the tape and turns to Theo. "She isn't calling your bluff. She's playing a different table and happens to be sitting at yours." Celeste's price is not a forecast of the next print. It is a function of her funding: what her capital costs, how long it is locked, and what her insurer affiliate would pay for whatever comes out of the restructuring. That is why the seat is, in the dossier's phrase, price-insensitive in a rally and slow to sell in a drawdown, and why it is the natural buyer when rule-bound holders have to sell.

The public record shows the same seat in other people's restructurings. In Carvana's July 2023 exchange, Apollo's deputy CIO of credit John Zito was quoted in the company's release (8-K Ex. 99.1) as pleased to support the agreement, "Working with Carvana, PIMCO, Ares and the ad hoc group of noteholders"; holders of more than 90% of the unsecured notes signed. And in November 2025 Optimum Communications sued six lenders in a cooperation agreement, Apollo, Ares, GoldenTree, Loomis, Oaktree and PGIM, alleging a group boycott (*Optimum Communications, Inc. v. Apollo Capital Management L.P.*, S.D.N.Y. No. 1:25-cv-09785); those are allegations, not findings, and §14.5 treats the co-op as a commitment device. The block buyer of day 16 is, by day 30, a member of a coalition. §16.2 models forced selling as a supply process from her side of the trade.

### When to apply it, and when it breaks

The table earns its keep where the rules are mechanical and the holdings are visible. CLO-routed loans are the best case. Trustee reports arrive monthly, the tests are written in the indenture, and the gap between "close to the limit" and "selling" is short and predictable, which is why a forced-seller score built from them can be traded. Insurer-held private placements are close to the worst case. Holdings arrive with a lag through statutory filings, designations can change on the regulator's discretion rather than an agency's, and the selling, when it happens, comes in large, rare blocks that a monthly score will not anticipate. Direct lenders sit in between: the queue is published each quarter, but which public assets get sold to meet it is the manager's choice.

The other way the table breaks is when it is filled in with logos. The same houses sit on opposite sides of different deals. Serta's prevailing lenders were loan managers, and so were its objectors; several of the Optimum co-op defendants have been the uptier's beneficiaries elsewhere. A seasoned practitioner looking at a new name checks three things before forming a view of the credit: who holds it, by vehicle rather than by firm; how close each vehicle is to its own rule; and the date on which that rule is next checked.

> **Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality.** *Myth:* the smart money disagreed with us about Halvorsen. *Reality:* most of the money that moved did not have an opinion. It had a test date.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** *Modelling the seat from the logo.* A forced-seller score that keys on manager names instead of vehicle-level state is wrong in both directions. It flags a firm's well-cushioned 2024 CLO because the same firm's 2019 vintage is at its limit, and it misses a manager that sold before the downgrade because its analyst read the 8-K. CCC tests are per vehicle. So are OC cushions, redemption queues and fund lives. The logo is a filing convention.

### The seat state vector

ROBO's first attempt, in the cold open, modelled every holder as a copy of the desk. What Theo needs instead is a record per holder per instrument that says what the holder is forced to do and when, and a score that adds those records up into one number per instrument per day. Chapter 21 uses the score as `forced_pressure` (§21.4, §21.7), and Kundu's paper is, in effect, its empirical justification: a fire sale is predictable when you know which holders are near their constraints.

The listing defines two things. The first is a record, `SeatState`, with the holder's vehicle, its seat type, its face in the instrument, the as-of date of the source the record came from (a trustee report, a statutory filing, a fund's tender notice), the next date its binding rule is checked, the name of that rule, and the slack, the distance to the threshold in the rule's own units. The second is a function, `forced_pressure`, that takes all the records for one instrument, the instrument's outstanding face, whether it is currently rated CCC, and today's date, and returns a number between 0 and 1: the share of the issue held by vehicles that are near a binding constraint with a test date inside the horizon, weighted by how near. It is built per vehicle because the tests are per vehicle, and it discards stale records because a trustee report from two months ago describes a holder that may no longer exist. The tightness function decays exponentially in slack, so a CLO at its limit counts fully and one with two points of cushion counts about a third.

Use it for loans and secured bonds held in CLOs, where the inputs are monthly and the rule is mechanical. It is a poor fit for insurer holdings and private placements, for the reasons above, and it says nothing about fundamentals; a high score is a statement about supply, to be paired with a view of value. The edge case that bites is the pre-emptive seller. A manager who sells the week before a downgrade still appears in last month's trustee report as a holder, with slack, so the score counts selling that has already happened. Cross-checking against TRACE volume since the report date catches most of it.

## 4C.10 Desk Debrief

- A bond has one price and seven ledgers. The loss from 92 to 70 is the same in all of them; what differs is when each ledger admits it.
- Every owner other than the desk is a rule with a date: a guard, a limit, a CCC bucket, a capital factor, a tender cap, a fund life. The desk's edge is not a better opinion of the credit but a better map of which rule binds the seller, and when it fires next.
- Horizon can flip the sign of a shared opinion. Expected drift grows with time and the typical swing only with its square root, so the seat with the tighter stop and the shorter clock sells what the patient seat buys.
- A rule-bound buyer is a predictable seller. The CLO's trustee report publishes its distance to the wall every month, and the cleanest entry is often just after the test date.
- A pair isolates the idio on the day it was sized for. Before that day it leaks the ruler, the borrow, the carry and the other seats' calendars; run the seat table down each leg, because forced selling arrives one leg at a time.
- The seat map is the coalition map. Who was forced to sell, and who was patient enough to buy, decides who sits in which class when the grace period starts.

**The Spec You Hand the Robot.** For every loan and secured bond on the desk's screen, build one `SeatState` record per holding vehicle from the latest trustee reports, with face, as-of date, rule, slack in the rule's own units and the next test date. Each morning, compute `forced_pressure` per instrument (Appendix F), drop records older than 45 days, and print the five names with the highest score next to their TRACE volume since the as-of date. A high score is a statement about supply, not value; pair it with the credit view before trading it.

### Exercises

1. *The cheapest cure.* A CLO with a \$400mm pool has a CCC bucket of 8.4% against a 7.5% limit, and its cheapest CCC loan is quoted at 55. It can cure the excess by selling a CCC loan at 90 or one at 70. What does each option cost the OC numerator, compared with doing nothing? *Solution.* The excess is \$3.6mm, haircut by $3.6 \times 0.45 = \$1.62$mm if kept. Selling \$3.6mm at 90 costs \$0.36mm; at 70, \$1.08mm. The manager sells the better loan, because the rule charges the excess at the worst price.
2. *When does drift win?* With an expected drift of 9 points a year and daily noise of 1.5 points, at what horizon does the expected gain equal one typical swing? *Solution.* Solve $0.036\,T = 1.5\sqrt{T}$: $\sqrt{T} \approx 41.7$, so $T \approx 1{,}740$ trading days, about seven years. Even the patient pod is not counting on drift to beat noise inside its horizon. It is counting on not being forced out before the drift arrives.
3. *Capital per spread.* An insurer can buy a BBB private placement at T+230 with a 1.3% factor or a BB public bond at T+340 with a 3.5% factor. Which is the better use of capital, and at what factor would the BB tie? *Solution.* $230/1.3 \approx 177$ against $340/3.5 \approx 97$, so the private. The BB ties at a factor of $340/177 \approx 1.9\%$.
4. *Kaz's hedge.* Kaz calls Owen's pair "fully hedged." Name two ways it loses money while Owen is right about the patents. *Solution.* A spread move before default (it was sized jump-to-default flat, not spread flat, and lost about \$200,000 on day 12), and a recall of the borrow on the 2031s, which forces a buy-in when unsecured holders organize. A rise in the financing haircut on the long is a third.
5. *Discount obligations.* Kaz assumes a "discount obligation" is a coupon the issuer is embarrassed about. What is it, and why might a CLO manager buy Halvorsen's loan at 72 on day 16 rather than sell it? *Solution.* A loan bought well below par is carried in the OC numerator at its purchase price, not at par, until it trades above a threshold (Kundu: typically 90) for more than 30 days. A manager with room in the CCC bucket buys it as cheap par that will count in full if the loan recovers.
6. *ROBO, again.* Theo asks ROBO to "flag the forced sellers in Halvorsen." What does ROBO build? *Solution.* A list of every account that sold, since every account that sold was, by definition, a seller. The precise version is the Spec above: forced is a statement about a vehicle's slack and test date before it sells, not about the fact that it did.
