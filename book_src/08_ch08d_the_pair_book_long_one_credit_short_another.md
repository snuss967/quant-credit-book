## Cold Open: The Weather Report

At 7:05 on Tuesday, 6 October 2026, the third week of this book's story clock, Wen Zhao opens the file she calls the weather.

It is a spreadsheet with one row for every borrowed bond in Dov Adler's book, refreshed each morning from the prime broker's lending feed and from a phone call Wen makes anyway, because the feed has been wrong twice. Each row carries the lender, the fee, the share of that lender's inventory of the bond that is already out on loan, and one column no feed provides: every recall notice the pod has received on that bond, dated, with its size and how many hours the prime broker took to find replacement bonds. Two years of that column make a time series. She calls it the weather because, like weather, it is public in principle, forecastable up to a point, and nothing anyone in the pod does will change it.

This morning the weather is on row seven. Row seven is the short leg of PB-19, the live descendant of the Ford replay that Chapter 8 used to show why a DTS-neutral pair carries nothing (PB-03, §8.4): long \$40 million market value of Ford Motor Credit senior unsecured notes, short \$60 million market value of GM Financial senior unsecured notes, about \$61 million face, borrowed from a single lender whose lendable inventory in that issue is \$300 million face *(illustrative throughout)*. Overnight the share on loan went to 85% and the fee was reset from 25 bp to 70 bp. The recall column has two new entries in ten days, \$5 million on 25 September and \$3 million on 1 October, and the second one took the prime broker a day and a half to cover.

Wen's rule of thumb comes from the one large study of the corporate-bond loan market. Asquith, Au, Covert and Pathak, with a large custodian lender's records for 2004–2007, found the fee roughly flat near 30 bp until about 70% of a lender's inventory is on loan, then rising by more than 10 bp for each further 10% (Asquith et al. 2013). At 85% that slope predicts something like 45 bp. Seventy is the slope plus somebody. She writes it in the margin in pencil: *70 > 45. Who else is short?*

Row three is Halvorsen's 7.25% notes due 2029, the short leg of PB-01, Dov's Kestrel pair: 50 bp, 61% on loan, no recalls. The following Tuesday it will be the only row anyone on the floor talks about (§1B.2), but this is not that Tuesday.

At 7:12 the x-ray arrives. Marcus Oyelaran runs the overnight factor x-ray for the platform's credit pods; he works for Gordon Pike, whose nine-minute call ends Dov's Tuesday in Chapter 1-B. Marcus never asks what a pod's view is. His emails are short and always end on the same kind of question.

*Book beta to CDX HY: +0.04 of gross (limit ±0.05). Rating-bucket factor: +0.11 of gross (limit ±0.10). Eight of your twelve live pairs are long the lower-rated name. PB-19 is the largest contributor. What is the book long that it did not mean to be?*

Dov reads both at 7:15, standing up, and does the arithmetic on the back of the card he must fill in by 7:30. The pair's thesis has been on the ledger since he opened it on 24 August, in his own handwriting, one sentence with a date in it: *Ford Credit's discount to GM Financial is a rating-bucket toll, not a credit gap, and half of it is gone by 31 March 2027.* If the gap between the two captives closes by 30 bp over the life of the trade, that is worth $4.5 \times 0.0030 \times \$40\text{mm} = \$540{,}000$ a year at a spread duration of 4.5. Against it, the borrow at 25 bp on \$60 million had cost \$150,000 a year; at 70 bp it costs \$420,000. Add \$160,000 for financing the long and about \$120,000 a year of two legs' half-spreads amortised over the expected hold, and the friction is \$700,000. Overnight the pair has gone from a thin positive expectation to a negative one, and nothing about Ford or GM has changed. The lender changed. Figure 8-D.1 sets the two mornings side by side.

He writes the choices on the card. *Cut the GM Financial short (and the long with it). Replace the borrowed bonds with GM CDS. Let the ratio drift and wait.*

"There's a fourth," Wen says, reading over his shoulder. "Do nothing, and let the lender decide for us."

"That's the third one with a worse ending."

Underneath the card sits the question the chapter answers: **what is this pair long that it did not mean to be, and who is going to charge for it?**

By the end of this chapter you will be able to open a pair the way Dov tries to: a thesis sentence with a date in it; a short leg named with its lender and fee, or its contract and basis; a ratio chosen from three rulers, with the reason; a size set by the stop or the borrow, whichever is smaller; and an exit written before the entry. ## 8D.1 Sourcing a Pair: The Thesis Sentence

Seven weeks earlier, on Monday 24 August, PB-19 began as a sentence. Dov was a dealer's high-yield trader before he was a PM, and the habit he kept is that a view without a date is a mood. The date in a thesis sentence is not a forecast. It is the day on which he has agreed, in advance and in writing, to admit he was wrong.

It came from something Bea Castellanos, the pod's documents analyst, had been saying since spring. Ford Credit and GM Financial are the captive lenders of two of Detroit's three manufacturers, and to a restructuring banker they are almost the same animal: each funds dealers and car buyers, borrows unsecured against a book of receivables, and lives by credit losses, used-car values and its parent's support. Yet on the desk's fictional run Ford Credit's notes traded wider of GM Financial's, per unit of DTS, than those differences seemed to explain. Bea's reading was that a slice of the gap was a rating-bucket toll, paid because some holders can own one bucket more easily than the other, and Dov had seen such a toll before. On 25 March 2020 S&P cut Ford to BB+ and Ford's paper became the headline of the fallen-angel wave; Chapter 1 tells that story in four acts (§1.8.2), the pod replayed it as PB-03 (opened in §8.4, closed on the basis in §24.7), and the lesson Dov took from the replay was that Ford's bonds can carry a discount that is about who is allowed to hold them rather than about Ford.

That is the RSSG shape of a good pair, and Theo would recognise it at once: a document, a maturity wall, a question about who is in the room. In the pod the instinct has to become a sentence that names what is long, what is short, and the day the claim expires.

Before he wrote it, Dov ran the intake, which the pod borrows from a long/short credit fund. Ironshield's process, as laid out in the Fixed + Floating write-up of its episode (E18, "Inside a Long/Short Credit Hedge Fund: Sizing, Shorts and LME Risk," 4 Aug 2026), is seven questions asked in a fixed order: does the name fit the fund; what does the business do and which numbers matter in its sector; what is changing; what is the bear case; what is the scenario-weighted return; what size does the downside support; and finally, long, short or neither. The write-up's own gloss is the part Dov copied onto the pod's intake sheet: "Running step 6 as a gate instead of a conclusion changes which names reach the model at all."

On 24 August Dov ran it as a conclusion. Steps one to five went well, as they do when the PM already likes the trade. Both issuers are large and liquid; what was changing, in Bea's reading, was nothing at either company and something in the holder base; the bear case was that the toll is a fair price for a real difference between the parents. Step five said the pair was worth about \$540,000 a year if half the gap closed. Step six, the size the downside supports, he answered at the end with the size he had already chosen, \$40 million of long against a DTS-matched \$60 million of short. Run as a gate, step six would have produced on 24 August the number Marcus would produce on 8 October, and would have saved him a Thursday.

Wen's question at the intake was about the other side. "Who lends it?" That day: one asset manager's agent lender, plenty of inventory, a fee of 25 bp. Nobody asked what the fee would be if the trade became popular.

> **View From Another Seat.** *A long/short credit fund, and a systematic fund.* A long/short credit fund off the platforms faces the same pair differently. The Fixed + Floating write-up of Ironshield's episode notes that a short "has to clear three hurdles", that the book "ends up net long", and that hedging high yield with high yield "removes the exposure he is paid to hold." Paid partly for owning credit, such a fund treats shorts as scarce tools and accepts a net long. A pod cannot: the platform will not pay for beta it can buy through an index, so the hedge must live inside the book even when the only short is an index, a CDS or an ETF with a basis. A systematic fund sees the same two captives as two rows in a cross-section. There is no thesis sentence; the pair exists only as the long and short ends of a residual-momentum sort, ranked on the firm-specific part of the parent's stock return (§10.5.4), DTS-matched by construction and resized every month. Its shorts are an optimiser's output constrained by a borrow feed, and its date is the signal's own decay. What it gives up is the document, which is where Bea found the trade.

## 8D.2 The Short-Leg Menu

At 7:31 Dov hands Wen the card with all three choices still on it and asks her to walk him through the menu, which is what the pod calls the six ways to be short a credit. She does it at the whiteboard, because the menu is the one thing a former banker, a former trader and a former sell-side analyst each think they already know.

At PJT, "short" was a word in a pitch. The deck said *the market is short the unsecured* and nobody asked who had lent the bonds. In the pod the short is a loan, and Wen starts there because it is the row most people get wrong. To short GM Financial's notes the pod's prime broker located the bonds (the convention allows about three days), borrowed them from the lender's agent, and sold them. The pod posted cash collateral of 102% of the bonds' value, marked to market every day, and earns a rebate on that cash. It owes the lender every coupon the bonds pay while they are out. And the loan is "on demand": it rolls overnight and can be recalled at any time, which is why the recall column exists (Asquith et al. 2013, Section 2). A borrowed bond is a library book the library can ask for at any moment, and the fee is the fine for keeping it.

> **Math, Gently.** *The rebate, or how a fee hides inside an interest rate.* When you borrow a bond you hand the lender cash as collateral, and the lender pays you interest on that cash, minus its fee. The interest it pays is the *rebate*. Picture a coat check that pays you a little less interest than the bank would; the difference is the ticket. With a cash rate of 4.00% *(illustrative)* and a fee of 0.30%, the rebate is $4.00\% - 0.30\% = 3.70\%$. On \$10 million of bonds you post \$10.2 million of cash and receive about $0.037 \times \$10.2\text{mm} \approx \$377{,}000$ a year, where the bank would have paid \$408,000; the \$31,000 gap is the fee. Now let the bond become hard to borrow and the fee rise to 5.00%. The rebate is $4.00\% - 5.00\% = -1.00\%$: you are paying interest on your own collateral, about \$102,000 a year, on top of the coupons you owe. A negative rebate is how the loan market says *special*. In general, $\text{rebate} = r^{\text{cash}} - \text{fee}$, and the pod's cost of a cash short is the coupon it owes, net of the rebate it earns. Appendix D (§D.3) has the full plumbing, including the repo route to the same short.

"The bond is the only short that is short exactly the thing," Wen says. "Everything else is short something nearby, and the distance is a basis with a mind of its own." Her table goes on the ledger under every pair.

| Short leg | What it is really short | The basis it brings | Where it runs out |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Borrowed bond | the exact bond | none on the bond; the fee, the recall and the risk of the bond going *special* (Asquith et al. 2013; Nashikkar and Pedersen 2007) | availability in exactly the names you most want to short; no public tape of bond short interest, and the SEC's 3 Dec 2025 order pushed Rule 10c-1a loan reporting to 28 Sep 2028 |
| Single-name CDS (buy protection) | the issuer's family of deliverable obligations | the CDS–bond basis, which moves with the bond's illiquidity, funding cost, counterparty risk and its lending fee (Bai and Collin-Dufresne 2019; Nashikkar, Subrahmanyam and Mahanti 2011); cheapest-to-deliver and manufactured events (§24.5, §24.8) | "only just over 100 US CDS names that trade with great regularity" and perhaps 200 more sporadically (Man_2022 p.10) |
| CDX or iTraxx | an index of issuers | the index–intrinsic basis, near zero in normal times and about 60 bp in 2008–09, plus a jump at each roll (Chapter 10-B); beta mismatch to one name | deep and almost entirely cleared, but it does not hedge one issuer's default |

Dov has known every row for years. What Wen adds is the sentence the pod writes on the ledger, and two years of them have settled into a pattern. An investment-grade name whose CDS trades goes short in the CDS, because the basis is cheaper to carry than the recall. A high-yield name with no CDS goes short in borrowed bonds with a recall plan beside it: the second lender, the size at which the pod buys in rather than re-borrows, and every coupon date owed. Beta goes in the index, the future or the ETF. Distress goes in neither, because the borrow costs what it costs around a bankruptcy and the CDS already prices the default; the pod shorts the junior claim in the same capital structure or does not trade.

That last row is where the banker's intuition and the borrow market collide. Asquith and his co-authors found an average fee of 33 bp (median 18 bp) in 2004–2007, small enough that most people stop listening before the bonds people actually want to short. A full-letter downgrade took the average fee from 38.4 bp to 52.3 bp. Bonds of companies that filed for bankruptcy cost 173 bp to borrow in the thirty days before the filing and 245 bp after it. The best short ideas from an RSSG seat (distress, a downgrade, weak documents, a maturity wall) are exactly the bonds whose fee goes from 30 bp to 200, and whose lenders want them back for the vote. **The fee is a function of the signal.** Its corollary, documented by Nashikkar and Pedersen (2007), is that bonds expensive to short also look rich against their CDS. The borrow fee and the basis are one variable seen from two sides, which is why Chapter 24 treats them together. Figure 8-D.2 draws the slope and these jumps.

"Now PB-19," Wen says. GM Financial is not distressed, so the fee's move from 25 to 70 bp is not the bankruptcy curve; it is the crowding curve, the Asquith slope plus whoever else has decided the same pair is cheap. Shorting the bonds at a Z-spread of 120 bp and a 70 bp fee costs the pod about 190 bp a year to be short the credit, \$1.14 million on \$60 million. Screen levels for five-year GM protection are around 110 bp. On the same notional that is \$660,000 a year, a saving of \$480,000, and the saving is not free. It is bought with an ISDA and a clearing account, with an auction instead of a bond on the day of a default, and with a basis that the pod will now own: 110 against 120 is a basis of −10 bp today, and nothing says it stays there. It is also bought with a question Wen writes before anything else: *which entity does the contract the dealers will quote actually reference, and are GM Financial's notes deliverable into it?* If the answer is the parent rather than the captive, the CDS leg is short a slightly different credit, and the basis is a parent–subsidiary spread that no screen quotes. The answer goes on the ledger as its own line, not in anyone's head.

At 8:40 Dov does not choose. He asks three dealers for indications on five-year GM protection, \$20 million each, a size at which a dealer can see a client doing something but not how much. The menu has told him what each short costs. It has not told him how much short he needs, and that depends on a ruler.

## 8D.3 Three Rulers, One Alpha

Marcus holds a ruler review with every credit pod once a month, in the small room with the broken blind; October's falls on Wednesday at 8:00. It is the least glamorous meeting on the platform. One page per pair, one question: if the market moved and nothing else did, what would this pair have made?

For PB-19 his page has four rows. A ruler is a rule for turning a dollar of long into dollars of short, and the three that every pod uses come straight out of Chapter 4-B and §8.4. The spread-duration (DV01) ruler matches the change in value for a one basis point move, $h = D^{\text{spr}}_L/D^{\text{spr}}_S$. The DTS ruler matches the change in value for a one percent *relative* move in spreads, $h = D^{\text{spr}}_L s_L/(D^{\text{spr}}_S s_S)$, which is §8.4's DTS-neutral pair. The empirical ruler trusts history: $h = (\hat\beta_L/\hat\beta_S)\,D^{\text{spr}}_L/D^{\text{spr}}_S$, where each $\hat\beta$ is a leg's trailing slope on the market's spread changes. Marcus adds a fourth, the platform's factor model, which picks the $h$ that leaves the least factor variance; with one relative-spread factor that is exactly DTS, and with more it is a compromise.

With both legs at a spread duration of 4.5, Ford Credit at 180 bp and GM Financial at 120 bp, and trailing 24-week betas that the noise has made look like 1.5 for Ford Credit and 0.7 for GM Financial when the truth (by construction of the illustration) is 1.8 and 1.2, the page reads like this *(illustrative; the factor-model row is the desk's fictional run)*:

| Ruler | Short GM Financial per \$1 of Ford Credit | Short on a \$40mm long | Spread carry, bp a year on the long |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: |
| Spread duration (DV01) | 1.00 | \$40.0mm | +60 |
| DTS | 1.50 | \$60.0mm | 0 |
| Empirical beta, 24 weeks | 2.14 | \$85.6mm | −77 |
| Platform factor model (adds a rating-bucket factor) | 1.65 | \$66.0mm | −18 |

The carry column bothers Dov first, because it pays the bills. The DV01 pair earns 60 bp a year, $180 - 1.00 \times 120$, for the oldest reason in credit: it is long the market. The DTS pair earns nothing, which is §8.4's point, and the beta pair *pays* 77 bp to be short the market, because the trailing window happened to catch the short leg in a quiet patch and turned a small estimated beta into a large short. Then Marcus turns the page over, and the back is the part of the meeting that people remember.

| P&L in \$000 on the \$40mm long | DV01 (1.00) | DTS (1.50) | Beta (2.14) |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: |
| Market widens 10% in relative terms (Ford Credit +18 bp, GM Financial +12 bp) | −108 | 0 | +138 |
| Both legs widen a parallel 10 bp | 0 | +90 | +205 |
| Idio: Ford Credit tightens 15 bp on its own | +270 | +270 | +270 |
| Idio: GM Financial widens 10 bp on its own | +180 | +270 | +385 |

"Read the third row first," Marcus says. Fifteen basis points of Ford-specific news is worth $4.5 \times 0.0015 \times \$40\text{mm} = \$270{,}000$ under every ruler, because the ruler only sizes the short. That is the alpha, the thing the platform pays for. Now read the first two rows, which are the market doing ordinary market things. Depending on the ruler, an ordinary day moves the pair by anything from −\$108,000 to +\$205,000. The alpha and the ruler error are the same size. Whatever a pod calls idio is whatever its ruler leaves behind, which is why Marcus asks pods not for their view but for their ruler.

Dov's ruler for PB-19 is DTS, and the best public experiment on the question backs him. Ben Dor, Dynkin and their Barclays Capital colleagues took weekly five-year CDS spreads on the constituents of CDX.NA.IG from November 2003 to December 2006, grouped 63 issuers into five industries, formed every one of the 392 same-industry pairs, gave each pair a random direction, and hedged it three ways: equal risky PV01 (the CDS version of the DV01 ruler), DTS, and a 24-week empirical beta (Ben_Dor pp.229–232). The DTS-hedged pairs had the lowest P&L volatility, 11.2 bp a week against 13.3 for PV01 and 18.0 for beta. Their hedge ratio moved least over time, with a standard deviation of 0.3 against 1.3 for beta. And the smallest share of their variance was systematic, 12.6% against 18.6% and 18.9%. The beta hedge won on exactly one measure: its P&L had the lowest average absolute correlation with the market, 0.11. That number is the trap.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Ben_Dor p.233:* "For an exaggerated illustration, imagine a simple strategy that randomly goes long or short one unit of the market each week at the flip of a coin. This strategy takes nothing but market risk; yet the correlation of its return with the market return over the long term will be zero."
>
> **Annotation.** Read this aloud when a backtest of a beta-hedged pair book reports a market correlation near zero. When the short leg's trailing beta drifts toward zero, the ruler divides by it and the short becomes enormous; if the estimate is wrong, the pair is a leveraged market position, long or short at random. Large errors with random signs wash out of a full-sample correlation and stay in the P&L. On a 2026 desk the test is not correlation but the systematic share of variance and the stability of the ratio, both of which Marcus prints, and the cure is a prior.

The cure comes from a second, more useful experiment: which better forecasts next quarter's beta, today's DTS ratio or last period's estimated beta? Regressed on the betas that were actually realised, the DTS ratio explained 36% of their variation over 26-week periods and 50% over 52 weeks, with a slope of about one and no significant intercept, while the prior-period beta explained 19% and 23%, and its coefficients said the best forecast was halfway between last period's beta and 1 (Ben_Dor p.236, Table 8.3). Betas mean-revert; DTS does not need to. So the authors re-ran the beta hedge with an adjusted beta, $(1+\hat\beta)/2$, and the standard deviation of the hedge ratio fell from 0.7 to 0.2 (Ben_Dor p.237, Table 8.2). That blend, with DTS as the prior and the data allowed to earn their deviation, is the pod's practice, and it is the shrinkage argument §8.4 already makes.

> **Math, Gently.** *Why dividing by a noisy small number is dangerous.* A hedge ratio is a fraction, and the empirical ruler puts an estimate in the denominator. Picture a seesaw whose pivot sits close to one end: a small slip of the pivot swings the long arm wildly. Suppose the short leg's true beta to the market is 0.5, so the right short is $1/0.5 = 2$ units per unit of market risk, and your 24-week estimate is off by 0.3 either way with equal odds. Half the time you estimate 0.8 and short $1/0.8 = 1.25$ units; half the time you estimate 0.2 and short $1/0.2 = 5$. The errors are symmetric in the estimate and wildly asymmetric in the hedge: on average you short 3.1 units against a right answer of 2, and in the bad half you are short 3 units of market you never meant to own. A noise of ±0.3 around a beta of 1.5 would barely matter ($1/1.2$ to $1/1.8$); the same noise around 0.5 is a different trade every month. That is why `pair_book.py` shrinks any trailing beta within 0.1 of zero to the DTS ratio and logs it, and why Ben Dor's $(1+\hat\beta)/2$ works: it moves the denominator away from the cliff. The full treatment of the estimator is §8.4 and §8.5.

The crisis is the part of the experiment that Dov does not quote at ruler reviews. The same authors re-ran the 392 pairs over 2007–2008 with 26-week betas. Volatility went up by an order of magnitude, to 159.1, 117.5 and 137.3 bp a week for PV01, DTS and beta. On the full set the systematic share was 21.6%, 25.6% and 41.1%, so DTS did not even have the lowest share; only with financials removed did it return to the top, at 19.2% (Ben_Dor p.273, Table 10.3). Figure 8-D.3 sets the calm years beside the crisis. The authors' summary is one line.

Marcus's last row is why the meeting runs long. His factor model has a rating-bucket factor that DTS does not see, and it says the Ford Credit leg carries more market than its spread alone implies; the factor ruler shorts 1.65 rather than 1.50, and at 1.65 the book's rating-bucket line would fall back inside its limit. Dov's answer is the one Marcus expected. "The bucket is the thesis. If I hedge the toll I've hedged the trade."

"Then the toll isn't idio," Marcus says. "It's a factor. If every pair long the lower-rated name earns it, the platform can buy it in one ticket, and it isn't going to pay you twenty percent of it. What I'll pay you for is Ford Credit being wider than *its bucket* says it should be. How much of your gap is that?"

Dov does not know, an honest answer and an expensive one. PB-19's hedge-method column, *DTS; rating-bucket factor left open*, now carries a number: of the book's 0.11 of rating-bucket exposure, PB-19 contributes 0.05 that its thesis claims and four other pairs contribute 0.06 that their theses never mention. Those four belong to the conversation Chapter 9 records from the week before, when the same x-ray found the book long value with extra steps; the resize that followed fixed the value line and left this one. PB-19's 0.05 is Dov's, and it will matter on Thursday.

## 8D.4 Two Jump Processes

While Marcus was printing Wednesday's page, the same pair was being reconstructed from the outside. Ines Okafor's market-making desk is not on Dov's platform and has never seen his book; it sees pods only through their RFQs.

At 4:40 on Tuesday Ines put three facts on the table. At 8:45 a client had asked for an indication on \$20 million of five-year GM protection, which is the size of a client asking several dealers for a third of something each. At 11:00 the desk had sold \$5 million of GM Financial notes it did not own and gone to borrow them for delivery, and the agent lender had quoted 70 bp with a note that most of the inventory was out. And nothing had happened to GM. "Somebody large is short GM Financial in borrow," she said, setting her coffee down like a chip. "Their lender is getting tired, and now they're pricing the way out. What's the hand?"

"Long Ford Credit against it," Theo said, before Kaz had uncapped a marker. "Same animal, other rating bucket. It's the pair a banker would pitch."

"Then let's price what they're holding." Kaz went to the whiteboard and wrote *Assume DTS-neutral, \$40mm long, \$60mm short*, and under it, out of habit, *let's just assume Gaussian*, and drew a bell. Then Kaz looked at the bell for a while and rubbed it out. "No. Each of those companies can default. A pair isn't one random variable with a spread. It's two jump processes with a hedge ratio between them."

> **Math, Gently.** *From a spread to a default rate, and from a default rate to an expected jump.* A credit spread is roughly the market's price for expected default losses, so it can be turned into a default rate. If a bond loses $1 - R$ of its value when the issuer defaults and the issuer defaults at rate $\lambda$ a year, the expected loss each year is $\lambda(1-R)$, and the spread that pays for it is $s \approx \lambda(1-R)$, so $\lambda \approx s/(1-R)$. Picture an insurance premium: the spread is the premium, the recovery is the deductible, and the intensity is how often the house burns down. A bond at 250 bp with 50% recovery implies $\lambda \approx 0.025/0.50 = 5\%$ a year; on \$100 of bonds that is a 5% chance of losing \$50, an expected \$2.50, which is the 250 bp. Kaz adds two cautions in the corner. This $\lambda$ lives in the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$: it is what the market charges, risk premium included, and the physical default rate is lower (Law 4). And it is an average; the event itself is all or nothing. The full credit triangle is §6.8; the survive-or-jump mixture behind it is §5.1.

Kaz's numbers were the obvious ones *(illustrative)*: Ford Credit at 95 and 180 bp, GM Financial at 98 and 120 bp, recovery 40 on both. The intensities follow from the box: $0.0180/0.60 = 3.0\%$ a year for Ford Credit and $0.0120/0.60 = 2.0\%$ for GM Financial. If Ford Credit defaults, \$40 million of bonds at 95 fall to 40, a loss of $55/95 = 57.9\%$, or **−\$23.2 million**. If GM Financial defaults, the \$60 million short at 98 gains $58/98 = 59.2\%$, or **+\$35.5 million**. The expected jump P&L under $\mathbb{Q}$ is

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[J] \approx \lambda_S N_S (P_S - R_S) - \lambda_L N_L (P_L - R_L) = 0.02 \times 35.5 - 0.03 \times 23.2 \approx +\$15{,}000 \text{ a year},
$$

where $N$ is face and $P - R$ is the loss per unit of face at default. Almost exactly zero, and not by accident: spread times duration tracks the loss rate, so a pair that is DTS-neutral is, roughly, jump-neutral *in expectation*.

"Everyone stops there," Ines said, "and it's the wrong place to stop." She drew what was really on the board: a tall spike near zero for the years nothing happens, a small spike at −\$23.2 million with a 3% weight, and a smaller one at +\$35.5 million with a 2% weight. The mean is fine. The shape is a coin that lands on its edge most of the time and occasionally takes your arm off. Put it next to what the pod is paid for. A 30 bp convergence thesis on the \$40 million long is worth $4.5 \times 0.0030 \times \$40\text{mm} = \$540{,}000$ a year. One default on the long erases $23.2/0.54 \approx 43$ years of it. On a \$400 million pod, the kind that sends \$20 million RFQs, \$23.2 million is 5.8% of capital in an afternoon, past the first of the two lines the press has reported for one large platform (5% and 7.5%; §8D.5). The expected value is a statement about many pods over many years. The stop is a statement about this one, this year. Figure 8-D.5 draws her board.

![Figure 8-D.5: Ines's board. One year of jump P&L for the DTS-neutral pair, \$40mm of Ford Credit long against \$60mm of GM Financial short, on a log scale: the mean is almost exactly zero, 95% of years show nothing, and the Ford Credit spike alone is larger than the \$20mm that is 5% of a \$400mm pod. Illustrative: intensities of 3.0% and 2.0% from spreads of 180 and 120 bp at a recovery of 40, defaults independent, the chapter's numbers.](assets/figures/ch08d_two_spikes.png)

"And when both go?" Theo asked.

"Then the pair makes the difference, about \$12 million, and you won't enjoy it," Ines said. A default wave that takes both captives would bring everything a pair hates at once: recalls, margin calls, wider fees and every other pod leaving through the same door. The BIS's account of the GM and Ford downgrades of May 2005, with relative-value trades losing on both legs while derivatives "gapped" (BIS Quarterly Review, June 2005), is the record; §23.6 replays it.

Kaz then pushed the formula until it broke. Suppose Ford Credit slides to 60 and 2,000 bp, with its spread duration down to 3. The DTS ratio becomes $3 \times 2{,}000 / (4.5 \times 120) \approx 11.1$: eleven dollars of GM Financial short for every dollar of Ford Credit. Ford Credit's jump to a 40 recovery is now only a third of its price, while every dollar of GM Financial short still carries a jump of 59%. The ruler that matched spread risk so well at 95 has stopped measuring anything; it is sizing a trade on a spread that no longer drives the price.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Ben_Dor p.250 (printed p.220):* "When an issuer is near default, its bonds tend to trade on price rather than spread. To protect against default, any long-short positions should be matched on a market-value basis rather than DTS-hedged."
>
> **Annotation.** The authors learned this from a strategy that was hedged, issuer-matched and still lost 4.73% in one month: their DTS-neutral utility steepener in July 2002, when El Paso, PSEG Energy Holdings and Williams defaulted or gapped. Removing that one month raises the strategy's information ratio from 0.2 to 0.8. For a pod the rule has two halves. Near default, size on market value, because the jump is the risk. And hedge a default only with the same issuer's capital structure, because no other issuer's bonds jump on the same day; that is Owen's pair next door (§4C.3) and Chapter 12-B's recovery-weighted sizing. In CDS form the same logic holds: a bought contract pays one minus the auction price, 60 points at a 40 recovery, and it pays it on the issuer you bought it on.

Mags had come over for the coffee and stayed for the whiteboard. "Back in my day a pair was two bonds and a prayer," she said. She read the board twice, which from Mags is applause. "This is better. It's still two bonds."

Before the desk went home Ines marked her indication two basis points wider than her screen, because a client whose lender is tired will pay to leave. Nobody thought that unkind. It was the second answer to the bold question: the lender charges the pod through the fee, and the dealer charges it through the offer, and both of them can see the weather.

## 8D.5 Sizing to the Stop

On Thursday at 7:40 Marcus walked to Dov's desk instead of emailing, which on the platform is a signal in itself, with two sheets.

The first is the training sheet every new credit PM receives in week one *(illustrative composite)*. A \$200 million pod; capital halved after a 5% drawdown, \$10 million; the pod closed after 7.5%, \$15 million. The shape is the one eFinancialCareers, citing the *Wall Street Journal*, reported for one large platform on 5 December 2025; no platform publishes its rule (Chapter 1-B quotes the report; Chapter 8-B owns the regime). The sheet's single idea is that a stop converts a loss budget into a size before conviction gets a vote. A high-yield bond at 98 with a recovery of 40 loses 58 points on default. The largest long that cannot take the pod through the firing line by itself is $\$15\text{mm}/0.58 = \$25.9$ million. If the platform wants one default to cost no more than 2.5% of capital, \$5 million, the answer is \$8.6 million. A capital-structure pair escapes the rule, which is why the sheet includes one: \$20 million of a first lien at 98 with a recovery of 70 loses \$5.6 million on default, a second lien at 70 with a recovery of 20 gains 50 points per dollar shorted, so \$11.2 million of second-lien short leaves the pair flat on the day of default. The same rule turns up outside the platforms, so it is arithmetic, not culture. How many bonds you can borrow does."

The second sheet was Dov's. His allocation is \$400 million, his first line sits \$24 million below his high-water mark (§1B.2), and on Thursday morning the book is \$11 million below that mark. The pair was opened at \$40 million.

The borrow gives a second number. The pod's own rule caps a borrowed short at 20% of the lender's inventory, which on \$300 million is \$60 million, exactly the current short. "Size to the stop and to the borrow at once," Marcus said, "and the smaller number wins. Today it's the stop."

> **Math, Gently.** *Why a random walk touches a line more often than you expect.* Treat a year of P&L as a random walk, each day adding a small random amount. Picture a walker on a pier with a railing five paces to the left: what matters is not where he ends the evening but whether he touched the railing on the way. For a walk with no drift there is a neat trick, the *reflection principle*: every path that touches the line and comes back has a mirror-image twin that touches it and keeps going, so the chance of touching a line at any time is twice the chance of ending below it. With 6% annual volatility and a line 5% down, ending below it has probability about 20% (5% is 0.83 standard deviations), so touching it at some point is about 40%. A drawdown stop is harsher still, because it is measured from the running peak, and the line moves up with every new high; with no drift the simulated chance of a 5% peak-to-trough drawdown within the year is about 72% (POD §4.3, illustrative). Drift helps, and drift is what the platform pays for: at a Sharpe ratio of 1 and 6% volatility the odds fall to 43%; at 4% volatility, to 12%. Volatility on allocated capital sets the survival rate; Chapter 8-B and §28.6 have the rest.

## 8D.7 The Exit, and the Day the Weather Changed

The recall came at 9:40 on Friday, 9 October, as an email from the prime broker with no adjectives. The lender wanted \$20 million face of the GM Financial notes back; its agent's note said the asset manager behind it had sold its position. The prime broker could find \$5 million elsewhere by the afternoon, at 95 bp. The other \$15 million had to be bought back or replaced by Monday's settlement.

"I said this week," Wen said. "I didn't say Friday."

Before he touched anything, Dov wrote PB-19's exit rules on the wall, because an exit decided during a recall is decided by the recall. The rules are the Ironshield write-up's, and each is a day someone remembers. When the thesis is wrong, the pair is cut at once, because a pair whose reason has gone is two unrelated positions with a hedge ratio between them; Owen's replay of GM in May 2005 (§23.6) is the pod's example of what waiting costs. When the thesis is intact but the P&L is moving against it, the pair is scaled toward the loss budget rather than defended; the 2:40 trim of the Halvorsen short in §1B.2, which in story time is four days away, is that rule done well. And when the budget is breached and the PM likes the trade more, somebody other than the PM decides, which is where Kestrel had gone. Gordon Pike's committee had met at 8:00 that morning, given PB-01 until the end of the month at its current size, and signed the ledger line itself.

PB-19 fitted none of them. The thesis was intact, the P&L was flat, and what had failed was the instrument. So Dov replaced the leg rather than the trade. He asked three dealers for five-year GM protection, about \$12 million each; one was Ines's desk, unsurprised to be asked again. The par spread was 110 bp against the standard 100 bp coupon, so the pod paid a small upfront, about $(110 - 100)\text{ bp} \times 4.5 \approx 0.45$ points, on top of the running coupon; a CDS short's cost is the coupon, the upfront and the basis, never the running spread alone. A contract at 110 bp carries less DTS per dollar than a bond at 120, so keeping the ratio's meaning took $\$22.5\text{mm} \times 4.5 \times 180 / (4.5 \times 110) \approx \$36.8$ million of protection. Then the pod bought back and returned every borrowed GM Financial note.

For forty minutes, between the first CDS fill and the last bond bought back, the pair was short GM twice: legging risk, the cost of two legs executed as two orders. §18.6.3 shows a day it cost a pod far more; here it cost a few thousand dollars.

What the switch bought: long Ford Credit earns 180 bp on \$22.5 million, about \$405,000 a year; the protection costs 110 bp on \$36.8 million, about the same; the fee is gone. Against a thesis now worth $4.5 \times 0.0030 \times \$22.5\text{mm} \approx \$304{,}000$ a year, the pair is thinly positive again. What it cost: a basis the pod now owns. The pair is now long Ford Credit's bonds against a GM contract whose spread can drift from GM Financial's bond spread for reasons unrelated to either captive (§24.3). Wen's ledger line changed from a lender and a fee to a contract and a reference-entity note, and the leak column gained an entry.

At 3:00 that afternoon Halvorsen held its lender call. That story belongs to Chapter 1, and its Tuesday to §1B.2.

> **Trend Watch.** *The short leg, 2022–2026.* In 2022 Man Group wrote that prime brokers' lending desks "now provide daily feeds with firm borrow availability and fees across the entire investable cash bond universe," something "largely unheard of even a few years ago when all cash bond security lending was essentially manual and ad hoc" (Man_2022 p.11). The market grew into the feeds: EquiLend reported corporate-bond lending revenue of \$463 million for 2025, up 8%, with balances up 22% and fees down 12% (Mondo Visione, January 2026), more shorting at lower average prices, which says nothing about the names everyone wants. Transparency did not keep pace: after the Fifth Circuit remanded Rule 10c-1a in August 2025, the SEC's order of 3 December 2025 pushed loan reporting to FINRA back to 28 September 2028, with public dissemination later still, and how much corporate-bond lending the rule will capture remains unclear (Proskauer, December 2025). The platforms kept hiring: on 2 June 2025 Hedgeweek reported Millennium's hires of a CDS and CMBS index trader, a corporate-credit partner from another fund and a rival's former US head of credit; on 20 August 2026 it reported that Balyasny had hired a BlackRock investment-grade co-head to run a team that will "trade both long and short positions in US high-grade corporate bonds and pursue relative-value opportunities." More pods running more pairs means more borrowed shorts in the same names, with no public tape of who holds them; the fee is the only crowding gauge a pod can see, which is why Wen keeps the weather and Chapter 22 treats the borrow rules as prices.

## 8D.8 When to Apply It, and When It Breaks

At 4:30 the screen produced a new candidate and Wen killed it in four minutes: long a sponsor-backed software company's first-lien loan, short its notes, and the notes had no lender on her sheet and no CDS. "No short leg," she said, and wrote it on the refusals list at the foot of the wall, which Dov privately thinks is the ledger's most valuable part.

The refusals list records the shapes in which a pair is the wrong tool, each with the chapter that taught it. A private loan has no short leg at all, and the public proxy shorts something else (Chapter 17-B). An exchange that pays consenting holders a premium is a categorical event, and the short leg of any pair on that issuer gaps *up* (Chapter 14). The legal layer can invert the legs after the trade is on: an uptier turns the long first lien into the junior claim (Chapter 13), and an AT1 can go to zero while the senior survives (§23.8). Sometimes the hedge is the trade, as in 2022, when a credit book's "negative duration" hedge was its biggest position (§8.7, Case A). And sometimes a book of twelve pairs is one factor in costume, which only the residual-correlation test catches (§8.8). PB-12, Hovnanian, sits at the top of the list because it is the purest case: once a CDS payoff can be negotiated by the people who hold it, there is no short leg, only a counterparty (§24.8.2).

The published record deserves the same honesty, and Wen keeps it taped beside the refusals. The best-documented credit relative-value strategy is capital-structure arbitrage, short rich CDS against stock. Over 2001–2004 a rule-based version earned a Sharpe ratio near 1.2 on 48 monthly returns, needed between \$45 and \$90 of capital per \$100 of notional to run at 10% volatility, and converged on "only a small fraction of the individual trades" (Duarte, Longstaff and Yu 2007). Out of sample, over 2005–2009, the classical rule had negative Sharpe ratios before and during the crisis (Avino and Lazar 2012). There is no peer-reviewed pairs test on cash corporate bonds at all. The pod's trade rests on mechanism and practice, not on a published backtest, and the pod says so.

## 8D.9 The Pair Book Engine

### Specification for AI Implementation

*The moment it was needed.* At 9:40 on Friday Dov needed one number the pod had computed in pieces all week: the size of PB-19 after the ruler (Wednesday), the stop and the borrow (Thursday) had each had their say, recomputed for a short leg that was about to change from a bond to a contract. It took twenty minutes and a spreadsheet. `pair_book.py` is that twenty minutes as one function call per question.

*What it computes, and why it is built this way.* Two `Leg` records and one `ShortCost` record (lender or none, fee, inventory, share limit, expected recall, financing, half-spreads, holding period, and for a CDS the coupon and upfront). `ratio` returns dollars of short per dollar of long under each ruler, importing `dts` and `dts_pair_hedge` from `ido_engine` (§8.9) so the DTS arithmetic has one copy in the book. `scenarios` is §8D.3's four-state table. `jump_table` gives each leg's loss at default, the expected jump under $\mathbb{Q}$ and the worst case as a share of capital, the quantities `risk_model.py`'s jump overlay uses (Chapter 8-B). `max_long` sizes the long to the stop and to the borrow and says which binds. `carry_friction` and `capture` give the lines of §8D.6, and `ledger_row` returns the row as a dictionary for the wall.

*Inputs and outputs.* In: \$mm market values, prices per 100, decimal spreads and fees a year, years, recoveries as fractions of par. Out: ratios, \$mm P&L by state, a jump dictionary, a sizing dictionary with a `binds` field, and a ledger row.

*The edge cases that bite, each a day this chapter showed.* A leg below 70 trades on price, so the ruler switches to market value and logs it (Ford Credit at 60, §8D.4). A short with no lender returns `declined`, never a size (Wen's refusals, §8D.8). A CDS short on a reference entity outside the liquid set is flagged *basis unquoted*. A trailing beta within 0.1 of zero is shrunk to the DTS ratio and logged rather than divided by (§8D.3). Transaction costs charge both legs, in and out, not one half-spread once; a CDS short's cost is its coupon plus its amortised upfront, not the running spread alone. ### Exercises

**1. Three rulers on someone else's pair.** *[ruler]* You are long \$40mm of issuer A (380 bp, spread duration 4.2) and hedge with issuer B (520 bp, 4.6); a trailing beta of A on B is 0.60. Size the short under DV01, DTS and beta, and say which ruler leaves the pair long the market when both spreads widen 10% in relative terms. *Check:* \$36.5mm, \$26.7mm, \$21.9mm. *Solution.* $40 \times 4.2/4.6$; $40 \times (4.2 \times 380)/(4.6 \times 520)$; $40 \times 4.2 \times 0.60/4.6$. On a 10% widening the long loses \$0.64mm and the shorts make \$0.87mm, \$0.64mm and \$0.52mm: DV01 is net *short* the market, DTS flat, beta net long. The long is the tighter name, so the order flips relative to PB-19.

**2. The two spikes.** *[jump]* Long \$10mm market value of L at 95 (180 bp), short \$15mm of S at 98 (120 bp), recovery 40 on both. Compute the loss if L defaults, the gain if S defaults, and the expected jump P&L under $\mathbb{Q}$. *Check:* −\$5.79mm, +\$8.88mm, about +\$3,900 a year. *Solution.* $10 \times 55/95$; $15 \times 58/98$; $0.02 \times 8.88 - 0.03 \times 5.79$. One default on L is 43 years of a 30 bp thesis (\$135,000 a year).

**3. The training sheet.** *[stop]* On \$200mm of capital with a 7.5% line, how large can a single high-yield long at 98 with recovery 40 be before one default breaches the line alone? What if one default may cost only 2.5% of capital? *Check:* \$25.9mm; \$8.6mm. *Solution.* $15/0.58$ and $5/0.58$, in face. The capital-structure pair on the same sheet needs \$11.2mm of second lien at 70 (recovery 20) against \$20mm of first lien at 98 (recovery 70): $20 \times 0.28 = 5.6 = N \times 0.50$.

**4. Cash or CDS.** *[menu]* A bond's Z-spread is 120 bp, its CDS trades at 110 bp and the borrow fee is 35 bp. What does it cost a year to be short \$15mm each way, and what does the difference buy? *Check:* \$232,500 against \$165,000. *Solution.* $0.0155 \times 15$ and $0.0110 \times 15$; the \$67,500 saving is paid for with an ISDA, margin, an auction, a reference-entity check and a basis. At a 100 bp cash rate the 35 bp fee is a 65 bp rebate.

**5. The capture ratio.** *[explain]* A pair's month: market line −\$12,960, rates residue −\$1,500, idio +\$40,500, borrow fee −\$4,375, financing −\$3,333, transaction cost −\$5,000. Group the lines and compute the capture ratio. *Check:* 0.33. *Solution.* Leak $= -12{,}960 - 1{,}500 = -\$14{,}460$; friction $= -\$12{,}708$; net $= \$13{,}332$; $\kappa = 13{,}332/40{,}500 = 0.33$. Chapter 8-C works the same month line by line.

**6. Break the beta ruler (build).** *[replication]* Simulate 60 issuers in five industries for 160 weeks with relative spread changes $x_{M,t} + x_{I,t} + u_{i,t}$, form all same-industry pairs with random directions, and hedge each with PV01, DTS and a 24-week beta. Show that the beta-hedged pairs have the lowest average absolute market correlation and the highest systematic share of variance, then add $(1+\hat\beta)/2$. *Solution sketch.* Use `ratio` and `pnl` week by week. When a short leg's trailing beta nears zero the beta ruler takes large market positions at random times and in random directions, so the correlation washes out while the variance stays (Ben_Dor p.233); the adjusted beta moves the denominator away from zero.
