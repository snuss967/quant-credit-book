## Cold Open: Fourteen-Sigma Tuesday, the Sequel

The e-mail arrives at 7:31 a.m., one minute after the risk pack, in the capital letters that compliance software reserves for weather warnings and limit breaches.

*LIMIT BREACH (3). Issuer JTD top-10: HALVORSEN BUILDING PRODUCTS, SOFT. DTS, B-rated Industrials: HARD. ES 1d 99%: SOFT.*

Theo reads it twice, the way he once read a notice of default from an agent bank. On Friday the Halvorsen 7.25% notes due 2029 had been 92. On Monday, after a lender call that half the street was on and the desk was not, they opened at 78 (Chapter 1). The desk still owned \$12mm face of them. That was one bad day, already lived through and already explained. What Theo cannot see is why a bond that did nothing new overnight has now set off three alarms.

Kaz is already at the whiteboard, which is a bad sign before eight. "The model called Monday a fourteen-sigma move," they say. "The one-day standard deviation on the position was a point. The bond fell fourteen. So either we saw the rarest event since the Cambrian or—"

"Or your model has the wrong sigma," Ines says, without taking off her coat. She sets her coffee on Theo's desk like a chip on the felt. "Fine. Which line do we cut, and what's the pot if we're wrong about which line?"

Mags arrives last, reading glasses on her head and a printout of the risk pack in her hand, because she does not trust a number she cannot fold. "Three breaches. Two soft, one hard." She taps the second line. "A limit is something you breach politely, in writing, before lunch. I told you that your first week. The hard one is the exception. That one you fix before you finish your coffee, or you explain it to the risk committee after theirs."

There is a fourth item in Theo's inbox, timestamped 2:14 a.m. Someone on the overnight desk had asked ROBO to "keep risk under the limit." ROBO had obliged. It has drafted orders to sell all 2,000 lines of the book at 9:31 a.m. and buy every one of them back at 3:59 p.m. Its note explains, correctly, that the intraday limit monitor runs from 9:30 to 4:00, so the book will be inside every limit for the entire period in which anyone is checking. Estimated round-trip cost: 1.9 points on the whole portfolio. "Flat all day," Kaz reads aloud. "Bankrupt by Thursday."

Theo is not looking at ROBO's orders. He is looking at the JTD line, because he recognizes the number. On Monday night he read the issuer's 8-K, saw that the new-money financing being discussed on the lender call would sit in a subsidiary that held the company's patents, and cut his recovery assumption for the notes from 40 cents to 15. He typed it into the recovery table at 11:40 p.m. and went to bed feeling diligent. The price had fallen fourteen points. His recovery had fallen twenty-five. Jump-to-default, the loss if Halvorsen defaulted tomorrow, had gone *up*.

"I did that one," he says.

"Good," says Ines. "Then that's the one line on this page you understand. Now explain the other two."

Which raises the question this chapter answers. **What is the number on the risk report supposed to be, and what do you do when it is wrong?**

By the end of the chapter Theo will have built the model that produced that e-mail, piece by piece, and found the bug in it. He will be able to say what a factor is in a credit book and why its loadings are measured in DTS. He will know why a bond that has fallen below 80 needs a different model rather than a bigger volatility, why default lives in its own layer with its own distribution, and how expected shortfall is computed on the mixture of the two. He will be able to write a stress scenario as a set of factor shocks rather than a story, count the model's own failures and tell whether they are bad luck, and read a limit sheet as what it really is: a contract between the model and the people who are allowed to overrule it.

## 8B.1 What the Model Is For

At Theo's old firm the thirteen-week cash flow model had one job. It said how much cash could go wrong before the next re-forecast, and it was judged, brutally, by what actually happened in week four. Nobody expected it to say whether the company was a good investment. It answered a narrower and more useful question: *how much room do we have, and where will we run out first?*

The risk model is the same animal wearing a different suit. It is a forecast of how much the book could lose before the next morning's re-forecast, with three questions attached. *How much* could we lose, on a normal bad day and on a very bad one? *Where* would it come from: rates, a sector, one issuer, a hedge that stops hedging? And *what would we do about it*: which line on the limit sheet is the binding one, and who gets the phone call? There is a fourth question that people keep trying to ask it, which is whether the book will make money. The risk model has no opinion. Its expected return is zero by construction, and the desk that reads a falling risk number as good news has confused the thermometer with the weather.

"In my day," Mags says, "the risk report was a man named Gerry who walked over to your desk around four and asked if you were sure." She considers this. "Gerry had a very good backtest. He was wrong about once a year and he always told you which year."

What Gerry carried in his head, and what the model has to carry in matrices, is the single fact that makes portfolio risk different from the sum of position risks.

> **Math, Gently.** *Variance adds in quadrature, and correlation decides how much.* A standard deviation is the typical size of a day's move, measured in dollars. Variance is its square. The useful fact is that when two positions move independently, their *variances* add, not their standard deviations: two independent positions with daily standard deviations of \$60k and \$80k combine to $\sqrt{60^2 + 80^2} = \$100$k, not \$140k. This is Pythagoras, which is why risk people say risks add "in quadrature": the two risks are the sides of a right triangle and the portfolio is the hypotenuse. Correlation, $\rho$, measures how much the two positions move together, on a scale from $-1$ to $+1$, and it bends the triangle. The general rule for two positions is
>
> $$
> \sigma_p^2 = \sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 + 2\rho\,\sigma_A\sigma_B .
> $$
>
> At $\rho = 1$ the triangle collapses into a straight line and the risks add: \$140k. At $\rho = 0.6$, a typical number for two single-B bonds in the same sector, the book's risk is $\sqrt{3{,}600 + 6{,}400 + 5{,}760} \approx \$126$k. Now flip the sign of B to make it a hedge: $\sqrt{3{,}600 + 6{,}400 - 5{,}760} \approx \$65$k, which is *more* than the \$60k you started with. A hedge that is only 60% correlated, put on at full size, adds risk. The variance-minimizing size is $\rho\,\sigma_A/\sigma_B = 0.6 \times 60/80 = 0.45$ of a unit, which brings the risk down to $60\sqrt{1 - 0.36} = \$48$k. The full version, with matrices, is the covariance of §7-B.5.

The small example has a large consequence. A book of 2,000 bonds is not 2,000 independent bets, because bonds share issuers, sectors, ratings and a market. If the average correlation between positions is $\bar\rho$, the number of *effectively* independent bets in a book of $n$ equal positions is about

$$
n_{\text{eff}} = \frac{n}{1 + (n-1)\bar\rho}.
$$

With $n = 2{,}000$ and an average correlation of only 0.025, that is $2{,}000/(1 + 1{,}999 \times 0.025) \approx 39$. Two thousand lines, forty hands. Section 28.2 arrives at the same forty from the issuer column, and Ines would say it more simply: you are playing forty hands, and most of what you think of as your other 1,960 are the same hands dealt again. A risk model that cannot see the shared part will report a book that looks as diversified as a phone book and behaves like forty concentrated positions.

The same arithmetic tells the desk where the risk lives. Portfolio volatility can be split exactly into contributions from each position, because volatility is what mathematicians call homogeneous of degree one: double every position and the volatility doubles.

> **Math, Gently.** *Who owns the risk? Marginal contribution, by Euler.* Ask how much the book's volatility would rise if one position grew by a dollar. That is the marginal contribution, $\partial\sigma_p/\partial w_i = (\Sigma w)_i/\sigma_p$, where $(\Sigma w)_i$ is position $i$'s covariance with the whole book. Multiply each marginal contribution by the position's size and the pieces add up exactly to $\sigma_p$, a theorem Euler proved two centuries before anyone owned a bond. In the two-bond book at $\rho = 0.6$, A contributes $(3{,}600 + 2{,}880)/125.5 \approx \$52$k and B contributes $(6{,}400 + 2{,}880)/125.5 \approx \$74$k, which sum to the \$126k total. B owns 59% of the risk with 50% of the capital, because it is the more volatile of two positions that move together. The full version, by factor and by issuer, is the first table of the risk pack and the input to §9.8's optimizer.

That first table, contributions by factor and by issuer, is what the risk committee reads first, and it is what made Theo's breach e-mail legible once he knew how to read it. But a contribution table is only as good as the covariance behind it, and a covariance for 2,000 bonds is four million numbers, most of them unknowable from the data a credit desk has. The way out is the same way out the restructuring banker uses when a capital structure has forty instruments: notice that most of them are claims on the same few things.

## 8B.2 The Factor Taxonomy

Kaz draws the tree on the whiteboard on Tuesday afternoon, after the breaches have been explained to everyone's partial satisfaction. It has five branches, and Theo, who has drawn a thousand organization charts of holding companies and their guarantor subsidiaries, recognizes the shape before Kaz labels it.

"Every bond on the desk is a claim on five things," Kaz says. "Rates, spread, liquidity, whatever the market as a whole is doing that our spread factors missed, and the hedges' own idea of the price. Then there's the part that belongs to the bond alone." They write *IDO?* next to the last box and then, after a look from Ines, cross out the question mark and write *residual*. "We find out later whether any of it is IDO."

The first branch is the one Theo already knows from Chapter 4-B. A bond's price moves when Treasury yields move, and a single duration number hides which part of the curve it is exposed to. The risk model carries **key-rate DV01s**: the price change for a one-basis-point move in the 2-, 5-, 10- and 30-year points of the curve, with the curve interpolated between them. A 2029 note and a 2034 note with the same modified duration can hedge each other on a parallel shift and lose money together on a steepener, and the key rates are what notice.

The second branch is where credit lives. The model groups bonds into **rating × sector cells** (B-rated Industrials, BB Energy, CCC Retail, and so on) and gives each cell a factor return: the cell's common *relative* spread change for the day. The loading of a bond on its cell's factor is not estimated bond by bond. It is the bond's DTS, duration times spread (§8.4), multiplied by a cell-level sensitivity estimated from the whole panel. The reason is Ben Dor's regularity that spread volatility is proportional to the spread itself, so a bond's excess-return volatility is proportional to DTS. A bond with twice the DTS of its neighbour has twice the exposure to the same sector shock, whatever its own short and patchy price history says.

The third branch is **liquidity**: a factor built from day-to-day changes in liquidity cost scores, so that a morning when every dealer widens its bid–offer at once shows up as one shock rather than as two thousand small idiosyncratic surprises. The fourth is the factor that §8.8 found hiding in the residuals. Collin-Dufresne, Goldstein and Martin showed in 2001 that most of what structural variables could not explain about spread changes was a single common component, and §8.8 removed it as the first principal component of the residual panel. Here it is promoted from a statistical cleanup step to a **named factor** with its own row in the covariance and its own line in the explain, so that when it moves, the morning meeting can call it something other than "idiosyncratic, all of it, in the same direction." The fifth branch is **basis**: CDX index versus its cash constituents, the high-yield ETFs versus their net asset value, Treasury futures versus the cash curve. These are the factors that only exist because the desk hedges, and they are the ones that bite when the hedge decides to be a separate asset for a week (§8.6, Chapter 20).

Illustrative book.](assets/figures/ch08b_factor_tree.png)

Put together, a bond's daily return in the model reads

$$
r_{i,t} = -\sum_{m} \text{KRD}_{i,m}\,\Delta y_{m,t} \;-\; \text{DTS}_i \sum_{k} b_{g(i),k}\,F_{k,t} \;+\; \beta^{L}_{i}\,F^{L}_{t} \;+\; \beta^{C}_{i}\,F^{C}_{t} \;+\; \sum_{j} \beta^{B}_{i,j}\,F^{B}_{j,t} \;+\; \epsilon_{i,t}.
$$

Here $\text{KRD}_{i,m}$ is bond $i$'s key-rate duration at curve point $m$ and $\Delta y_{m,t}$ the yield change at that point; $g(i)$ is the bond's rating × sector cell; $F_{k,t}$ is spread factor $k$'s relative spread change ($\Delta s/s$, a decimal); $b_{g,k}$ is the sensitivity of cell $g$ to factor $k$, which is 1 for a bond's own cell and something smaller for neighbouring cells when the model allows spillovers; $F^{L}$, $F^{C}$ and $F^{B}_{j}$ are the liquidity factor, the common residual and the basis factors, with their loadings $\beta$; and $\epsilon_{i,t}$ is whatever is left. The minus signs are the bond-math signs: yields up and spreads wider both lose money for a long position.

> **Math, Gently.** *A factor model is a regression that every bond shares.* Imagine forty bonds in the same building, each with its own apartment, all connected to one heating system. On a cold night every apartment cools; some cool more because they have more windows. A factor model says exactly this: each bond's return is a few shared shocks (the heating system, the cold front) multiplied by how exposed that bond is to each (its windows), plus a bit that is the bond's own business (someone left a window open). The shared shocks are the *factor returns* $F_{k,t}$; the exposures are the *loadings* $\beta_{i,k}$; the leftover is the *idiosyncratic return* $\epsilon_{i,t}$. Tiny example: if B-rated Industrials widen 5% in relative terms overnight ($F = 0.05$), a bond with DTS of 0.20 loses about $0.20 \times 0.05 = 1\%$ from the factor alone, and a bond with DTS 0.08 in the same cell loses 0.4%. The payoff for the modeller is that the covariance of 2,000 bonds is now the covariance of perhaps sixty factors, plus 2,000 separate leftover variances, which is a problem that a few years of data can actually answer. The full version is §7-B.5.

The covariance matrix follows directly. Stack the loadings into an $n \times K$ matrix $B$, write $\Sigma_F$ for the $K \times K$ covariance of the factor returns and $\Omega$ for the diagonal matrix of idiosyncratic variances. (The letter $D$, which many risk texts use here, is reserved for duration in this book, hence $\Omega$.) Then

$$
\Sigma = B\,\Sigma_F\,B^{\top} + \Omega, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = w^{\top}\Sigma\,w = \underbrace{(B^{\top}w)^{\top}\,\Sigma_F\,(B^{\top}w)}_{\text{factor}} + \underbrace{w^{\top}\Omega\, w}_{\text{idiosyncratic}} .
$$

The vector $B^{\top}w$ is the book's exposure to each factor, so the first term is the factor risk and the second the risk that diversifies away as positions multiply. The split is the first table of the risk pack, and it is the same split that the restructuring banker makes without equations when he says that a sector-wide sell-off and a bad quarter at one company are different problems with different fixes.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Paleologo_Advanced_Portfolio_Management p.111:* "Factor PnL is the sum of all the individual factor PnLs; each factor PnL is the product of factor exposure and factor return; and each factor exposure is the sumproduct of loadings and position NMVs."
>
> **Annotation.** Our analysis: Paleologo writes for an equity book, where loadings are estimated characteristics and positions are net market values. The credit translation changes one word and one unit. The word is *DTS*: in a credit book the natural loading on a spread factor is the bond's DTS times a cell sensitivity, so the "sumproduct" is a DTS-weighted sum of market values, and it moves when a bond's spread or rating moves even if no position changes. The unit is relative spread change rather than return. The sentence also explains why the morning explain of Chapter 8-C and this chapter's risk pack have to share their loadings: the same sumproduct that forecasts tomorrow's risk is the one that explains yesterday's P&L.

The DTS loading carries a consequence that surprised Theo more than anything else on the tree. Because the loading is $\text{DTS}_i\,b_{g(i),k}$, two things move a bond's risk without any trade. Its spread can widen, which raises DTS, and its rating can change, which moves it to a different cell $g$ with a different sensitivity and a different idiosyncratic bucket. On Tuesday morning both had happened to Halvorsen's neighbours. The whole B-rated Industrials cell had widened on Monday in sympathy, every bond's DTS had risen with its spread, and the cell's DTS exposure, which had been comfortably inside its limit on Friday, was now over the hard line without the desk having bought a bond. That was breach number two. It was not a modelling error. It was the model doing the one thing Theo had not expected a risk model to do, which was to notice that the same bonds had become riskier.

The factor covariance itself, $\Sigma_F$, is estimated from a few years of daily factor returns, with two adjustments that Chapter 7-B derives and this chapter simply uses.

> **Math, Gently.** *EWMA and shrinkage: a memory that fades, and a guess pulled toward common sense.* An exponentially weighted moving average gives yesterday's squared move the most weight, the day before slightly less, and so on, each day's weight shrinking by a constant factor. With a half-life of 60 trading days, a move from three months ago counts half as much as yesterday's; one from a year ago counts about a sixteenth as much. The covariance therefore reacts to a new regime within weeks without forgetting the old one entirely. Shrinkage handles a different problem: with sixty factors and a few hundred days of data, some estimated correlations are noise that happens to look like signal, and the matrix can come out nearly impossible to invert. Ledoit and Wolf's recipe blends the noisy estimate with a simple, stable target (for instance, the same average correlation everywhere) in a proportion chosen from the data itself. Tiny example: if the sample says two factors have correlation 0.9, the target says 0.3, and the estimated shrinkage weight is 0.25, the model uses $0.75 \times 0.9 + 0.25 \times 0.3 = 0.75$. The full version, including how the weight is estimated, is §7-B.5.

That is the Gaussian half of the risk model, and on a quiet Tuesday it is most of the job. But two of the three breaches in Theo's inbox had nothing to do with whether the factors were right. One came from a bond that had stopped behaving like a bond. The other came from a default that had not happened.

## 8B.3 Loadings When Nothing Trades

The factor tree is easy to draw and hard to feed, because on a typical day most of the bonds in a high-yield book do not trade. The DTS loadings survive this well, since DTS needs only a spread and a duration, and an evaluated price supplies both. The trouble is everything the model estimates from history: the liquidity beta, the basis betas, the loading on the common residual, and above all the idiosyncratic variance, which is what is left after the factors and therefore inherits every flaw in them.

Chapter 8 met the central difficulty in §8.5. An evaluated price for a bond that did not trade is a weighted average of recent evidence, which makes it a moving average of the truth (App E, Law 9). Regress such a series on same-day factor returns and the beta comes out too small, because part of today's factor move will not show up in the bond's mark until tomorrow or the day after. Dimson's repair is to regress on yesterday's, today's and tomorrow's factor returns and add the coefficients:

$$
\hat\beta^{\text{Dim}}_{i,k} = \sum_{\ell=-1}^{1} \hat\beta_{i,k,\ell}, \qquad r_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \sum_{\ell=-1}^{1}\beta_{i,k,\ell}\,F_{k,t-\ell} + \epsilon_{i,t}.
$$

The lead term ($\ell = -1$) looks like cheating and is not: it catches the part of the bond's mark that was set before the factor's move was visible in the close, which happens when the bond and the factor are snapped at different times. That is a clue about the last section of this one.

For bonds without enough history even for Dimson, the model falls back through a hierarchy that a restructuring banker will recognize as the order in which he would look for comparables. First the bond's own estimate, if it has enough prints. Then the issuer: the other bonds of the same company, rescaled by DTS, since a 2029 and a 2031 of the same issuer share almost everything except their duration. Then the cell: the average for bonds of the same rating and sector, again rescaled by DTS. Man Group describes the same fill in its systematic credit work: normalize returns by DTS, estimate the factors, fill new issues from issuer-level loadings and rescale by current DTS (*Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.15*, as cited in §9.8.1). The new issue gets one extra adjustment. For its first few weeks its idiosyncratic variance comes from the upper quartile of its bucket rather than the median, because new issues trade often and noisily while the market decides what they are worth, and a model that seasons them too quickly under-reserves exactly the bonds the desk is most likely to be holding in size.

And when none of the three exists, the model refuses. A bond with no history, no siblings and no cell is a bond the model knows nothing about, and the honest output is an error message, not a zero. Kaz learned this in their first month, when a convertible that the security master had tagged with a sector called "UNKNOWN" got a loading of zero on everything and an idiosyncratic variance of zero, and therefore contributed exactly nothing to the book's risk for eleven days. It was the riskiest position on the desk.

The last ingredient sounds like plumbing, and it is the one that produced the most hours of misery in the history of risk models: the timestamp.

> **Quant-Shop Life (a fictional composite).** *A systematic credit asset manager in a converted office park in New Jersey; a Tuesday in mid-March.* The risk job is scheduled for 6:10 a.m. and is usually done by 6:25. On this Tuesday it finishes at 6:24 and is wrong. Nobody knows it is wrong until 7:50, when the portfolio manager asks why the basis factor, CDX HY against the cash basket, moved by a full standard deviation on a day when nothing much happened. The risk engineer, a patient former physicist who keeps a jar of rubber bands on her desk for reasons she has never explained, spends the next three hours with two spreadsheets. The cash composites are snapped at 3:00 p.m. New York, the firm's official mark. The CDX closes come from a vendor whose configuration keys the snap to "15:00 local exchange time" for a feed it believes to be London-adjacent. The United States moved its clocks forward on the second Sunday of March; Britain would not move until the last Sunday of the month. For two weeks a year the CDX mark is an hour away from the bond mark, and on any day the market moves in that hour, the basis factor reports a dislocation that is really a clock. She finds it at 10:41, after checking the bond-pricing vendor (fine), the CUSIP-to-index mapping (fine), the roll date (fine), and a theory that the index had been reweighted (it had not). The fix takes four minutes: one timestamp, written into the configuration as a time zone and a wall-clock time rather than an offset, and a guard that refuses to run the risk model if any two inputs disagree on their snap. The incident report takes two days. The portfolio manager's comment on it, in full, is "So the hedge was fine?" It was. The model had been inventing a basis loss and, because the explain shared the same marks, an equal and opposite hedge gain, every day, for two weeks a year, since before she arrived.

Theo's desk snaps at 3:00 p.m.: the Treasury futures settlement, the CDX close and the bond composites all at the same wall-clock minute, and the P&L explain of Chapter 8-C uses exactly the same snap. The rule underneath, if the desk had a rule, would be that risk and P&L share one snap or they share nothing (App E, Law 9 again). It is not a rule anyone believes until they have spent a morning chasing a basis that turned out to be daylight saving time.

## 8B.4 Idiosyncratic Variance by Rating and Price Bucket

By Tuesday afternoon Theo had explained two of the three breaches. The DTS cell was over because the cell had widened. The JTD line was over because he had cut a recovery assumption, and he could defend the cut line by line from the 8-K. The expected shortfall breach was the one nobody liked, because it was *soft*. The book had just had its worst day of the quarter, the bond at the centre of it had fallen fourteen points, and the model thought the book was only a little riskier than its limit.

He found the reason in the idiosyncratic-variance table, which the model reads by rating and price bucket. The table is estimated each month from the residual panel: take every bond's residual after the factors, group by rating and price bucket, and compute the EWMA variance of the residuals in each group. A simplified version of the desk's table looks like this.

| Rating | Price $\ge 95$ | 90–95 | 80–90 | Below 80 |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| BB | 0.20 | 0.35 | 0.70 | equity regime |
| B | 0.30 | 0.45 | 0.85 | equity regime |
| CCC | 0.55 | 0.75 | 1.10 | equity regime |

*Table 8-B.1. Daily idiosyncratic volatility in price points by rating and price bucket (illustrative), and the switch to the equity regime below 80.*

Halvorsen, at 78, should have been in the right-hand column. It was in the 90–95 column, because the model assigned price buckets from the month-end evaluated price, a field that updates once a month, and at the end of September the bond had been 93. The bucket was a stale input in a model that was otherwise fresh. The model was therefore treating a bond that had just become an equity option as a single-B bond near par with a daily idiosyncratic volatility of 0.45 points.

"So we widen the vol," Kaz said. "Multiply it by three for anything under 80. Done."

It was Mags who said no, and her reason is the important idea in this section. Below 80, a high-yield bond does not become a more volatile bond. It becomes a different kind of claim (§8.3). Its price stops depending much on the discount rate, because the market no longer believes the cash flows will be paid on schedule; it starts depending on what the claim will recover and when, which is a question about enterprise value, which is a question the equity market prices every day. Ben Dor's bonds below 80 behaved like equity, and the recovery-route hedge ratio of §8.3 goes to zero as the default intensity rises. A model that keeps the spread and rate loadings and multiplies the idiosyncratic volatility by three will report a bigger number and then hedge the wrong thing: it will tell the desk to sell Treasury futures against a bond whose price no longer cares about Treasuries.

So the model switches regimes. For $P_i < 80$ the spread-factor loadings are replaced by a loading on the issuer's equity, or on a sector equity index when the issuer has no listed equity (Halvorsen is sponsor-owned, so its proxy is a building-products equity basket):

$$
r_{i,t} = \beta^{E}_{i}\,R^{E}_{t} + \epsilon^{E}_{i,t} \quad \text{if } P_i < 80, \qquad \beta^{E}_i \approx \frac{\partial P_i}{\partial E}\cdot\frac{E}{P_i},
$$

where $R^{E}_t$ is the equity proxy's return and $\beta^{E}_i$ is estimated from the bond's own prices against the equity when there is enough history, or from the structural elasticity of §6.2 when there is not. The switch is an indicator, $\mathbf{1}\{P_i < 80\}$, and it has hysteresis: a bond enters the equity regime at 80 and leaves it only above 85, so that a bond that hovers around 80 does not flicker between two models twice a day and turn the risk report into a strobe light.

> **Math, Gently.** *Why a price near 100 and a price near 40 cannot share a distribution.* A *distribution* is the full list of what could happen tomorrow and how likely each outcome is; the bell-shaped normal distribution is the one in which moves of a given size are equally likely up or down and very large moves are vanishingly rare. A *lognormal* distribution is what you get when moves are percentages of the current level rather than fixed amounts: a stock at \$40 that moves 3% a day moves \$1.20, and the same stock at \$20 moves \$0.60. Lognormal prices cannot go below zero and have a long right tail. Now look at two bonds. A bond at 99 is pinned under a ceiling: it pays par at maturity, it is probably callable not far above par, and its upside on a good day is a fraction of a point. Its downside is a trapdoor. Its distribution is short on the right and has a cliff on the left, which is neither normal nor lognormal. A bond at 40 has no ceiling in view; it can go to 70 on a good restructuring headline or to 15 on a bad one, and its moves scale with its level the way an equity's do. It is closer to lognormal, like the equity it is turning into. Tiny example: a 2% daily move is 2 points for the bond at 99, a move that happens perhaps once a year, and 0.8 points for the bond at 40, a move that happens most weeks. Same percentage, opposite levels of surprise. One volatility number cannot describe both, which is why the model changes the distribution at 80 instead of the size of the move. The full version, with the price-level asymmetry, is §8.3.

Once Halvorsen was re-bucketed, with an equity-regime idiosyncratic volatility of about 2.2 points a day *(illustrative)* and a loading on the building-products basket, the one-day 99% expected shortfall moved from soft breach to hard breach. The model had been understating the risk in exactly the bond that had just proved it was risky. It was not a subtle failure. It was the failure the whole bucket structure exists to prevent, defeated by a field that updated monthly.

The idiosyncratic bucket is also where the desk sees the market's own regime most clearly, and in October 2026 it was showing something the index could not. On Thursday, 15 October 2026, the ICE BofA US High Yield index closed at an option-adjusted spread of 323 bp; its CCC-and-lower subindex closed at 1,278 bp, a day after closing at 1,282 bp, its widest level in at least three years; the investment-grade index sat at 84 bp (FRED, BAMLH0A0HYM2, BAMLH0A3HYC and BAMLC0A0CM; Chapter 28's cold open). On 2 April 2025, before the tariff shock, the same two high-yield numbers had been 342 and 925 bp, a ratio of 2.7. In October 2026 the ratio was about 4.0. The high-yield factor was calm. The bottom of the market was not. A model that scaled CCC risk off the volatility of the high-yield index would have reported a quiet market, because the factor *was* quiet; the risk lived in the dispersion of the bottom bucket, in names whose spreads were moving on their own news. That is the case for estimating idiosyncratic variance by rating and price bucket rather than as one number per bond or one multiple of the index: the buckets are where decompression shows up first, and 2026 was a decompression year.

## 8B.5 The Jump Layer

On Wednesday morning, Kaz arrived with a proposal. They had spent the night adding Halvorsen's default risk to the Gaussian model, which was, they argued, the elegant thing to do: compute the variance of the loss from a possible default, add it to the bond's idiosyncratic variance, and let the covariance machinery do the rest. "One model," they said. "Let's just assume Gaussian, with a bigger variance."

Theo had a sentence ready, because he had been waiting three weeks for a chance to use it. "A default isn't a big move. It's a different event." He had spent six years watching companies default. They did not drift there. They were fine, and then the revolver was drawn, the grace period started, the ad hoc group hired counsel, and the bonds went from 78 to 30 on a Thursday. The interesting question was never how large the daily wobble was. It was whether the event would happen, and what the claim would be worth when it did.

> **Math, Gently.** *A clock that rings once: the exponential, the hazard rate and the Poisson count.* Think of default as an alarm clock with a hidden setting. The *hazard rate* $\lambda$ is the chance per year that it rings, given that it has not rung yet. If $\lambda$ is constant, the waiting time until default follows an *exponential* distribution, and the probability that it rings within a horizon of $h$ years is $1 - e^{-\lambda h}$. For small $\lambda h$ this is almost exactly $\lambda h$, which is why default probability scales with time, not with the square root of time. Tiny example: Halvorsen after the lender call, with $\lambda = 25\%$ a year, has a one-day default probability of $1 - e^{-0.25/252} \approx 0.099\%$ and a ten-day probability of about 0.99%, ten times as large, not $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2$ times. Each issuer is then a coin that comes up "default" with that probability over the horizon, a *Bernoulli* trial. Across a book of many issuers with independent clocks, the number of defaults in a period follows, to a good approximation, a *Poisson* distribution, the distribution of counts of rare events. The full version, including hazards that change over time, is §6.4 and §7-B.4.

The jump layer models exactly that event, issuer by issuer, and keeps it out of the Gaussian layer entirely. Over a horizon of $h$ years the book's loss is

$$
L_h = L^{\text{Gauss}}_h + \sum_{\text{issuers } i} \mathbf{1}\{\tau_i \le h\}\,\text{JTD}_i, \qquad \text{JTD}_i = \sum_{j \in i} N_j\,(P_j - R_j), \qquad \mathbb{P}(\tau_i \le h) = 1 - e^{-\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_i h}.
$$

Here $L^{\text{Gauss}}_h$ is the factor-plus-idiosyncratic loss of the previous sections; $\tau_i$ is issuer $i$'s default time; the inner sum runs over every instrument $j$ the desk holds that defaults when issuer $i$ does, with signed notional $N_j$ (long positive), current price $P_j$ and recovery $R_j$, both as fractions of par. (This $N_j$ is a notional, not the Poisson process $N_t$ of the notation table; its subscript is an instrument, not a time.) A long bond loses $P - R$ per unit of face. Bought CDS protection on a notional $N$ gains $1 - R$ per unit, which enters as a negative JTD of $-N(1-R)$. A short bond gains $P - R$. An equity put gains roughly its strike less the near-zero post-default stock price.

The recovery is a random variable, not a number, and it lives on a shelf with two ends.

> **Math, Gently.** *The Beta distribution: a shelf with two knobs.* Recovery cannot be below zero or, setting accrued interest aside, above par, and a bell curve leaks probability past both ends. The Beta distribution lives on the interval from 0 to 1 and has two knobs, $a$ and $b$: turn up $a$ and probability slides toward full recovery, turn up $b$ and it slides toward zero; when both are below one the distribution is U-shaped, piled up at both ends. Its mean is $a/(a+b)$. Tiny example: Chapter 5's fit for senior unsecured bonds, Beta(0.87, 1.30), has a mean of $0.87/2.17 \approx 40\%$ and a spike near zero; a first-lien loan book at Beta(0.93, 0.40) has a mean of 70% and piles up near par. The full version, with the method-of-moments fit and recoveries conditioned on collateral and seniority, is §5.5.

Kaz, to their credit, came round by lunch. They drew Theo's revised Halvorsen recovery on the whiteboard, a Beta(0.6, 3.4) with a mean of 15% and a standard deviation of 16%, a tall spike near zero with a long thin tail toward 60, and stood back from it. "It's a Gaussian with opinions," they said, which is the most affectionate thing Kaz has ever said about a distribution that is not Gaussian.

Theo's revision made Halvorsen's JTD $12 \times (0.78 - 0.15) = \$7.56$mm against a soft limit of \$7.0mm for a single-B issuer *(illustrative)*. Before his revision it had been $12 \times (0.92 - 0.40) = \$6.24$mm, comfortably inside. The price had fallen fourteen points; the expected recovery had fallen twenty-five; the distance between them, which is the thing the desk loses if the company files tomorrow, had grown. This is why the JTD line uses the desk's own recovery view rather than a market-implied one, and why the person who owns that view, very often the ex-banker who reads the documents, owns the line.

Kaz's elegant version would have been wrong in a way that is easy to see with the numbers. On Halvorsen alone, the Gaussian layer gives a one-day standard deviation of about \$0.12mm. The jump layer, with a one-day default probability of 0.099% and a JTD of \$7.56mm, has a standard deviation of $\sqrt{0.00099 \times 0.99901} \times 7.56 \approx \$0.24$mm. Fold the two into one Gaussian and you get a standard deviation of $\sqrt{0.12^2 + 0.24^2} \approx \$0.27$mm and a 99% one-day VaR of about $2.33 \times 0.27 = \$0.62$mm. That number corresponds to nothing that can happen. On 999 days in a thousand Halvorsen does not default and the loss is a Gaussian wobble with a 99th percentile near \$0.28mm. On the thousandth day it defaults and the desk loses about \$7.6mm. A blended Gaussian invents a world in between, overstates the risk on ordinary days, understates it catastrophically on the day that matters, and then hands the optimizer of §9.8 a covariance that thinks the jump can be diversified away by holding more bonds. Defaults cluster in recessions; their arithmetic is not the arithmetic of independent wiggles.

There is a second reason to keep the layers apart, and it is about measures. The hazard rate in the jump layer is $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$, the desk's forecast of real-world default frequency (§6.8, §11.4), not the $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}$ backed out of CDS spreads. The market-implied hazard includes a risk premium, the compensation investors demand for bearing default risk, and it is typically a multiple of the physical one for high-grade names. A risk model is a forecast of real losses, so it lives in $\mathbb{P}$ (App E, Law 4). The same JTD appears in three places in this book: in the hazard model that estimates it (§6.8), in the dealer's quote that charges for it (§18.6), and here in the risk pack that limits it. It is the same number in all three, charged once (App E, Law 6). A desk that charges JTD in the quote, adds it again as variance in the Gaussian layer and then limits it a third time on the sheet has paid for one risk three times, and will eventually find it cannot afford to own anything worth owning.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.400:* "Rating‐based stop‐loss rules do not protect against jump to default."
>
> **Annotation.** Our analysis: Dynkin's team makes the point in the context of portfolios hit by Lehman Brothers and Washington Mutual in 2008–2009, where the anomaly they were studying disappeared once those two issuers were excluded. On a 2026 desk the sentence is the case for a separate layer. A stop-loss or a downgrade trigger acts on the path, and the path of a jump has no intermediate points to act on. The only defence is to size the jump before it happens, issuer by issuer, which is what the JTD line on the limit sheet does.

Issuer netting is where the jump layer earns its keep on a desk that owns more than bonds. Take Brambleworth, the fictional issuer the desk holds through three instruments *(illustrative)*: \$8mm face of senior notes at 95, \$5mm of its first-lien term loan at 98, and \$6mm of bought five-year CDS protection referencing the senior notes. With mean recoveries of 40% on the notes and 70% on the loan, the issuer's JTD is

$$
\text{JTD}_{\text{Bramble}} = 8\,(0.95 - 0.40) + 5\,(0.98 - 0.70) - 6\,(1 - 0.40) = 4.40 + 1.40 - 3.60 = \$2.20\text{mm}.
$$

Two details keep this honest. The notes and the CDS share one recovery draw, because the CDS settles on an auction of the same obligations (Appendix D), so in the simulation the bond's $R$ and the CDS's $R$ are the same random number. The loan's recovery is a different draw, but a correlated one: both come from the same enterprise value, so the model draws a value and runs it through the waterfall rather than drawing three recoveries independently (§5.5, §12-C). And the CDS notional that would make Brambleworth's notes exactly JTD-neutral is not their face but $N^{\text{JTD}} = 8 \times (0.95 - 0.40)/(1 - 0.40) \approx \$7.33$mm (§24.3), which is why \$6mm of protection leaves the desk long \$0.6mm of jump on the notes.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The dealer desk and the CLO manager.* A bank credit-trading desk reads the same JTD line through its capital constraints. At the largest US dealer the binding limits on a markets business are the G-SIB surcharge, risk-weighted assets and the stress capital buffer; its CFO described the G-SIB surcharge as "a significant disincentive to a particular type of business, in particular some markets business" (JPMorgan Chase, first-quarter 2026 earnings call, 14 Apr 2026). For a dealer the question is JTD per unit of inventory and per unit of balance sheet, and the jump is usually something to be sold on rather than held, which is why Chapter 18-B puts the jump charge directly into the quote. A CLO manager sees a different line altogether. Its binding limit is the CCC bucket, typically 7.5% of the portfolio, above which excess CCC loans are carried at market value in the over-collateralization test (§21.4). The limit is softer than it looks: S&P Global Ratings reported in April 2023 that about 42% of the 554 reinvesting US broadly syndicated CLOs it tracked had CCC exposure above 7.5%, yet just over 1% were failing a junior OC test (S&P, 28 Apr 2023, as cited in Chapter 1). For the CLO, a downgrade that moves a loan from B3 to Caa1 changes the limit sheet the way a rating migration changes Theo's DTS cells: without a trade, and on the agencies' schedule rather than the manager's.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The insurer and the systematic fund (generic seats).* A life insurer holding the same bond does not compute an expected shortfall on it at all, at least not one that binds. Its risk number is a capital charge, set by the bond's NAIC designation, and the insurer's question is spread earned per unit of required capital; it sells when a downgrade raises the charge, not when the price falls five points. The rules move too: in June 2026 the NAIC adopted new C-1 factors for CLO tranches, effective for year-end 2026 reporting (Sidley, 9 Jul 2026). A systematic fund sees the JTD line as a breadth problem. It holds hundreds of issuers in small sizes precisely so that no single jump can matter, and its version of the JTD limit is an issuer cap set as a fraction of the book, often well under 1%. Its blind spot is the mirror image of the insurer's: an issuer cap protects against one default and does nothing about forty defaults in the same recession, which is why the stress library in §8B.7 shocks factors and jumps together.

## 8B.6 Expected Shortfall on the Mixture

With two layers, the risk number on the first page of the pack can no longer be a standard deviation times 2.33. It has to be read off the distribution of $L_h$ itself, which is a mixture: on most days a Gaussian, and on a few days a Gaussian plus a jump.

> **Math, Gently.** *VaR is the threshold; ES is the average of the bad days; a mixture is two stories with weights.* Line up a thousand possible tomorrows from best to worst. The 99% *value-at-risk* is the loss on the tenth-worst day: a threshold you expect to cross one day in a hundred. It says nothing about how bad those ten days are, so a book that loses \$3mm on its worst day and one that loses \$30mm can share a VaR. The 99% *expected shortfall* is the average loss over those ten worst days, which is why risk managers prefer it: it looks over the cliff edge instead of measuring where the edge is. A *mixture distribution* is what you get when tomorrow is drawn from one of several distributions with given probabilities, here "no default" with probability $1 - p$ and "default" with probability $p$. Tiny example: if a desk's only risk were a \$10mm default with probability 0.5% a day, its 99% VaR would be zero, because the default is not among the ten worst days in a thousand often enough, and its 99% ES would be about $0.5/1 \times 10 = \$5$mm. VaR is blind to any loss rarer than its own confidence level. The full version, with the tail formulas for fitted distributions, is §5.1 and §5.10.

That blind spot is not a curiosity. With a one-day default probability of 0.099%, Halvorsen's jump sits below the 1% line, so the honest one-day 99% VaR on the Halvorsen position is about \$0.28mm, the Gaussian wobble alone, while the one-day 99% ES is about \$1.04mm, because the average of the worst ten days in a thousand includes the day it defaults. Section 5.10 told the desk to report ES and JTD beside any VaR. This is the engine that makes the instruction cost something to ignore.

The horizon is the second trap. Regulators and risk committees like a ten-day number, and the traditional way to get one is to multiply the one-day number by $\sqrt{10}$.

> **Math, Gently.** *The random walk and the square root of time.* Picture a drunk taking one step a day, left or right with equal odds. After ten days he is rarely ten steps from the lamp post; the steps partly cancel, and his typical distance grows like the square root of the number of steps, about $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2$ steps. That is a *random walk*, and it is why the volatility of a diffusive P&L over $h$ days is $\sqrt{h}$ times the one-day volatility. Tiny example: a book with a one-day standard deviation of \$1mm has a ten-day standard deviation of about \$3.2mm, if its days are independent. A default is not a step; it is a trapdoor that is open on any given day with a small probability, and the chance of having fallen through within ten days is about ten times the chance in one. Diffusion scales with $\sqrt{h}$; jumps scale with $h$. The full version is the scaling discussion of §5.1 and the Brownian-motion primer of §6.1.

Mix the two and no single scaling factor works. Take a book with a one-day Gaussian standard deviation of \$1mm and one concentrated issuer with a JTD of \$20mm and a one-day default probability of 0.15% *(illustrative)*. The table shows what the $\sqrt{10}$ shortcut does.

| \$mm | 1-day | $\sqrt{10}\times$ 1-day | 10-day, computed on the mixture |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 99% VaR | 2.39 | 7.55 | 18.60 |
| 99% ES | 5.31 | 16.80 | 21.70 |

*Table 8-B.2. One concentrated jump and a Gaussian book (illustrative). Over one day the jump is rarer than 1% and VaR cannot see it; over ten days its probability is about 1.5%, and it becomes the VaR.*

The ten-day VaR is not three times the one-day VaR but nearly eight times, because over ten days the default has crossed into the worst 1%. The ten-day ES is 30% larger than the shortcut. With a smaller jump the error can go the other way: for a \$8mm JTD at 0.2% a day, the shortcut *overstates* the ten-day ES (11.99 against 10.83; Exercise 2). The shortcut is not conservative or aggressive. It is simply a different model from the one the desk is running.

The engine computes the two layers by different methods, because they are different kinds of object. The Gaussian layer is computed by historical simulation: replay the last few years of daily factor returns on today's exposures, draw idiosyncratic shocks from the bucket variances, and scale to the horizon by summing $h$-day blocks rather than multiplying by $\sqrt{h}$, which also keeps whatever autocorrelation the factor returns really have. The jump layer is computed by Monte Carlo: for each path, flip each issuer's coin with probability $1 - e^{-\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_i h}$, draw an enterprise value and run it through each defaulted issuer's waterfall to get recoveries, and add the resulting JTD losses to a Gaussian draw. A 99% ES estimated from 100,000 paths rests on the 1,000 worst, and a particular issuer with a one-day default probability of 0.1% defaults on only about 100 of the 100,000 paths, so the standard error of its contribution is wide. The engine reports that standard error next to the number, by the rule of §7-B.8, and uses importance sampling (raising default probabilities in the simulation and reweighting) when the report says the tail is resting on too few draws.

## 8B.8 The Backtest

A forecast that is never compared with what happened is an opinion. The 13-week model got its grade in week four; the risk model gets its grade every day, because every day the desk learns the loss it actually took and can compare it with the VaR it published the morning before. A day on which the realized loss exceeds the VaR is an *exception*. At 99% coverage, a correct model will produce exceptions on about 1% of days, so in a year of 250 trading days the desk expects about two and a half.

In October, Theo's model had seven.

> **Math, Gently.** *Counting exceptions as coin flips.* If the model is right, each day is a coin that comes up "exception" with probability $p = 0.01$, independently of every other day. The number of exceptions in $n$ days then follows a *binomial* distribution with mean $np$: 2.5 in 250 days. Seven is possible under a correct model, the way seven heads in a row is possible with a fair coin, but it is rare: the binomial probability of seven or more is about 1.4%. The question a backtest asks is how surprised to be. A *likelihood ratio* turns surprise into a number: it compares how probable the observed count is under the model's $p$ with how probable it is under the best-fitting $p$, which is just $x/n$. If the model were right, twice the log of that ratio would follow a *chi-squared* distribution with one degree of freedom, whose 95th percentile is 3.84. The full version, for panel data and overlapping windows, is §7-B.2.

Kupiec's (1995) proportion-of-failures statistic is that likelihood ratio. With $x$ exceptions in $n$ days at coverage $p$,

$$
\text{LR}_{\text{POF}} = -2\ln\frac{(1-p)^{\,n-x}\,p^{\,x}}{(1 - x/n)^{\,n-x}\,(x/n)^{\,x}} \sim \chi^2_1 .
$$

For seven exceptions in 250 days at $p = 0.01$, the numerator's log is $243\ln 0.99 + 7\ln 0.01 = -34.678$ and the denominator's is $243\ln 0.972 + 7\ln 0.028 = -31.930$, so $\text{LR}_{\text{POF}} = -2(-34.678 + 31.930) = 5.50$. That exceeds 3.84, so the model fails at the 5% level, and falls short of 6.63, so it survives at 1%. Basel's traffic-light scheme puts five to nine exceptions in 250 days in the yellow zone, which is about the same verdict in a different dialect.

The count is half the story. Christoffersen (1998) asked whether the exceptions arrive independently or in bunches. A model that reacts too slowly to a change in regime does not miss randomly; it misses on Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday of the same bad week, and then catches up. His test treats the exception sequence as a two-state Markov chain, a system whose state tomorrow depends only on its state today, and compares the probability of an exception after a quiet day, $\pi_{01}$, with the probability of one after an exception, $\pi_{11}$. Theo's seven came in four clusters: three consecutive days in the week of the Halvorsen gap, two consecutive days a week later, and two isolated days. The transitions are $n_{00} = 238$, $n_{01} = 4$, $n_{10} = 4$, $n_{11} = 3$, so $\hat\pi_{01} = 4/242 = 1.7\%$ while $\hat\pi_{11} = 3/7 = 43\%$. An exception made another exception twenty-six times more likely. The independence statistic is

$$
\text{LR}_{\text{ind}} = -2\ln\frac{(1-\hat\pi)^{\,n_{00}+n_{10}}\,\hat\pi^{\,n_{01}+n_{11}}}{(1-\hat\pi_{01})^{\,n_{00}}\,\hat\pi_{01}^{\,n_{01}}\,(1-\hat\pi_{11})^{\,n_{10}}\,\hat\pi_{11}^{\,n_{11}}} = 13.49 \sim \chi^2_1, \qquad \hat\pi = \frac{n_{01}+n_{11}}{n_{00}+n_{01}+n_{10}+n_{11}},
$$

and conditional coverage, the sum, is $\text{LR}_{\text{cc}} = 5.50 + 13.49 = 18.98 \sim \chi^2_2$, against a 1% critical value of 9.21. The model fails, and the clustering says why: its memory is too long. A 60-day half-life took weeks to notice that October was not September, and a bucket assignment that updated monthly took longer still.

The backtest runs bucket by bucket as well as for the whole book: realized volatility of residuals divided by predicted, for each rating and price bucket, with a ratio near one meaning the bucket is honest. In October the sub-80 bucket's ratio was 2.6, which is the price-bucket bug of §8B.4 caught by its own statistics.

A failing backtest licenses a change, and the change has a procedure: a written finding, a challenger model run in shadow beside the champion, a comparison on data the challenger was not tuned on, and a promotion through the same rings and canaries that §27.8 uses for code. What the failure does not license is retuning the model in the drawdown that produced the exceptions. Two years earlier, in the week Larkspur Foam's loan fell to 84 on the drop-down headline (Chapter 13), a junior quant at the desk had shortened the EWMA half-life from 60 days to 15 on the third day of losses, "to make it more responsive." The re-run backtest was immaculate. It had been tuned on the very exceptions it was then graded on, which is look-ahead in a lab coat, and the shorter half-life spent the following quiet quarter overstating risk by 40% and squeezing the desk out of positions it would have been paid to hold. Mags's verdict at the time was the shortest model review in the desk's history: "You wrote the exam and then you graded it."

## 8B.9 The Limit Sheet

The risk committee met on the first Monday of November, and Mags prepared Theo for it the way she once prepared junior lawyers for a confirmation hearing: by telling him about the worst one she knew.

### Case Study: The Whale and the Five Limits (JPMorgan's Chief Investment Office, 2012)

"Everything you need to know about limit sheets," she said, "is in a Senate report about a bank that had a perfectly good one."

The book was the Synthetic Credit Portfolio of JPMorgan Chase's Chief Investment Office, a portfolio of credit index and tranche positions originally meant to hedge the bank's credit exposure. In the first quarter of 2012 its traders tripled its net notional, from \$51bn to \$157bn, including at least \$62bn in a US investment-grade credit index, \$71bn in a European investment-grade index and \$22bn in a US high-yield index (Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, *JPMorgan Chase Whale Trades: A Case History of Derivatives Risks and Abuses*, staff report, 15 Mar 2013, PDF p.12). The portfolio had five risk metrics with limits or advisories attached: VaR, CS01, a credit-spread-widening-of-10% measure called CSW10%, stress-loss limits and stop-loss advisories. In the first three months of the year it breached all five, and from 1 January to 30 April the limits and advisories were breached more than 330 times (PDF p.15).

Theo, who had been reading this chapter's drafts for a month, recognized every line. CS01 is his CS01 ladder. CSW10% is his DTS cell limit, a loss for a 10% relative widening, under another name. The stress limit is his stress library. Only the jump line is missing, and the report notes something close to it: concentration limits, "commonplace for the same instruments" at the bank's investment bank, were absent at the CIO (PDF p.15).

"So the sheet was fine," he said. "What went wrong?"

"Everything that happens *after* the sheet," Mags said.

In January 2012 the portfolio breached the VaR limit of both the CIO and the bank as a whole for four days, a breach reported to the bank's most senior management (PDF p.15). While it was in breach, an alternative VaR model was "hurriedly adopted," approved on 30 January, and it immediately cut the portfolio's VaR by 50%, from \$132mm to \$66mm (PDF pp.16, 188). The breach ended. The positions did not. A recommendation had been made to lower the CIO's VaR limit from \$95mm to \$70mm when the new model went live; the report found that it was not done, and summarized the result in a sentence Mags read aloud: "rather than remove the offending credit derivative positions to reduce the amount of risk in the SCP, JPMorgan Chase removed the brakes instead" (PDF p.191). The new model, it later emerged, ran on spreadsheets fed by hand and contained "calculation errors involving hazard rates and correlation estimates that improperly lowered the VaR results" (PDF p.194). On 10 May the bank revoked it and reinstated the old one, and the revised first-quarter VaR came out at \$129mm, twice what had first been reported (PDF pp.16, 21).

The CS01 limit had a different fate. The portfolio exceeded it by 100% in January, by 270% in early February and by more than 1,000% by mid-April (PDF p.15). By 30 March it had been in breach for 59 trading days. On 17 April an automated end-of-day notice read, in full, "MtM cs bpv limit is in excession by 1074% and has been in excession for 71 days" (PDF p.211). A risk measure called the Comprehensive Risk Measure had warned in February of a possible \$6.3bn loss over a year; it was dismissed internally as "garbage" (PDF p.15). The report's verdict on the breaches as a group was that they "were largely ignored or ended by raising the relevant risk limit" (PDF p.15). The portfolio lost at least \$6.2bn in 2012 (PDF p.12).

Kaz read the hazard-rate sentence twice. "They put the jump inside the spreadsheet," they said. "Hazards and correlations, hand-keyed, feeding a VaR. No separate layer, no backtest on the new model before it went live, and it was swapped in during the breach it was supposed to measure." They looked at the Larkspur half-life note still pinned above their monitor. "That's §8B.8 with ten figures."

Ines put it as a hand. "Five tells, all showing, for four months. Nobody at the table folded. They changed the deck instead."

What the desk took from the case is less a list of prohibitions than a description of what a limit is for. A limit is a pre-agreed conversation: it says which number, crossing which line, obliges which person to explain what to whom, by when. Its value is entirely in the conversation happening. A model change that halves the risk number is a limit change, whether or not anyone edits the limit, and so it goes to the same committee. A breach that lasts seventy-one days has stopped being a breach and become the new limit, decided by nobody. And an override is a loan of risk capacity, which is why on Theo's desk every override has a size, a reason, a name and an expiry date, and a second override of the same line in a quarter puts the line itself on the committee's agenda.

### The sheet

Here is the sheet Theo's desk runs, with the soft and hard thresholds that turn exposures into conversations *(illustrative levels for a \$400mm high-yield book)*.

*Table 8-B.4. The limit sheet. A soft breach starts a written explanation from the PM by noon; a hard breach requires a reduction by the close or a written override, with an expiry date, from the risk function's delegated approver; anything open for three days escalates to the desk head and the chief risk officer's office.*

Two rows carry ideas from earlier in the book. *Days to liquidate* is the 13-day model to the 13-week model's sibling, as Theo put it when he added a second file to the USB stick: a position of \$q$ in a bond that trades $\text{ADV}$ a day, sold at participation $\kappa$, takes $q/(\kappa\,\text{ADV})$ days to exit, and the limit bounds the forced-seller problem of §16.2 from the desk's own side. *Share of an issue* is the concentration limit the Whale did not have, and it bounds something no volatility measures: whether the desk *is* the market in a bond, in which case the mark is the desk's own opinion and the exit is a negotiation.

ROBO had been asked, early in the project, to "set sensible limits." It set every line to the book's current exposure plus one dollar, which was sensible in the sense that the book was inside every limit at the moment of setting, and put the desk in breach on eleven lines by 9:32 a.m. the following day. The specification it got the second time, in §8B.11, says where each threshold comes from.

The committee's first question that Monday was the one every risk committee asks. "Why is the ten-day ES 11% higher than on Friday, when the desk sold bonds on Friday?" Theo had the answer, because the model's change report had produced it at 7:30: on Friday evening one rating agency had completed a sector review of building products and downgraded twelve issuers, and the desk's rating rule (the middle of three agency ratings) had moved forty bonds from the B cells to the CCC cells. Every one of them now had a larger cell sensitivity, a higher idiosyncratic bucket and a tighter JTD limit. Nothing had traded. The book was riskier because the same bonds now meant something different, which is the DTS loading of §8B.2 working exactly as designed. The committee chair wrote one word on her pad, which Theo later learned was *good*.

## 8B.10 When to Apply It, and When It Breaks

A factor risk model is at its best on ordinary days in a book with many issuers, where its job is to say where the risk is and to stop the desk from building a concentration by accident. It is at its worst on the days it is most needed, and a seasoned risk manager reads it with four suspicions.

The first is that correlations are regimes, not constants. The rate–spread correlation $\rho_{y,s}$ changed sign twice between 2021 and 2025 (§8.2, §8.7), and in April 2025 it changed sign twice in seven sessions. The model therefore takes $\rho_{y,s}$ as a regime input from the hidden Markov model of §12.4 rather than as one estimated number; where §12.5's covariance holds the rate–spread correlation at zero for a multi-asset book, this engine supplies the regime value instead. The second is that correlations rise together in exactly the stress the model was built for, which is why the stress library shocks factors jointly rather than trusting the covariance to do it.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The dealer in March 2020.* A dealer's risk system sees the same failure from the other side of the trade. Describing March 2020 to analysts, Morgan Stanley's then chief financial officer said: "Historical relationships broke down and liquidity dried up. Clients reacted by reducing risk and raising cash" (Morgan Stanley first-quarter 2020 earnings call, 16 Apr 2020). For a dealer, broken relationships mean hedges that do not hedge and inventory that cannot be sold at the mark; for a buy-side desk, the same sentence means that the covariance estimated on calm years is a description of a market that no longer exists until the regime passes.

The third suspicion is about inputs. Stale marks understate volatility, because a moving average moves less than the thing it averages, and the de-smoothing of §8.5 and §12.5 is part of the model, not an option. And the model is only as good as the security master (§27.3) and the corporate-actions feed (Appendix B, §B.16): a bond that was exchanged, tendered or called and is still in the master at its old terms will carry a loading, a DTS and a JTD for a claim that no longer exists.

The fourth is numerical. A factor covariance with sixty factors can be nearly singular, and an optimizer fed such a matrix will find enormous positions that the matrix believes are riskless.

> **Math, Gently.** *The condition number: how close a matrix is to dividing by zero.* A covariance matrix stretches some directions and squashes others; the condition number is the ratio of its largest stretch to its smallest. A value of 10 means every direction is roughly equally well measured. A value of 10 million means some combination of factors looks almost riskless, usually because the data cannot tell two factors apart, and anything that inverts the matrix will lean on that combination hard. Tiny example: two factors with correlation 0.999 give a two-by-two correlation matrix with condition number $(1+0.999)/(1-0.999) \approx 2{,}000$. The engine raises an alarm above a threshold and shrinks harder (§7-B.5).

Finally, the hedges themselves have a band. A KRD or CDX hedge that is rebalanced on every wiggle pays the bid–offer all day, and one that is never rebalanced lets the mismatch become risk. If the mismatch $x$ drifts like a random walk with variance rate $s^2$, the desk pays $c$ per unit traded and charges itself $\tfrac{\gamma}{2}x^2$ per unit time for holding the mismatch, the best policy is to leave $x$ alone inside a band $[-b, b]$ and trade only at its edges. Inside the band the mismatch spends equal time everywhere, so its average squared size is $b^2/3$; pushing it back at the edges costs about $c\,s^2/(2b)$ per unit time. Minimizing $\gamma b^2/6 + c\,s^2/(2b)$ gives

$$
b^{*} = \left(\frac{3\,c\,s^{2}}{2\,\gamma}\right)^{1/3}.
$$

Since $\gamma$ is risk aversion times the variance of the factor being hedged, costly hedges get wide bands and hedges against volatile factors get narrow ones, and both only by the cube root: halving the cost narrows the band by about a fifth. That is the band in the KRD row of Table 8-B.4, and the derivation in hedge-matrix form belongs with §8.6.

> **Trend Watch.** *The volume inputs moved in 2026.* A liquidity factor built from trading volumes rests on series that changed definition this year. In its second-quarter 2026 release of 30 July 2026, published as ICE agreed to acquire it for \$167 a share in cash (an equity value of about \$6.0bn), MarketAxess said it was "suspending its practice of issuing monthly volumes press releases" (MarketAxess Q2-2026 release, pp. 4–5, 9; ICE 8-K, Exhibit 99.1). FINRA's affiliate-principal indicator, voluntary from 31 August 2026 and mandatory from 2 August 2027, suppresses the inter-affiliate leg of qualifying trades from dissemination (FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-16, 26 Aug 2026); FINRA's own baseline put such trades at up to about 5.7% of 2025 transactions, counted by trades rather than dollars (SR-FINRA-2026-009, pp. 19–21). Tradeweb's September 2026 report noted that the change lowered reported US high-grade TRACE volumes and that prior periods were not restated. *What it means for the desk:* any volume-based liquidity factor or days-to-liquidate input needs a structural-break dummy at 31 August 2026, and platform series cannot be spliced across it.
