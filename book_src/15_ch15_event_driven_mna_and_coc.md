# Chapter 15: Event-Driven M&A, Regulatory Spreads & Change-of-Control

> *"In the case of an acquisition, which the investor judges is a 20% probability, the risk of being short the sub notes is that they potentially will appreciate to 100 (almost a certainty if they contain a change of control put), a loss of 45."* (Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.270)

## Cold Open: Friday the Thirteenth, Aisle Four

Friday, December 13, 2024, 7:40 a.m. Theo's EDGAR alert fires on an 8-K from The Kroger Co., Item 2.04: *Triggering Events That Accelerate or Increase a Direct Financial Obligation.* Kroger has delivered a notice of special mandatory redemption on four tranches of the notes it sold in August to pay for Albertsons, \$4.7 billion in all, to be redeemed on December 18 at 101% of principal plus accrued interest. Three days earlier, a federal court in Oregon had enjoined the merger. Albertsons had terminated the agreement the same day.

The alert is the last page of a story the desk had been reading, not very carefully, for four months. It is worth going back to the first page.

**Tuesday, August 27, 2024.** The new-issue screen shows Kroger pricing \$10.5 billion of senior notes in seven tranches, from a 2026 out to a 2064. The money is for Albertsons, a deal signed on October 13, 2022, so long ago that the desk's newest analyst was a college junior when it was announced. The Federal Trade Commission and a group of states had sued to block it on February 26. Twelve days before the bond deal, Kroger had launched exchange offers for the Albertsons family of notes, and \$6,819,427,000 of the \$7,441,608,000 on offer had come in by the early deadline: Albertsons holders swapping into Kroger paper before anyone knew whether the two companies would ever be one.

Theo has the Fiftieth Supplemental Indenture open in one window and the pricing supplement in the other, because a systematic desk has not cured him of reading the definitions. The four shortest tranches, the 2026s, 2027s, 2029s and 2031s, \$4.7 billion together, carry a special mandatory redemption. The three long ones, the 2034s, 2054s and 2064s, \$5.8 billion, do not. If the merger agreement is terminated, or the deal has not closed by an outside date, Kroger must send a notice and redeem the four short tranches at 101% plus accrued, on a redemption date "no earlier than the third business day and no later than 30 days following the date of such notice" (Kroger 8-K, Exhibit 4.3, Aug. 27, 2024, EDGAR).

In his old job this was a page of the description of notes that the associates proofread at two in the morning. On this desk it is a payoff.

"Why a hundred and one?" Kaz asks. "Why not a hundred?"

"Because nobody wants to lend a company money for three years and get it handed back in three months at exactly what they paid," Theo says. "You've done the credit work, you've filled the slot in the portfolio, and now you have cash and a hole. The point is an apology for the hole."

"Question," Kaz says, reading over his shoulder. "Is 'special mandatory' stronger or weaker than 'mandatory'?"

"Weaker," Theo says. "I think. Mandatory redemption, in a sinking-fund bond, happens on a schedule whether anyone likes it or not. Special mandatory happens only if something goes wrong."

"So it is a mandatory that is optional."

"Ask me again when the judge rules," Ines says, without looking up.

Mags is more interested in which tranches got the clause. "Look at what they kept. If the deal dies, Kroger gives back the short money and keeps the long money. Thirty- and forty-year paper at those coupons is the hard part of any financing, and they wanted it whether or not they ever bought a single Safeway." That is the desk's reading, not Kroger's stated reason, and it will matter in December. For now it means that on one Tuesday the same issuer, with the same lawyers and the same ranking, sold seven bonds of which four had a deal-break option stapled to them and three did not.

**Thursday, November 14 to Tuesday, November 26, 2024.** The first thing on Theo's monitor on the fourteenth is somebody else's broken deal. Tapestry and Capri, sued by the FTC in April, have called off their merger, and Tapestry's 8-K that month reports the redemption of about \$6.1 billion of its own special-mandatory-redemption notes at 101, paid from cash on hand, with a \$61.1 million premium expensed (Tapestry 8-K, Nov. 2024, EDGAR). Theo prints it and tapes it to the side of his screen. "One point on six billion," he says. "That's the whole option, paid in one morning."

The Kroger hearing in Portland is over. It ran three weeks, and the lawyers made their closing arguments in mid-September (Grocery Dive, Sept. 17, 2024). Since then there has been nothing: no ruling, no leak, no guidance, just a judge with a record and a calendar nobody else can see.

"The hearing closed in September," Mags says. "The judge has had the file for two months. Somebody is getting a ruling for Christmas."

In the meantime the client flow has started to lean. Ines's screen shows a run of client sells in the Kroger 2027s, offered a few cents under par, around 99.90 *(illustrative price)*. The desk is a market maker; when clients sell, the desk buys, and the question of whether it wants to keep what it bought is now hers.

"The price already contains the judge," Kaz says. Kaz says this the way other people say the sun comes up.

"Then tell me what the judge is worth," Theo says. "If the deal breaks, these turn into a 101 claim that settles within thirty days of a notice, and the ruling might be three weeks away. What is that claim worth today? And what is the same bond worth if the deal closes and it's just a Kroger 2027?"

Kaz writes three numbers on a sticky note. If the deal closes, the 2027s are an ordinary three-year Kroger bond, and the issuer's other bonds say an ordinary three-year Kroger bond is worth about 99 *(illustrative)*. If the deal breaks, they are worth 101 plus a few months of accrued interest, discounted for a few weeks, about 101.5 *(illustrative)*. The market price, 99.90, sits between the two. "Where it sits is the probability," Kaz says. "Ninety-nine-ninety is nine-tenths of a point up a two-and-a-half-point ruler. Call it 36% that the judge blocks it." Section 15.4 turns that sticky note into an estimator, with all the ways it can lie. Kaz is pleased with it for about an hour.

Ines is not. "That's the pot and the odds. Now who at this table knows more than you? Those sellers are either telling us something about the judge, or telling us they need cash before year-end and don't care about a point. I'd like to know which before I size it."

The risk system is no help. It shows the 2027s with a spread duration of about 2.8, exactly like any other three-year Kroger bond, because the security master has a field for coupon, a field for maturity and a field for call schedule, and no field for a judge. Theo asks the risk manager whether the system could show two durations, one for each ruling. The risk manager asks which one should go in the morning report. Theo says both. The risk manager says the morning report has one column. This is the most important conversation of the week and it takes ninety seconds by the coffee machine.

"Here's how I'd size it," Ines says later, to nobody in particular, which is how she teaches. "If the judge blocks, I make about a point and a half on every bond, in a few weeks. If the judge lets it through, the bond drifts back to wherever the curve says an ordinary Kroger 2027 belongs, and I lose about ninety cents. At Kaz's 36% those two numbers cancel. That's what an implied probability *means*. So it's only a trade if I think the judge is more likely to block than the market does, and the worst case is ninety cents plus whatever Treasuries and the Kroger curve do to me while we wait. That's not a bet you lose big on. It's a bet you lose *slowly* on. So the size is whatever I can hold through a bad month in Treasuries without having to explain it to anyone."

"Then the judge is the smallest of the three risks," Kaz says.

"The judge is the only one of the three you get paid for knowing. The other two you just have to survive."

Theo looks at the three numbers and realizes that buying the bond is not one bet but three. It is a bet on the judge: a block turns a 99.90 bond into a 101-plus claim in a few weeks. It is a bet on the Kroger curve: if the deal closes, he owns a 2027 Kroger bond whose spread depends on how much debt Kroger took on to buy Albertsons. And it is a bet on Treasuries: the close leg is a three-year bond with a duration near three, the fail leg is a bond that matures in a month, and a rally in rates makes the close leg worth more than the redemption, which quietly turns the holder from long the deal-break into short it.

It is November 26. Do you buy the 2027s, how big, and which of those three bets are you making: the judge, the Kroger curve, or Treasuries?

**Tuesday, December 10 to Thursday, December 12, 2024.** The ruling arrives on a Tuesday: the court grants the preliminary injunction. Albertsons terminates the merger agreement the same day. On Wednesday, Kroger delivers its own termination notice and announces a \$7.5 billion share buyback, including a \$5 billion accelerated share repurchase (Kroger release, Dec. 11, 2024, EDGAR). On Thursday it sends the special-mandatory-redemption notice. The redemption date is set for Wednesday, December 18, the fourth business day after the notice, one day longer than the shortest clock the Fiftieth Supplemental Indenture allows.

Theo notices something on Wednesday that he will think about for a long time. The 2034s, the long bonds that never had the clause, move on the buyback news. For three months he had treated them as the control group, the clean Kroger bonds against which the 2027s' option could be measured. On the day the option paid, the control group moved too, for reasons that had nothing to do with a judge and everything to do with what a company does with \$4.7 billion it suddenly no longer needs to borrow.

The desk had kept a little under half of what clients sold it in late November, which Ines describes, depending on who is asking, as either disciplined or cowardly. Kaz's sticky note had said 36%. The judge had said 100%. Neither number had been available on November 26 at any price, which is the point of a probability.

Which brings us back to 7:40 a.m. on Friday the thirteenth.

"I don't understand," Kaz says, which is unusual enough that two people look up. "Nobody defaulted. Nobody was downgraded. Why would a company *mandatorily* redeem bonds at a *premium*?"

"Because they borrowed \$4.7 billion to buy a grocer they're no longer allowed to buy," Theo says, "and the supplemental indenture says that if the grocer goes away, the money goes back. At 101. Within thirty days of notice."

"So it's a refund with a tip."

"Price is what you pay," Mags says. "A hundred and one is what you get, eventually, after the judge."

Ines has already pulled the hand history: every print in the four short Kroger tranches since the August deal. She circles a cluster of client sells in late November, a few cents under par. "What was the pot, what were the odds, and who at this table knew more than us? Somebody sold the 2027s below par two weeks before a ruling that turned them into a 101 claim settling in five days. Either they knew something about the court's calendar that we didn't, or they needed the cash more than the point. The markouts will tell us which."

Theo plugs in the USB stick with his thirteen-week cash flow model, for emotional support. It has a row labelled "Regulatory" that has been blank for six years. He types "Dec 10: PI granted" into it and feels, for the first time, that the row has a purpose.

ROBO, asked to "price the redemption," returns 101.000. It is not wrong. It is just eleven days late.

Theo scrolls the timeline he has been building since August. October 13, 2022, signing. February 26, 2024, the suit. August 27, the bonds. Mid-September, closing arguments. December 10, the ruling. December 18, the cash. Twenty-six months from signing to ruling, and five business days from notice to money.

"Everything in this file is written as if the holder owns an option," he says, mostly to himself. "The redemption, the 101, the put in the old Albertsons bonds. But Kroger chose which tranches got the option. Kroger and Albertsons could have agreed to move the date. Kroger decided what to do with the money the morning after. And the only person who decided *when* any of it happened was a judge in Portland who has never heard of us." He writes it on a sticky note and puts it next to the Tapestry 8-K: *Who actually owns this option, and who owns the clock?*

The rest of the chapter is the answer, and it comes in three movements. The 101 change-of-control put looks like the holder's option, but its moneyness is an accident of the year the bond was printed and its survival is the issuer's choice (§15.2–15.3). The special mandatory redemption is the one place the market prices the clock honestly, and even it goes blind (§15.4). And the clock itself is a survival problem in which the deals still waiting are not a random sample of the deals that started (§15.5). The three cases, Kroger, Electronic Arts and Warner Bros. Discovery, are the same lesson told three times: the credit event was the paragraph nobody read, not the merger.

**The question this chapter answers:** *how do you price a cash flow whose trigger is a federal judge, whose strike was written into an indenture months before anyone knew how the judge would rule, and whose clock is set by a regulator who counts in working days?*

By the end of this chapter you will be able to: write every M&A-contingent cash flow in a bond, loan, convertible or CDS contract as an explicit payoff function of (deal outcome, trigger, price at trigger); price the 101 change-of-control put as a compound option whose moneyness is mostly a coupon-vintage accident; treat portability as a short digital option on leverage that the sponsor gets to choose; back a market-implied deal-break probability out of a special-mandatory-redemption tranche by curve-matching it against the same issuer's non-SMR bonds; model regulatory timelines with Kaplan–Meier, Nelson–Aalen, Cox and competing-risks estimators built from scratch; and recognize the moment a covenant you thought was an option is about to be defeased out from under you.

## 15.1 The Event-Driven Credit Map: Six Cash Flows That Fire on a Deal

### 15.1.1 Desk framing: the merger is the underlying, the regulator is the clock

An equity merger-arbitrage desk sees one number: the gap between the target's share price and the offer. A credit desk sees a cluster of contractual cash flows, each written by a different lawyer in a different year, each switched on by a different event, and each owned by a different kind of holder. The RSSG reader already reads these documents better than anyone on the floor. The new skill is to stop reading them as prose and start reading them as payoff functions.

Here is the one-line version of the whole chapter. Let $\kappa$ be the terminal state of a pending deal (close, abandon, litigated block) and $\tau$ the date it resolves. Every M&A-sensitive instrument has a price of the form

$$
P_0 \;=\; \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\, \sum_{k \in \{c,a,b\}} \mathbf{1}_{\{\kappa = k\}}\; e^{-\int_0^{\tau} r_u\,du}\; \Pi_k(\tau) \,\right] \;+\; \text{carry to } \tau,
$$

where $\Pi_k(\tau)$ is the value of the instrument immediately after outcome $k$ arrives at time $\tau$. Two hard problems hide in that expectation. The first is $\Pi_k$: what the bond, loan or convert is worth in each terminal state, which is where option theory, covenants and the acquirer's curve live (§15.2–15.4). The second is the joint law of $(\kappa,\tau)$: which outcome, and when, which is where HSR waiting periods, Second Requests, European Phase II, CFIUS, the Surface Transportation Board and district-court calendars live (§15.5). Practitioners spend their time on the first and guess the second. The second is where the money is, because the market prices the payoffs well and the clock badly (§15.6).

> **Math, Gently.** *What that expectation is doing: the market's book, three light switches and a discount factor.* Three ideas are packed into the formula, and each is simpler than its notation. The first is $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}$, the *risk-neutral* expectation. A bookmaker's odds are not the bookmaker's beliefs; they are the odds at which the book balances, so that enough money arrives on each side that the bookmaker stops caring who wins. Pricing "risk-neutrally" means pricing with the market's book, not with your own opinion: the probabilities under $\mathbb{Q}$ are whatever probabilities, dropped into a plain average, reproduce the prices people are actually paying. They contain the market's fear and its appetite, which is why they differ from the true odds, $\mathbb{P}$. The second idea is the *indicator* $\mathbf{1}_{\{\kappa = k\}}$. It is a light switch: it reads 1 if the deal ends in outcome $k$ and 0 otherwise. The sum over $\{c,a,b\}$ is a panel of three switches of which exactly one ends up on, so the sum simply picks out the payoff of whatever happens. The third is the *discount factor* $e^{-\int_0^{\tau} r_u\,du}$: a dollar that arrives at $\tau$ is worth less than a dollar today by the interest you could have earned in between, and with a constant rate it is just $e^{-r\tau}$. Now a tiny number, ignoring coupons. Give close, abandon and block $\mathbb{Q}$-probabilities of 0.70, 0.20 and 0.10, and say the bond is worth 100, 101 and 85 the moment each arrives. The book's average is $0.70 \times 100 + 0.20 \times 101 + 0.10 \times 85 = 98.7$. If resolution is half a year away and the rate is 4.4%, the discount factor is $e^{-0.044 \times 0.5} \approx 0.978$, and the price is about 96.6. That is the whole formula, done by hand. The full version, including which measure goes in which slot and what happens when you mix them, is §15.2.2 Step 6.

Ines puts it in poker terms. "The payoff is the pot. The clock is how many streets are left. Most people at the table can count the pot. Almost nobody can tell you the odds that the river gets dealt before the outside date."

### 15.1.2 The six contingent cash flows

Before the table, take three real instruments for a walk through it, because a table of clauses is easier to remember once you have seen a bond fall into one of its rows.

Start with the Electronic Arts 1.850% notes due 2031, printed in February 2021 when nobody could imagine wanting a 1.85% coupon to be worth less. On September 29, 2025, a sponsor group agreed to buy EA, and the 2031s fell into row 1: an investment-grade change-of-control put at 101, which pays only if the deal closes *and* the rating agencies take the notes below investment grade inside a window. Had the new owners decided simply to retire the bonds, they would have moved to row 3, the make-whole call, which lets the issuer repay at the greater of par and the present value of the remaining payments discounted at Treasuries plus a small fixed spread. For a 1.85% coupon in a 4% world that present value is well under par, so the make-whole would have cost exactly 100. Row 3, for a low-coupon bond, is a par call with extra paperwork. What EA's owners actually did belongs to neither row, and the case study in §15.2 tells it.

Next, the Kroger 2027s from the cold open. They live in row 2: a special mandatory redemption that pays 101 plus accrued if the deal dies, and pays nothing extra if it closes. The holder of a row-2 bond is long the break; the holder of a row-1 bond is long the close. The same desk can own both in the same week and root for opposite outcomes in the same courtroom.

Last, a loan. In June 2026 Warner Bros. Discovery refinanced a bridge with a \$13 billion term loan B at Discovery Global Holdings whose change of control, "such as the consummation of the previously disclosed proposed acquisition of the Company by Paramount Skydance Corporation," permits acceleration (WBD 8-K, June 2026, EDGAR). That is row 5, written with unusual candor: the trigger is named in the document, and the lenders signed knowing which deal would fire it (§15.8). Rows 4 and 6, the convertible's fundamental-change table and the CDS successor rules, wait for later chapters and for the forced-flow view below.

| # | Clause | Typical trigger | Payoff to the holder at trigger | Who owns the option | Forced flow it creates |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 | Change-of-control (CoC) put | HY: CoC alone. IG: CoC *and* a downgrade below investment grade by the named agencies within a window ("double trigger") | $(101 + \text{AI} - P^{\text{post}})^{+}$ via a repurchase offer | Holder | IG funds selling new fallen angels; HY funds tendering |
| 2 | Special mandatory redemption (SMR) | Deal terminated, or not closed by the SMR outside date | $101 + \text{AI}$ in cash within days, versus holding $P^{\text{close}}$ | Holder long a digital whose sign depends on rates | Short-tranche holders forced to reinvest; issuer forced to fund |
| 3 | Make-whole / optional call | Issuer chooses, usually post-close refinancing | $\max(\text{PV at } y^{\text{TSY}}+\text{spread}_{\text{mw}},\,100)$ | Issuer | Acquirer refinancing target debt |
| 4 | Convertible fundamental-change put and make-whole table | Cash merger, delisting | Par put, plus a conversion-rate increase read off a table indexed by stock price and date | Holder (put), contractual (table) | Convert-arb hedge unwind; delta collapses |
| 5 | Loan CoC event of default | Consummation of the CoC | Acceleration, so repayment at par or a refinanced loan | Lenders (often waived, priced as a consent) | CLO reinvestment; repricing |
| 6 | CDS successor determination | Succession event under the ISDA Definitions | Reference entity reassigned, or orphaned | Protection buyer and seller | Basis dislocation; orphaned protection |

Kaz reads row 3 and frowns. "Make-whole. Is that a spa?"

"It's where bondholders go to feel better about leaving early," Theo says. "Whether they get in depends on what state the spa is in."

He is only half joking. Row 3 looks like the tidiest entry in the table: the issuer's option, a formula, a Treasury rate. Outside bankruptcy it usually is, which is why a strategic acquirer refinancing target debt after closing simply pays it. The trouble starts when a deal fails and the target ends up in Chapter 11, as Spirit Airlines did (§15.7), or when a solvent company uses bankruptcy to repay expensive bonds early. Then whether a make-whole is owed at all turns on three things the formula does not show: the wording (does the premium attach only to an "optional redemption," or also to repayment after acceleration?), the circuit, and whether the debtor is solvent. The four decisions that a desk most often hears cited make the point:

| Case | Court | Setting | Outcome on the make-whole |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| *Energy Future Holdings* (*Delaware Trust Co. v. EFIH*) | 3d Cir., 2016 | Repayment in Chapter 11 of notes whose indenture required a premium on optional redemption | Owed: a voluntary Chapter 11 filing did not let the debtor escape the express premium |
| *Momentive* (*In re MPM Silicones*) | 2d Cir., 2017 | Notes accelerated automatically by the filing | Not owed: repayment after acceleration was not an optional redemption under that indenture |
| *Ultra Petroleum* | 5th Cir., 2022 | Solvent debtor | The make-whole is the economic equivalent of unmatured interest, disallowed in the ordinary case; owed here under the solvent-debtor exception |
| *Hertz* | 3d Cir., 2024 | Solvent debtor, unimpaired noteholders | Same characterization; a solvent debtor must pay post-petition interest at the contract rate, make-whole included (Chapter 16, §16.6) |

Read the table as a scoreboard, not as settled law: one practitioner tracker in June 2026 told its readers to treat the alignment as "current, not settled" (Nelson Mullins, *Red Zone* blog, "Make-Whole and Solvent-Debtor Circuit Scoreboard," June 17, 2026). What it means for the security master is that row 3 needs two fields, not one: the make-whole formula, and whether the indenture expressly attaches the premium to acceleration. The second field is worth nothing in a successful merger and a great deal in a failed one.

Two further entries are not cash flows at all but *counter-options held by the issuer or acquirer*: the consent solicitation that strips covenants before the trigger, and legal or covenant defeasance, which replaces the issuer's promises with a box of Treasuries. Both kill entry 1. Both appear in the case studies (§15.2, §15.8). An RSSG banker has drafted all eight. A systematic desk has to store all eight as fields in its security master, because a CoC put without its defeasance article is a payoff without its knock-out barrier. "Technically," Theo says. "It's an event of default because the lenders underwrote the old owner. In practice it means the loan gets refinanced at par, which a CLO loan trading at 99 thinks is the best news it's had all year."

### 15.1.3 The forced-flow view

Chapter 1 drew the credit ecosystem as a graph of who is a forced seller to whom. M&A adds edges to that graph on dates the deal calendar announces in advance, which is the rare case where a forced flow is both large and predictable. The easiest way to see the four edges is to let them all land in the same week. Take Wednesday, December 18, 2024, the day Kroger's redemption settled, and surround it with three composites of the kind any December calendar carries *(the last three are illustrative, not events of that week)*.

At nine in the morning the SMR edge pays. Kroger's \$4.7 billion arrives as cash in the accounts of the investment-grade funds that held the 2026s, 2027s, 2029s and 2031s, a month after Tapestry had repaid about \$6.1 billion the same way in November 2024. None of those holders planned to have the cash. Most of them are benchmarked to an index that just lost four bonds, so the cash has to go somewhere in the short end of the IG market by the end of the month, in a size that moves the short end of an issuer curve and of the index itself.

By lunchtime the desk is talking about the fallen-angel edge, because a sponsor buyout of an investment-grade company is due to close before month-end and the agencies have said where the ratings will land. When an IG target bought by a sponsor is downgraded to high yield at or near close, every IG-index-constrained holder becomes a seller within days, and HY funds become buyers only after the bond enters their index at a month-end rebalance. Dynkin's evidence that fallen angels outperform after the downgrade "due to price pressures around the downgrade event" (Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.411) is the price of that gap between the two calendars. If the bond also carries a CoC put struck at 101 and trades in the 80s, the edge is short-circuited: holders do not sell to HY funds, they put the bond back to the issuer. Unless the issuer finds a cheaper door (§15.8).

In the afternoon a cash merger closes on a company with a convertible outstanding, and the convert edge fires. A cash merger collapses a convertible's equity delta to zero on the close date and turns the bond into a par-plus-make-whole claim. The arbitrageur who was long the convert and short the stock must buy the stock back, and the stock-borrow fees that subsidized the trade disappear on the same day.

And somewhere in the loan market a borrower with a change-of-control event of default is bought, and the CLO edge fires. A loan repaid at par on a change of control hands CLO managers cash to reinvest and removes a loan from the collateral pool, which matters most for loans trading below par or bucketed in CCC (Chapter 21).

> **View From Another Seat.** *The CLO manager.* To a CLO manager the merger is barely the story. A loan bought at 99 and repaid at par on a change of control is a point of par build, which is welcome, and a reinvestment problem, which is not: in a tight market the replacement loan pays less spread or carries a worse rating, and the cash sits earning nothing while the manager looks. What the manager actually watches is the test sheet. A typical CLO limits loans rated CCC/Caa1 or below to 7.5% of the pool, marks the excess at the lowest market value in the CCC bucket, and runs within a few percentage points of its tightest overcollateralization test (Kundu, ESRB Working Paper 141, March 2023). So a merger that takes a CCC loan out at par is a gift that loosens the bucket, and a merger that leaves a B3 borrower with two more turns of debt is a downgrade waiting to land in it. The same deal that an equity arbitrageur prices as a spread, and a credit desk prices as a put, the CLO manager prices as a change in two ratios on a monthly trustee report.

Each edge is a known flow on an unknown date. The rest of the chapter is about estimating the date.

> **Trend Watch.** *The 2023–2026 mega-deal and IG take-private wave.* The aggregate value of transactions reported under the Hart–Scott–Rodino (HSR) Act rose from about \$1.6 trillion in FY2023 *(approx.; FY2023 report, not rechecked)* to \$2.1 trillion in FY2024 and \$2.5 trillion in FY2025, while the number of reported transactions stayed roughly flat (1,805, 2,031 and 2,006); transactions above \$1 billion were "approximately one-fourth" of reported transactions in FY2024 and 31.8% in FY2025 (FTC/DOJ *Hart-Scott-Rodino Annual Reports*, FY2023–FY2025, Appendix A). Fewer, bigger deals means more of the market's event risk sits in IG capital structures. The marquee examples are all 2025–26: Electronic Arts agreed on September 29, 2025 to be taken private by PIF, Silver Lake and Affinity Partners at \$210 per share, about \$55bn of enterprise value, described in the announcement as "the largest all-cash sponsor take-private investment in history" (EA press release, Sept. 29, 2025, EDGAR); Union Pacific agreed on July 29, 2025 to acquire Norfolk Southern at about \$85bn of enterprise value, filing an application of roughly 7,000 pages with the Surface Transportation Board on December 19, 2025, which the Board rejected as incomplete on January 16, 2026; the railroads refiled on April 30, 2026, and on May 28, 2026 the Board found the revised application complete but asked for supplemental information (Surface Transportation Board decisions; Union Pacific releases, EDGAR); and Warner Bros. Discovery signed with Netflix in early December 2025 before signing with Paramount Skydance on February 27, 2026 at \$31.00 per share in cash plus a per-diem ticking fee, closing October 6, 2026 (WBD 8-Ks, EDGAR). What it means for the desk: a sponsor buying an IG company with 2020–21 vintage low-coupon bonds outstanding creates a deep-in-the-money CoC put on an IG security, which the IG market had not had to price at scale for a generation, and the issuer's response (tender, consent strip, defeasance) is now the main determinant of what that put pays.

A generation is long enough for a market to forget how its smoke detectors work, and the IG market had last tested this one in earnest when RJR Nabisco's bondholders learned what an LBO does to an investment-grade coupon. The next section takes the 101 put apart, starting with the question those bondholders learned to ask too late: who has to agree that there is a fire, and who gets to unplug the alarm?

## 15.2 Anatomy and Pricing of the 101 Change-of-Control Put

### 15.2.1 Desk framing: a smoke detector with two licensed inspectors

A high-yield change-of-control put is a smoke detector. When someone acquires control of the issuer (typically more than 50% of the voting stock, or a sale of substantially all of the assets), the issuer must offer to buy back every note at 101% of principal plus accrued and unpaid interest. The offer goes out within a fixed number of days after the event, often 30, and stays open for a window usually measured as 30 to 60 days; then the repurchase settles. Each holder chooses: tender at 101, or keep the bond and live with the new owner.

The investment-grade version is a smoke detector that goes off only if there is a fire *and* two separately licensed inspectors agree, within sixty days, that it is a fire. The defined term is usually "Change of Control Repurchase Event" or "Change of Control Triggering Event," and the EDGAR counts in §15.3 show that one or the other appears in hundreds of registered IG prospectuses a year. The trigger needs a change of control *and* a "Rating Event": the notes are rated below investment grade by each of the named agencies (sometimes all three, sometimes two of three) within a window that typically opens at the earlier of the change of control and its public announcement, runs for about 60 days after the change of control, and stays open while any agency has the notes publicly under review for downgrade. That drafting is typical, not universal. Read the definition in the document you actually own, because the gap between "each of" and "any two of" is a probability you are about to estimate.

Why 101? Because the drafters of the late 1980s wanted the holder to be worth at least par after a leveraged buyout, and a point on top made the tender offer clean. The premium is the tip. The put is the *par*. Remember this the next time someone tells you the 101 is "only a point": on a bond trading at 77, it is twenty-four points.

Mags was there. "In 1988 we learned about event risk from RJR Nabisco, the deal that taught investment-grade bondholders they were lunch. Then we spent two years inventing the poison put." (The measured damage is in Asquith & Wizman (1990) and Warga & Welch (1993); the covenant response is in Crabbe (1991), Lehn & Poulsen (1991) and Cook & Easterwood (1994).)

She tells the rest of it whenever someone on the desk says "investment grade" as if it were a promise. In the autumn of 1988 a management group and then KKR bid for RJR Nabisco with money borrowed against RJR itself, and the company's existing bonds, investment-grade paper owned by insurers who had bought them to sleep at night, were suddenly the junior claims on a company that would carry several times the debt. "Nobody had lied to them," Mags says. "That was the worst part. The indenture didn't say the company couldn't borrow twenty-odd billion dollars. It just never occurred to anyone that it would." One large insurer sued on the theory that the bonds carried an implied promise not to be levered out of investment grade, and lost: the court read the indenture and found no such promise (*Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. RJR Nabisco, Inc.*, S.D.N.Y. 1989). The academic papers above put numbers on what LBOs of that era cost unprotected bondholders. The market's answer was to write the promise down, and the 101 put is the descendant of what it wrote. "Back in my day you found out about an LBO from the tape and about your covenants from your lawyer, in that order," Mags says. "Now the tape and the lawyer are both on Theo's screen and he still has to read page forty."

**When the put matters, and when it is decoration.** Four things have to be true at once for a change-of-control put to be worth real money. The coupon has to be below the break-even coupon $c^*$ of §15.2.2 Step 4, or the post-close bond trades above 101 and nobody tenders. The acquirer has to be a sponsor or a weaker credit, because a stronger strategic parent pulls the bond onto its own curve and the investment-grade Rating Event never fires. The indenture must not let the issuer covenant-defease its way out of the repurchase covenant; if it does, the put is worth at most the gap between 101 and the cost of a box of Treasuries, and a sponsor will close that gap for you. And no consent solicitation can be live, or likely, before the trigger, because once a majority strips the covenant the minority's put goes with it. The put breaks when any one of the four fails, and the failures are not equally visible. Coupon and acquirer are on the screen. A consent solicitation arrives with a press release. The defeasance article sits in the back half of a base indenture dated years before the bond was printed, often in a section the prospectus summarizes in a sentence. That is why a seasoned analyst checks the third condition first: it is the one the market is most likely to have missed, and the only one the issuer can exercise *after* the put is already deep in the money.

Theo has a question that he has been asking since his first week. "But what does the indenture say? Not the summary. The definitions." He is right to ask, because every term in the payoff below is a defined term, and each defined term is a parameter.

### 15.2.2 Derivation: the put as a compound option

**Notation for this chapter.** The style bible reserves $\tau$ for default time. The deal-resolution date from §15.1 is the first event this chapter cares about, so we write $\tau_c$ for the closing date when the deal closes, and keep $\tau$ for default where it appears. Leverage collides with loss given default $L = 1-R$, so pro-forma leverage is $\ell_{pf}$, defined as debt over EBITDA (or over $\text{EV}$ where stated). Deal-clock hazards are $h_k(t)$, never $\lambda$, which stays reserved for default intensity.

**Setup.** A bond pays an annual coupon $c$ (percent of par, paid semiannually) and matures at $T$. A deal is signed at $t_0$; if it closes, it closes at a random time $\tau_c$. Let $\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{deal}\}}$ indicate that it closes and $\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}}$ that the contractual trigger fires (the change of control alone in HY; the change of control *and* the Rating Event in IG). The strike is $K = 101$ plus accrued interest. Under $\mathbb{Q}$ with short rate $r_u$, the put is worth

$$
V_{\text{CoC}}(t_0) \;=\; \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\, e^{-\int_{t_0}^{\tau_c} r_u\,du}\; \mathbf{1}_{\{\text{deal}\}}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}}\,\big(K - P^{\text{post}}_{\tau_c}\big)^{+} \,\right],
$$

where $P^{\text{post}}_{\tau_c}$ is the price the bond would trade at immediately after closing if nobody tendered. It is a compound option: an option (the deal closes and triggers) on an option-like payoff (the put is worth something only if the post-close bond trades below the strike).

> **Math, Gently.** *A compound option is a raffle ticket that enters you in a second raffle.* Buy a ticket in the first draw and you win nothing directly; you win the right to a ticket in the second draw, where the actual prize is. The change-of-control put works the same way. The first draw is the deal: it has to close, and in investment grade the agencies have to agree it is a fire. Winning that draw hands you the second ticket, which is an ordinary put struck at 101 on whatever the bond is worth after closing. The second ticket can still be a blank: if the post-close bond trades at 104, a put at 101 is worth nothing. Tiny number *(illustrative)*: a 60% chance of winning the first draw and, given that, a second ticket worth 10 points on average gives $0.6 \times 10 = 6$ points, before discounting, *if* the two draws are unrelated. The next two steps are about why, in an LBO, they are not. The full version is the formula just above and Steps 1–2 below.

**Step 1: factor what can be factored.** Assume (A1) the closing time is independent of rates, so the discount factor can be pulled out at the expected close $\bar\tau_c$ as $B(t_0,\bar\tau_c)$, and condition on the deal:

$$
V_{\text{CoC}} \;\approx\; \pi_d\, B(t_0,\bar\tau_c)\; \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}}\,(K - P^{\text{post}})^{+} \,\middle|\, \text{deal}\right],
\qquad \pi_d = \mathbb{Q}(\text{deal}).
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *Conditional expectations, and why the umbrella gets wetter than you think.* The bar in $\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid \text{deal}]$ is read "given": average only over the states of the world in which the deal closes, and ignore the rest. That is all a conditional expectation is, an average over a filtered list. The trap comes when you multiply two averages that are secretly related. Suppose you want to know how wet your umbrella gets in a year. "The probability I carry an umbrella" times "the average rainfall" understates it badly, because you do not carry the umbrella at random; you carry it when it rains. The two are positively related, and their *covariance* is the measure of that relationship: positive when they tend to be large together, zero when they are unrelated. The IG ratings trigger is the umbrella. It fires when post-close spreads are wide, which is exactly when the post-close bond is cheap and the put is deep in the money. Tiny number: say the trigger fires in half the states of the world; in those states the put's intrinsic value is 24 points, and in the other half it is 0. The honest average of "trigger times intrinsic" is $0.5 \times 24 = 12$. The shortcut, "probability of trigger" times "average intrinsic," gives $0.5 \times (0.5 \times 24 + 0.5 \times 0) = 6$. The shortcut is off by a factor of two, and that factor, $M = 2$, is the conditional moneyness ratio of Step 2. The full version, the covariance identity and the definition of $M$, is in "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section.

**Step 2: do not factor the trigger.** It is tempting to write the inner expectation as $\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\cdot\mathbb{E}[(K-P^{\text{post}})^+]$. That is assumption (A2), independence of trigger and payoff, and it is false in exactly the deals you care about. The ratings leg fires when the post-close spread $s^{\text{post}}$ is high, and a high spread is precisely when $P^{\text{post}}$ is low and the put is deep in the money: the umbrella is carried when it rains. So the honest inner expectation is the shortcut *plus* a positive covariance, and it is convenient to package that correction as one multiplier, the **conditional moneyness ratio** $M$: the put's average intrinsic value in the states where the trigger fires, divided by its average intrinsic value across all states. In the umbrella box the put is worth 24 points when the trigger fires and 12 on average, so $M = 2$, and the shortcut prices the put at half its value. With $M$ in hand, the put is $V_{\text{CoC}} \approx \pi_d\,\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\,B\,M\,\mathbb{E}[(K-P^{\text{post}})^+]$. For a sponsor LBO, $M>1$ and the independent approximation understates the put. For a strategic IG acquirer, $\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\approx 0$ because the Rating Event cannot fire when the parent is a stronger credit, and the put is worthless however far below 101 the bond trades. Microsoft buying Activision is the textbook strategic case: a AAA parent means the second inspector never shows up. The covariance identity and the definition of $M$, written out in full, are in "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section.

**Step 3: price the bond at the trigger.** With $t_i$ the remaining coupon times and $y^{\text{TSY}}_t$ the Treasury zero curve,

$$
P^{\text{post}} \;=\; \sum_{i} \tfrac{c}{2}\,e^{-(y^{\text{TSY}}_{t_i} + s^{\text{post}})\,t_i} \;+\; 100\,e^{-(y^{\text{TSY}}_{T} + s^{\text{post}})\,T},
\qquad s^{\text{post}} \;=\; s_{\text{acq}} + \Delta_{\text{struct}} + \Delta_{\text{lev}}(\ell_{pf}),
$$

where $s_{\text{acq}}$ is the acquirer's (or new capital structure's) spread at the relevant tenor, $\Delta_{\text{struct}}$ the penalty for legacy notes left structurally subordinated at an operating company or holding company, and $\Delta_{\text{lev}}$ a Merton-style map from pro-forma leverage to spread (Chapter 6). Discount on the post-close curve, never on the target's pre-deal curve: after the trigger the target's pre-deal curve is a historical document.

> **Math, Gently.** *Present value, the annuity factor, and a derivative as a slope.* Present value is a time machine for money: a payment due in $t$ years is worth $e^{-yt}$ of itself today, because that is the amount which, invested at yield $y$, grows into the payment. A bond's price is just a stack of these, one for each coupon and one for the principal. Use the table below. Into a sponsor LBO the post-close yield is 7.9%, so the 100 of principal due in 4.5 years is worth $100\,e^{-0.079 \times 4.5} \approx 70.1$ today. The coupons are nine half-yearly payments of 0.925 on the 1.85% bond, and once each is discounted they add up to about 6.9. Price: about 77.0. Strike minus price: $101 - 77 = 24$ points of put. The *annuity factor* $A$ is the value today of one point of annual coupon, paid half a point at a time over the bond's life; here it is about 3.71, less than 4.5 because the later half-points are discounted more. A *derivative* is nothing more than a slope: how much the price moves per unit of something else. Because price is coupon times $A$ plus a principal that does not depend on the coupon, the slope of price in coupon is simply $A$. Every extra point of coupon adds about 3.7 points of price, which means it removes 3.7 points of put. Twenty-four points of put divided by 3.71 points per point of coupon is about 6.5 more points of coupon before the put is worth nothing: $1.85\% + 6.5\% \approx 8.3\%$. That is the break-even coupon $c^*$, and the straight line that hits zero there is the hockey stick in Figure 15.2. The full version is the derivative and formula immediately below.

**Step 4: coupon vintage dominates.** Differentiate $P^{\text{post}}$ in the coupon:

$$
\frac{\partial P^{\text{post}}}{\partial c} \;=\; \tfrac12\sum_i e^{-(y^{\text{TSY}}_{t_i}+s^{\text{post}})t_i} \;\equiv\; A(s^{\text{post}}),
$$

the annuity factor per point of annual coupon. Price is linear in coupon with slope $A$, so the intrinsic value $(K - P^{\text{post}})^+$ is a hockey stick in $c$ with a kink at the **break-even coupon**

$$
c^{*}(s^{\text{post}}) \;=\; \frac{K - 100\,e^{-(y^{\text{TSY}}_T+s^{\text{post}})T}}{A(s^{\text{post}})}.
$$

Any bond with $c < c^*$ has an in-the-money put if it triggers. Plug in a remaining life of 4.5 years and a flat Treasury zero rate of 4.4% *(illustrative throughout)*. Into a strategic acquirer at 60bp, $y = 5.0\%$, $A \approx 4.08$, $100e^{-yT}\approx 79.9$ and $c^*\approx 5.2\%$. Into a sponsor LBO at 350bp, $y=7.9\%$, $A\approx 3.71$, $100e^{-yT}\approx 70.1$ and $c^*\approx 8.3\%$. The full grid:

| Bond (4.5 years remaining, *illustrative*) | Coupon | $P^{\text{post}}$, strategic buyer (60bp) | $P^{\text{post}}$, sponsor LBO (350bp) | Intrinsic if triggered, LBO |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| 2021 vintage | 1.85% | ≈87.4 | ≈77.0 | ≈24.0 |
| 2023 vintage | 5.00% | ≈100.3 | ≈88.7 | ≈12.3 |
| 2009-style high coupon | 6.875% | ≈107.9 | ≈95.6 | ≈5.4 |

Same issuer, same acquirer, same trigger: a four-to-one spread in what the put is worth, driven entirely by the year the bond was printed. The put does not care about the credit story. It cares about $101 - P$. This is aha number one of the chapter: **moneyness is a coupon-vintage accident**, and the 2020–21 vintage of sub-3% IG coupons turned every one of those bonds into a deep-in-the-money LBO put the day rates went to 4%. "Nobody wanted to buy it at 1.85% either," Mags says. "They bought it anyway. Now they get paid for their regret."

**Step 5: the trigger probability.** The ratings leg is a classification problem. A serviceable model is a logistic

$$
\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d} \;=\; \sigma\!\left(\alpha + \beta_{\ell}\,\ell_{pf} + \beta_{s}\,\mathbf{1}_{\text{sponsor}} + \beta_{r}\,\text{notch}_{\text{acq}}\right),\qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
$$

fit on past deals, with $\text{notch}_{\text{acq}}$ the acquirer's rating in notches below AAA. It returns roughly one for sponsor LBOs that add five or more turns of debt and roughly zero for IG strategics. The interesting cases are in the middle: an IG acquirer that levers up for a large deal and promises to deleverage, where the agencies' "under review" language holds the window open and the answer depends on whether the issuer's deleveraging plan is believed.

> **Math, Gently.** *The logistic function is a dimmer switch.* Feed $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$ any number from minus infinity to plus infinity and it hands back a number between 0 and 1, which is what you want from something you intend to call a probability. At $z = 0$ the light is half on: $\sigma(0) = 0.5$. At $z = 2$ it is mostly on, $\sigma(2) \approx 0.88$; at $z = -2$ it is mostly off, $\sigma(-2) \approx 0.12$; by $z = 5$ it reads 0.993 and you cannot tell it from fully on. The model's job is to add up evidence in $z$. Each turn of pro-forma leverage adds $\beta_{\ell}$; being a sponsor adds $\beta_s$; each notch of weakness in the acquirer adds $\beta_r$. A sponsor that adds five turns of debt pushes $z$ so far to the right that the dimmer is pinned at "on," and an AA-rated strategic buyer sits so far to the left that it is pinned at "off." Neither case needs a model. The deals that do are in the middle of the dial, where the switch is most sensitive: the slope of $\sigma$ is $\sigma(1-\sigma)$, largest at $z=0$, where one unit of evidence moves the probability by about a quarter. That is the arithmetic behind "it depends on whether the deleveraging plan is believed." The full version, with fitting and calibration on small samples, is §7-B.3.

**Step 6: say which measure.** A $\pi_d$ backed out of the equity merger-arbitrage spread is a risk-adjusted, $\mathbb{Q}$-like number: Mitchell & Pulvino (2001) show that risk-arbitrage returns behave like a short position in index puts, so market-implied failure probabilities exceed physical ones, especially in bad markets. A hazard estimated from historical deal outcomes (§15.5) is $\mathbb{P}$. Never put the two in one formula without a risk-premium adjustment and a sentence saying so.

> **Math, Gently.** *$\mathbb{P}$ versus $\mathbb{Q}$, as an insurance premium versus the actuarial odds.* A house insurer's actuary may know that one house in a thousand burns down each year, yet the premium is priced as if it were two in a thousand. The gap is not a mistake. It pays the insurer for holding a risk that arrives all at once, in bad years, when capital is scarce. Merger arbitrage is the insurance business for deals. Arbitrageurs who buy the target's stock are selling insurance against a break, and they charge for it. Suppose the equity spread on a pending deal, read as a probability, implies a 25% chance of failure, while the historical record for comparable deals says 15%. The ten points between them are the price of fear. Mitchell & Pulvino (2001) show why the fear is rational: risk-arbitrage returns look like those of a short position in index puts, paying steadily in calm markets and losing together when markets fall, which is exactly when deals break. So which number goes where? The 25% is a $\mathbb{Q}$ number and belongs in a pricing formula such as $V_{\text{CoC}}$ when the question is "what should this bond trade at?" The 15% is a $\mathbb{P}$ number and belongs in an expected-PnL or risk calculation, such as the integral in §15.5.2, when the question is "what will I actually earn, on average, and how often will I be wrong?" The trade, if there is one, is the gap. The full treatment of the change of measure is in Chapter 6 and §6-B.6.

**Step 7: bound the Bermudan error.** The holder actually has a window of 30–60 days to tender, so the put is Bermudan-ish, not European. Treat it as European at $\tau_c$ and bound the error by the value of waiting: over a 45-day window the post-close bond moves by roughly $D^{\text{spr}}\,\sigma_s\sqrt{45/365}$, a point or two for a four-year bond with 150bp of annual spread volatility. That is small against twenty-four points of intrinsic and large against half a point; the bound tells you when to care.

> **Math, Gently.** *A random walk, and why risk grows with the square root of time.* A man leaves a lamppost and takes one step a second, each step left or right at random. After 100 steps he is not 100 steps away; on a typical night he is about 10 away, because the steps partly cancel. Distance grows with the square root of the number of steps, not with the number itself. Make the steps tiny and very frequent and the drunk's path becomes the Brownian motion $W_t$ of stochastic calculus, whose spread after time $t$ is $\sqrt{t}$. Spreads behave like that walk over short horizons, which is why an annual spread volatility $\sigma_s$ is scaled to a shorter window by $\sqrt{t}$, not by $t$. Tiny number: 150bp of annual spread volatility over a 45-day tender window is $150 \times \sqrt{45/365} \approx 53$bp. Multiply by a spread duration of about 3.7 for the four-and-a-half-year bond and the post-close price can wander about 2 points while the window is open. That is the Bermudan error bound: the most that waiting to decide inside the window can plausibly be worth. The full version of the random walk, and of the calculus built on it, is in Chapter 6 and §6-B.2.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** (1) *The covenant is not the cash flow.* A consent solicitation can strip the change-of-control covenant before the trigger (Warner Bros. Discovery, June 2025, §15.8), and covenant defeasance can release the issuer from it after the trigger (Electronic Arts, August 2026, below). Model the issuer's counter-option or your put is a payoff without its knock-out barrier. (2) *Accrued interest and record dates.* The repurchase price is 101 plus interest accrued to, but excluding, the repurchase date; if that date falls after a record date, the coupon goes to the record holder and the repurchase price drops accordingly. Off-by-one-coupon errors are worth 1–3 points on a high-coupon bond. (3) *Structural subordination.* Tapestry's 2023 prospectus states that the new notes would be "structurally subordinated to certain indebtedness assumed in connection with the Capri Acquisition"; legacy target notes left at an operating subsidiary can be senior *or* junior to the new money, and $\Delta_{\text{struct}}$ can have either sign. (4) *The wrong curve.* Discounting at the target's pre-deal spread overstates $P^{\text{post}}$ for LBOs and understates it for strategic buyers. (5) *The definition you did not read.* "Each of" versus "any two of" the agencies; a window that ends 60 days after the change of control versus 60 days after its announcement. Each is a different $\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}$.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Veronesi_Fixed_Income_Securities p.470:* "The key to the dynamic replication is that the issuer behaves as the model prescribes it should. However, exercising an American option optimally is not mandatory, and it is perfectly reasonable that the issuer may exercise sub-optimally."
>
> **Annotation.** Veronesi is writing about callable bonds, where the issuer's option is to call early and the model assumes it does so optimally. The change-of-control put inverts the roles but keeps the lesson: the *holder* owns the put, and the *issuer* owns a set of counter-options (tender below 101, strip the covenant by consent, defease, refinance through an exchange offer) that the textbook payoff omits. A 2026 desk prices the put net of those counter-options, and the issuer's choice among them is not a random variable with a nice distribution; it is a cost-minimization problem the sponsor solves with expensive lawyers. Where the modern desk departs from Veronesi: we do not assume the counterparty is sub-optimal by accident. We assume it is optimal for a payoff we forgot to write down, and we go find the clause.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Schmid_Credit_Risk_Pricing_Models p.153:* "Credit options offer protection against changing default free interest rates and changing credit spreads. Hence, they allow hedging of mark-to-market exposure to fluctuations in spreads and interest rates: hedging long positions with puts, and short positions with calls."
>
> **Annotation.** Schmid's credit put is a traded instrument whose strike is a spread. The change-of-control put is a credit put embedded in the bond, with a strike fixed in price (101), a trigger written by a lawyer, and an exercise window set by an indenture. The table above shows why the embedded version is also a *rates* option: most of the intrinsic value on the 1.85% notes comes from Treasury yields, not from the LBO spread. A desk that hedges an LBO-candidate portfolio with CDS index protection is hedging the spread leg and staying unhedged on the leg that drives moneyness. Where the modern desk departs: the embedded put is not tradeable on its own, so its value shows up only as a cross-sectional spread anomaly across coupon vintages of the same issuer (exercise 3).

#### The Full Derivation: the trigger covariance and $M$

Step 2 replaced the independence shortcut with the identity

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}}(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right]
\;=\; \pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right] \;+\; \operatorname{Cov}\!\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}},\,(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right).
$$

The ratings leg fires when the post-close spread $s^{\text{post}}$ is high, and a high spread is precisely when $P^{\text{post}}$ is low and the put is deep in the money. The covariance is positive. Define the **conditional moneyness ratio**

$$
M \;=\; \frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\mid \text{trig}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right]} \;=\; 1 + \frac{\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}},(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right)}{\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right]},
$$

so that $V_{\text{CoC}} \approx \pi_d\,\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\,B\,M\,\mathbb{E}[(K-P^{\text{post}})^+]$, the expression used in Step 2. The second equality for $M$ follows by dividing the identity by $\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\,\mathbb{E}[(K-P^{\text{post}})^+]$ and using $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}}X] = \pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid\text{trig}]$ for $X = (K-P^{\text{post}})^+$. When trigger and payoff are independent the covariance vanishes and $M = 1$; Listing 15.1's third check measures $M$ by repricing with the trigger probability replaced by its path average.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**What ROBO built from the vague version.** Theo typed "price the CoC put on the U.S. Steel 6.875% 2029s." ROBO priced a vanilla European put struck at 101 on the bond using the target's pre-deal spread volatility, ignored the acquirer, ignored the trigger, and returned a healthy number for a bond that was about to migrate onto a stronger parent's curve and trade above 101. It did exactly what it was told.

*The precise spec for Listing 15.1 (inputs, outputs, edge cases and the variance-reduction rule) is collected with the chapter's other two in [The Engine: Full Specifications](#ch15-engine-specs), just before The Spec You Hand the Robot in §15.9.*

Here is the trouble with Theo's first request, and with every honest attempt to answer it in a spreadsheet. The put pays only if the deal closes and the trigger fires. It is worth something only if the post-close bond trades below 101. And those two facts are tangled together, because the ratings leg fires when the spread is wide, which is exactly when the put is deep in the money. Pricing one bond that way is an afternoon. Pricing every coupon vintage of one LBO candidate, then repricing the lot when Treasuries move fifteen basis points or when Kaz asks what happens if the sponsor defeases, is a week nobody wants. So the desk writes the pricer once, tests it against cases whose answers are known in advance, and lets ROBO call it.

Listing 15.1 is three small functions and a page of checks. `tsy_zero_curve` is a fixed Nelson–Siegel shape for continuously compounded Treasury zero rates, labeled illustrative because it is; any function from tenor to rate can replace it. `bond_price` counts coupons backward from maturity, builds a matrix of discount factors at the Treasury zero plus a spread for every tenor, and multiplies it by the cash flows, so a vector of spreads comes back as a vector of prices in one pass. `price_coc_put` does the derivation's work. It draws post-close spreads from the sampler you supply, clips them at zero, reprices the bond at its life remaining after `t_close`, and takes the shortfall against 101 as intrinsic value. The deal and trigger events are never simulated. Each path carries `deal_prob`, the discount factor, the trigger probability, one minus the defeasance probability, and intrinsic value, multiplied together, and the average is the answer. Because the trigger probability can be a function of that path's spread, the covariance from Step 2 survives, and the reported `moneyness_ratio` is the $M$ of the derivation.

The four checks do real work. The first collapses the sampler to one spread and matches the closed form to ten decimals. The second shows that a 6.875% bond priced into a tight acquirer curve is exactly worthless. The third builds an LBO from a mean-preserving lognormal spread and a logistic ratings leg, then reprices with the trigger probability replaced by its path average. The seed is fixed, so both runs see the same draws and the gap is the covariance, not noise. The fourth lets the sponsor defease whenever intrinsic value exceeds five points.

> **Math, Gently.** *Normal versus lognormal, and the half-sigma-squared that keeps turning up.* A normal distribution is the symmetric bell: it puts as much probability a given distance below the mean as above it, and it will cheerfully produce negative numbers. That is fine for a change in spread and wrong for a spread level, because a credit spread cannot be minus 40bp. A *lognormal* is what you get when the logarithm of a quantity is normal: the quantity itself is always positive, and it grows and shrinks in percentages, so a 350bp spread is as likely to go to 700 as to 175. That is why the sampler in Listing 15.1 is lognormal, and why clipping spreads at zero is there only as a guard against a sampler someone swaps in later. The lognormal has one quirk worth knowing by heart. Its long right tail drags the mean above the median by a factor of $e^{\sigma^2/2}$, where $\sigma$ is the volatility of the log. So if you want draws whose *mean* is 350bp, you cannot simply center the log at $\ln 350$; you have to shift the log-mean down by $\tfrac12\sigma^2$. That is all "mean-preserving" means. Tiny number: with $\sigma = 0.5$, $e^{-0.125} \approx 0.88$, so the median draw is 88% of the mean, about 309bp for a 350bp mean. This same $\tfrac12\sigma^2$ is the correction Itô's lemma forces on any lognormal process: when a quantity moves by percentages, its logarithm drifts down by half the variance, and Chapter 6 meets it in the firm-value dynamics of the Merton model. It is the tax code of stochastic calculus, and it is not optional. The full version is in Chapter 5 (the lognormal) and Chapter 6 (Itô's lemma).

Use it to rank a book of LBO candidates by embedded put value and to see which leg a price leans on. Do not use it where a consent solicitation has already stripped the covenant, unless you set the trigger probability to zero yourself. It treats the tender window as a single date, discounts at a known rate, and prices the post-close bond off today's zero curve rather than a forward curve. The edge case that bites is the coupon grid. Keep `maturity_years - t_close` on the half-year grid. Off it, `bond_price` returns a dirty price without complaint and the code compares it with a flat 101, understating the put by the accrued interest, up to half a coupon.

*The full listing is [Listing 15.1 in Appendix F](#code-15-1).*

Two things to notice in the output. First, the 1.85% bond's put is worth something like eighteen to nineteen points at signing under these illustrative inputs, which is most of the gap between where an LBO candidate's low-coupon bonds trade and where its high-coupon bonds trade. Second, check (4) sends the value to roughly zero: a sponsor that defeases whenever defeasing is cheaper than paying 101 leaves the holder with nothing but the scheduled cash flows. That is not a hypothetical.

### Case Study: Electronic Arts 2025–26, the In-the-Money Put That Got Defeased

This story takes one sentence to tell and ten months to live: a sponsor bought a company whose bonds carried a 101 put deep in the money, and did not pay it. The desk watched it happen across three meetings, and was right about it in the first one without knowing what to do with being right.

**Tuesday, September 30, 2025: the morning after.** The morning meeting has one item, and it was announced the evening before. On September 29, 2025, Electronic Arts agreed to be acquired by PIF, Silver Lake and Affinity Partners for \$210 per share in cash, about \$55 billion of enterprise value and a 25% premium to the unaffected close of \$168.32 on September 25, with PIF rolling its existing 9.9% stake. The announcement called it "the largest all-cash sponsor take-private investment in history" (EA press release, Sept. 29, 2025, EDGAR).

The equity desk two rows over is arguing about the premium. The credit desk is looking at two bonds. EA had two series outstanding from February 2021: \$750mm of 1.850% Senior Notes due 2031 and \$750mm of 2.950% Senior Notes due 2051. Their prospectus supplement gives holders a "Change of Control Repurchase Event" offer at "101% of the aggregate principal amount of the notes to be repurchased plus any accrued and unpaid interest" (EA 424B2/424B5 prospectus supplement, Feb. 2021, EDGAR). Run them through Step 4. With 2026 Treasury yields in the 4s, the 2031s were worth roughly 89–90 at Treasury flat and the 2051s, a 2.95% coupon with a quarter-century left, far less *(illustrative)*. Add any LBO spread and both are deep in the money. Mark this as the cleanest real-world example of aha number one: the most valuable thing about the EA 2031s was the year they were printed.

Kaz has already run the §15.2 engine, because Kaz runs engines the way other people check the weather. A sponsor take-private of this size will be financed with a great deal of debt, so the logistic of Step 5 is pinned at "on": both agencies will take the notes below investment grade, and the trigger probability given a close is close to one. The deal has a long regulatory road but a motivated buyer. Feed in a sponsor-style post-close spread of 350bp *(illustrative)* and the 2031s' put is worth something in the high teens of points; the 2051s', on a bond whose price before the news was in the mid-60s, is worth more than forty. "The single most valuable thing EA owns this morning," Kaz says, "might be a promise it made in 2021 to buy back its own bonds." By lunchtime the 2031s have rallied hard, and the 2051s, near 65 before the LBO news, would trade up toward 90 on hopes of 101 (Fancourt, Bond Vigilantes, Feb. 16, 2026).

Theo asks for the indenture. Not the prospectus supplement, which describes the put, but the base indenture from 2016 that the 2021 notes were issued under, with its supplements. He reads it the way he used to read credit agreements at RSSG, from the definitions out, and on the second pass he finds what he is looking for: the article on legal defeasance and covenant defeasance. The issuer may deposit with the trustee enough U.S. Government Obligations to pay every scheduled payment on a series, and on doing so be released from a list of covenants. Theo cannot tell from a first read whether the change-of-control repurchase covenant is on that list in a form that survives, and he says so. But the possibility is there in the text, and it changes the payoff completely.

Nobody listens, in the specific way a desk does not listen to a correct point that has no trade attached. Ines asks whether it is in the price. Theo does not know. Kaz says that defeasance means the holder is paid in full in advance, so why would a holder mind? "Because 'in full' means the scheduled payments," Theo says. "Not 101. On a 2.95% bond due 2051, the scheduled payments are worth a lot less than par at today's Treasury yields." Mags says, "Write it down. Nobody will read it." He types one line into the free-text comment field of the security master: *base indenture has covenant-defeasance article; check whether CoC repurchase covenant is released.* The field has no structure and no alert. It will sit there for four months.

**Wednesday, February 11 to Tuesday, February 17, 2026: the tender.** On February 10, EA launches tender offers for both series, scheduled to expire on July 30. We do not reproduce the tender consideration from the offer documents here; pull them before citing it. The market's reading arrives within the week. On Monday, February 16, David Fancourt, writing as a guest on Jupiter's Bond Vigilantes blog in "Electronic Arts: How to madden bondholders," lays out the price path for the 2051s (near 65 before the LBO news, near 90 on hopes of 101) and puts the tender equivalent near 74. His estimate is that defeasing the bonds instead of honoring the change-of-control offer saves the buyers over \$250mm. On Tuesday the *Cloud 9fin* podcast episode "What's in the EA endgame?" (Feb. 17, 2026) says the LBO "upended expectations" for the 2031 and 2051 bonds, with investors weighing a tender and a defeasance strategy that could strip covenants instead of a straightforward 101 payout.

Mags reads the post twice. "They're tendering below par for a bond with a 101 put?"

"They're tendering below par for a bond they think doesn't have a 101 put," Theo says. "The tender is the defeasance, offered in advance at roughly the same price. Take it now, or take it in August."

Ines runs the hand, because that is what she does when a price moves for reasons nobody on the desk predicted. "What's the pot? The gap between 101 and whatever a box of Treasuries costs, times a billion and a half of face. What are the odds? That depends on whether the sponsor's counsel reads the indenture the way Theo does, and I'd bet they read it first. Who at this table knows more than us? The sponsor's counsel, obviously. The tender agent. Theo, since September, which nobody acted on." She pulls up the desk's prints. "And whoever has been selling us 2051s since Tuesday. That's the person I'd like to have lunch with."

Kaz reruns the engine with the fourth check switched on: a `defeasance_prob` callable that returns one whenever the put's intrinsic value exceeds a few points, because a sponsor defeases precisely when defeasing is cheaper than paying 101. The put's value collapses to roughly zero, as check (4) promised it would, and the bond is worth only its scheduled payments, discounted at something close to Treasury yields because a Treasury deposit is about to stand behind them. For the 2051s that is a number in the low 70s *(illustrative, at long Treasury yields near 5%)*, which is to say a little below where Fancourt put the tender.

It is February 17. You own the 2051s, marked near 90. The tender is worth about 74. The sponsor is signalling defeasance. Do you tender, hold, or hit the street bid, and why?

**March to July 2026: the boring part.** For five months almost nothing happens, which on a desk means a great deal of work. Theo files a ticket asking the data team to turn his free-text comment into a structured field, `defeasance_releases_coc`, with three allowed values: yes, no, and "read it yourself." The ticket is triaged as low priority, because no position limit depends on it. In April the acquisition notes price, and Kaz replaces the illustrative 350bp in the engine with a spread read off the new 7.25% secured coupon, which changes the put's value on the screen and nothing else, since the `defeasance_prob` callable has already set it to zero. Ines spends a week reconciling the desk's EA marks against two pricing vendors who disagree by four points on the 2051s, one of which is still pricing the put and one of which is not. The vendor that is still pricing the put is wrong, but it is wrong in the direction that makes everyone's month-end look better, and that conversation takes longer than the reconciliation. By July the ticket has been upgraded, because a risk manager has asked why the desk's two EA bonds have two different stories in two different systems. Nobody has asked whether the answer is in the indenture.

**Tuesday, August 4, 2026: the close.** All regulatory approvals were in hand by July 30, 2026, and the deal closed on August 4, 2026: about ten months from signing to close.

The August 4, 2026 closing 8-K describes the financing: a first-lien term loan B of \$6,125mm plus €1,725mm, a term loan A of \$3,250mm, a \$500mm revolver, and notes issued on April 8, 2026 consisting of \$2,875mm of 7.250% senior secured notes due 2033, €1,080mm of 6.250% senior secured notes due 2033 and \$2,500mm of 8.750% senior notes due 2034, with 40% equity claws at 107.25, 106.25 and 108.75 (EA 8-K, Aug. 4, 2026, EDGAR). A 7.25% secured coupon on the new money tells you where $s^{\text{post}}$ was going to be for the old money.

The tender had expired on July 30. Only \$68.830mm of the 2031s and \$7.922mm of the 2051s were tendered, leaving \$681.170mm and \$742.078mm outstanding (same 8-K). About 5% of the face accepted. What the participation tells you is that 95% of holders preferred whatever came next.

What came next was one sentence. At closing, Parent "caused the Company to defease certain obligations" under the 2016/2021 indenture by irrevocably depositing U.S. Government Obligations sufficient to pay principal, premium, if any, and interest when due (same 8-K). Our reading, which the reader should check against the indenture's defeasance article and the agencies' rating actions: a deposit of Treasuries that covers every scheduled payment satisfies the payment obligations, and under a covenant-defeasance provision it releases the issuer from covenants including the repurchase covenant. The holders now own a Treasury-collateralized 1.85% or 2.95% bond that pays on schedule. They do not get 101 today. The sponsor's arithmetic is the Step 4 table read backwards: defeasing costs the present value of the remaining cash flows at Treasury yields, about 89–90 per 100 for the 2031s and considerably less for the 2051s *(illustrative)*, against 101 for a put that pays in cash. On roughly \$1.4bn of remaining face, the saving is in the low hundreds of millions *(illustrative)*, consistent with Fancourt's February estimate of over \$250mm.

So, back to February 17. The engine had already given the answer, and it is less dramatic than the price path. Once the `defeasance_prob` callable is switched on, the put is worth about nothing and the bond is worth its scheduled payments at near-Treasury yields, so the three doors compare like this. *Tendering* at about 74 bought you out at roughly the defeased value, perhaps a point or two above it: little loss against what you would end up owning, little gain either, and an end to the option that the sponsor might, for some reason, choose to pay 101 after all. *Holding* left you with roughly the same economic value in August plus that option, which was worth little but not zero while the deal was pending, and which a holder who cared about tracking error, or who was not allowed to own a Treasury-backed orphan, might value differently. *Hitting the street bid* was the best door if it was open at a price materially above the defeased value, because anyone paying you 85 or 88 for the 2051s that week *(illustrative)* was buying a put that a defeasance article had already knocked out. The 95% who stayed were not obviously wrong; the tender did not pay them to leave. The people who were wrong were the ones who bought at 90 on the theory that 101 was a contract. Exercise 1 asks you to do the arithmetic properly.

What do the 95% own now? A 2.95% bond due 2051 whose payments are backed by a deposit of Treasuries, with no covenants worth the name, no change-of-control put and, for practical purposes, no Electronic Arts credit risk. It is a long Treasury strip with a corporate CUSIP and a coupon from a different interest-rate era: about sixteen years of duration and almost no spread *(illustrative)*. They were promised an option on a sponsor's balance sheet. They were handed duration.

**What the desk should have flagged.** Not "LBO candidate, low coupon, long the put." The right flag was "LBO candidate, low coupon, *and the base indenture contains a covenant-defeasance article that the CoC covenant does not survive*." That is a document feature, and it belongs in the security master as a field alongside the trigger definition (Chapter 13 treats document features as state variables; Chapter 25 extracts them with NLP), not as a line of free text in a comment box that nobody queries. In the pricing engine, it is the `defeasance_prob` callable: the issuer's counter-option is exercised when it saves money, which is exactly when the put is deep in the money. Kaz priced the put. Theo read the defeasance section. Theo was right.

> **View From Another Seat.** *Three seats, one defeased bond (generic descriptions, no firm's stated view).* **The insurer.** For a life insurer's asset-liability book, a defeased 2051 is a curious gift: long, Treasury-backed duration that matches long liabilities and is cheap in capital terms, but that yields roughly what Treasuries yield, which is not why the insurer bought a corporate bond. The ALM desk does not mourn the put. It asks whether the bond still earns its place in a portfolio built to harvest spread, and usually concludes that it is now a Treasury that is harder to sell. **The multi-strategy relative-value pod.** For a pod running IG relative value, the EA story was a curve trade from September to August: the 2031s against the 2051s, through the tender, on the question of which series the sponsor would treat better and when. The pod's risk manager wants it flat the new 8.75% notes, because those are a high-yield bet on the new company, and an IG relative-value book that is accidentally long a levered buyout is no longer the book the risk committee approved. **The opportunistic credit fund.** For a fund that buys complexity, the 7.25% secured print in April was the most informative number of the year: it marked the new senior curve, and it made the legacy unsecured notes look structurally junior to more than \$9 billion of secured debt if they were neither repaid nor defeased (§15.2, blow-up 3). At 65, a bond with three exits (paid at 101, defeased at Treasury value, or left as junior unsecured paper behind a secured stack) is worth buying if two of the three exits clear the price. At 90, only one does.

Mags, finishing her 1988 story: "Then in 2026 a sponsor learned about the poison put and spent one afternoon inventing a Treasury deposit." She considers this. "It's good work. I hate it."

The book's most expensive three words, "open market purchase," have a cousin, and it is "defease certain obligations."

> **Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality.** *Myth:* the 101 put protects you in an LBO. *Reality:* it protects you if the bond trades below 101, the trigger fires, both inspectors agree inside the window, the covenant survives every consent solicitation between signing and closing, and nobody defeases it. Otherwise it protects the billing of the sponsor's counsel.

## 15.3 Portability: The Short Option You Did Not Know You Sold

### 15.3.1 Desk framing

Kaz hears the word in a morning meeting and brightens. "Portability. Like a phone number? Bondholders keep their coupon when they switch carriers?"

"Close," Ines says. "The issuer keeps the number. You get dropped."

A portability clause, usually drafted as a "Specified Change of Control Event" or a "Permitted Change of Control" carve-out, says that a change of control which would otherwise trigger the 101 put does *not* trigger it if conditions hold. Typical conditions, in the order a covenant analyst checks them: the change of control happens within a window after issue (two or three years is common); pro-forma leverage on the closing date is at or below a test, often the leverage at issuance or a fixed multiple; the carve-out can be used once; and sometimes the acquirer must meet a size or experience test. The details vary by deal and the analyst's job is to read them. The pricing job is the same every time: the holder sold the issuer a digital option on leverage, and the sponsor gets to choose the leverage.

**When portability bites, and when it is a footnote.** Three conditions have to line up. The sale has to happen inside the window, which is why portability matters most in the first two or three years after a sponsor-friendly deal and fades as the clock runs. The buyer has to be able to finance the purchase at or below the leverage test, which means a buyer that wants to keep the existing debt in place because it is cheaper than anything it could raise today. And the add-back definition has to be generous enough to make the test passable, which is the subject of the derivation below. When all three hold, the clause is worth real money to the sponsor and costs the holder the put in exactly the states where it would have paid. When the buyer is a strategic acquirer that will refinance the target's debt anyway, portability is irrelevant: nobody ports a capital structure they are about to retire. A real case makes the point about wording. The *Cloud 9fin* episode "Focus and Foresight" (May 11, 2023), in which Will Caiger-Smith talks to covenant expert James Wallick, asks "when is a change in control not a change of control?" about Focus Financial's \$7bn buyout, financed without refinancing the company's existing debt, and the answer turns on the wording of the credit agreement. For the definition itself, the portability segment of *Cloud 9fin*'s "Amigo Loans, Portability, Shipping" (Sept. 3, 2021) is the covenant explanation; the derivation that follows is the pricing. The seasoned check is the first condition, because it is a date: if the window has closed, the rest of the analysis is a history lesson.

### 15.3.2 Derivation: a short digital on a number the counterparty chooses

**Exogenous version.** If pro-forma leverage at the change of control, $\ell_{\tau_c}$, were an exogenous random variable, the holder's put would survive portability only when the test fails:

$$
V^{\text{port}}_{\text{CoC}} \;=\; V_{\text{CoC}}\cdot \mathbb{Q}\!\left(\ell_{\tau_c} > \bar\ell \,\middle|\, \text{deal},\text{trig}\right),
$$

and the issuer's digital is worth $V_{\text{CoC}}\cdot\mathbb{Q}(\ell_{\tau_c}\le\bar\ell\mid\cdot)$. With $\ln \ell_{\tau_c}\sim\mathcal{N}(\ln \ell_0 + \mu\tau_c,\ \sigma_\ell^2\tau_c)$, the survival probability is $\Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln\ell_0 + \mu\tau_c - \ln\bar\ell}{\sigma_\ell\sqrt{\tau_c}}\right)$, a barrier problem with a known answer. It is also the wrong model.

**Endogenous version.** The acquiring sponsor sets new debt $F_{\text{new}}$ *after* reading $\bar\ell$. Let $E^{\text{rep}}$ be reported EBITDA at closing, $a\ge 0$ the add-back ratio the definition permits, so that the test is computed on $E^{\text{adj}} = (1+a)E^{\text{rep}}$, and let $F_{\min}$ be the least debt that finances the purchase given the largest equity cheque the sponsor will write. The sponsor can pass the test whenever $F_{\min} \le \bar\ell\,(1+a)\,E^{\text{rep}}$, and it will pass whenever the cost of the 101 offer exceeds the value of the turns of leverage it gives up. Take the limiting case in which the put is always more expensive than the forgone leverage (true for the deep-in-the-money puts in §15.2). The put survives only on the event

$$
\mathcal{A} \;=\; \left\{ E^{\text{rep}} < \frac{F_{\min}}{\bar\ell\,(1+a)} \right\}.
$$

Put a lognormal on reported EBITDA, normalized so that its mean is one at signing, and the probability that the holder keeps the put is the share of that distribution lying below the barrier $F_{\min}/(\bar\ell(1+a))$. Add-backs move the barrier to the left. With $F_{\min} = 6.05$ turns of signing EBITDA and a 5.0x test, the put survives only if EBITDA comes in below $6.05/5.0 = 1.21$ times its signing level when no add-backs are allowed, and below $6.05/(5.0 \times 1.2) \approx 1.01$ when 20% add-backs are: twenty points of "adjusted" move the barrier from comfortably above the expected outcome to almost exactly on it. The closed form, a normal probability, and its derivative in $a$ are in "The Full Derivation" at the end of §15.3.

Two conclusions. First, every point of permitted add-back lowers the probability that the holder keeps the put, and the derivative is largest exactly where the test is close to binding. Mags has been "deeply suspicious of anything called adjusted" for thirty years; here is the derivative that justifies her. Second, the put that survives lives on $\mathcal{A}$, the states in which EBITDA came in low. Those are the states where the holder most wants the put, so the residual is not worthless. What the holder sold is the put's value in the *good* states, where the bond would have traded a few points below 101 and the sponsor would otherwise have had to pay up. This is a contract-design argument under $\mathbb{P}$; pricing the residual put is under $\mathbb{Q}$ and uses the §15.2 engine restricted to $\mathcal{A}$.

Ines turns it into a hand. "You are not playing against the deck. You are playing against a player who sees the test before choosing the bet. If he can make the bet that passes, he will. You only get paid when he cannot."

Ines's hand is easy to say and annoying to size. A covenant analyst can read the add-back definition and report that it is generous; she cannot tell you what a generous definition does to the probability that your put exists at all. The closed form above answers that for one lognormal and one set of inputs. A desk screening a stack of portability clauses wants the answer across a grid of add-back ratios, a proof that the machinery agrees with the formula, and a look at which states of the world the surviving puts live in. That is a dozen lines of code, and a dozen lines are cheap to verify.

`portability_put_survival` is the endogenous version from §15.3.2 with the sponsor's decision made mechanical. It draws reported EBITDA at close as a lognormal with mean one, so signing EBITDA is the unit of account. It computes `f_min`, the least debt that finances the purchase, as one minus the maximum equity share, times the enterprise value multiple, in turns of signing EBITDA. Then it asks, path by path, whether even that minimum debt exceeds the leverage test applied to adjusted EBITDA, which is `lev_test` times one plus the add-back times the draw. If it does, the sponsor cannot port and the put survives. The function returns three numbers: the share of paths on which the put survives, the mean EBITDA on those paths, and the mean on the paths where the deal ports. The last two carry the lesson, because they show the surviving put living in the weak states and the ported deals in the strong ones. It prices nothing. The residual put's value comes from running the §15.2 engine on the surviving states.

Three checks guard it. Survival never rises as the add-back grows across a five-point grid; because the seed is fixed, every grid point sees the same draws, so noise cannot break that. At a 20% add-back the simulated frequency sits within four binomial standard errors of the closed-form normal probability from the derivation. And mean EBITDA given survival is below mean EBITDA given porting.

Use it to screen a corpus of portability clauses and to turn "generous add-back definition" into a number. Do not use it for a sponsor who would sooner pay 101 than give up turns of debt, since the model assumes the sponsor ports whenever it can. It also tests one gross multiple of reported EBITDA at close, so a test on net debt, on coverage, or on a base fixed at issuance needs different code. The edge case that bites is an empty side. Push the volatility toward zero with the test far from binding, or raise the add-back until nothing survives, and the survival probability is exactly one or zero while one conditional mean comes back as `nan`. That `nan` will travel quietly into any table that averages across clauses.

*The full listing is [Listing 15.2 in Appendix F](#code-15-2).*

With these illustrative inputs a sponsor needing 6.05 turns of signing EBITDA, against a 5.0x test, ports the deal whenever adjusted EBITDA clears about 1.2 times its signing level with no add-backs, and whenever reported EBITDA clears about 1.0 with 20% add-backs. The survival probability falls from the mid-80s percent to below 40% across the grid. Same bond, same sponsor, same test; the only thing that moved was the word "adjusted."

#### The Full Derivation: the put's survival probability under portability

With $\ln E^{\text{rep}}\sim\mathcal{N}(-\tfrac12\sigma_E^2,\ \sigma_E^2)$ normalized so that $\mathbb{E}[E^{\text{rep}}]=1$ at signing,

$$
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}) \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln\!\big(F_{\min}/(\bar\ell(1+a))\big) + \tfrac12\sigma_E^2}{\sigma_E}\right),
\qquad \frac{\partial\,\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})}{\partial a} \;=\; -\frac{\varphi(\cdot)}{\sigma_E\,(1+a)} \;<\; 0 .
$$

The probability is a standard normal CDF evaluated at the log-distance between the barrier and the median of $E^{\text{rep}}$, measured in units of $\sigma_E$; the $+\tfrac12\sigma_E^2$ is the mean-preserving shift of §15.2's lognormal box. The derivative follows from the chain rule through $-\ln(1+a)$ in the argument, and its magnitude is largest where $\varphi$ peaks, at an argument of zero, which is the case in which the test is close to binding.

## 15.4 Special Mandatory Redemptions: The Deal-Break Gauge in the Acquirer's Curve

### 15.4.1 Desk framing: a bond with a deal-break digital stapled to it

An acquirer that has signed a deal but not closed it has a timing problem. It wants its financing locked now, because a committed bridge loan is expensive and the bond market can shut in a week. It does not want to own \$5bn of debt and no target if the regulator says no. The special mandatory redemption solves both. The acquirer sells bonds today; if the merger agreement is terminated, or the deal has not closed by an outside date, it must redeem them, usually at 101 plus accrued, within days of a notice. Kroger's supplemental indenture sets the redemption date "no earlier than the third business day and no later than 30 days following the date of such notice" (Kroger 8-K, Exhibit 4.3, Aug. 27, 2024, EDGAR).

Three drafting details decide the price, and the RSSG reader will find all three in the description of notes:

- **No escrow.** Synopsys's March 2025 prospectus says it plainly: "The proceeds from this offering will not be deposited into an escrow account" (Synopsys 424B5, Mar. 2025, EDGAR). The 101 is an unsecured promise by the acquirer, so between issue and redemption the holder is long the acquirer's credit.
- **An extendable outside date.** Tapestry's SMR fired if Capri was not acquired by February 10, 2025 "(or such later date mutually agreed between us and Capri)" (Tapestry 424B5, Nov. 2023, EDGAR). The clock belongs to the two parties who most want the deal to live.
- **The tranche carve-out.** Kroger put the SMR on its four short tranches (2026, 2027, 2029, 2031; \$4.7bn of \$10.5bn) and not on the 2034, 2054 and 2064 bonds. HPE did the reverse for Juniper: SMR on the 2029, 2031, 2034 and 2054 notes, none on the 2026 and 2027 notes. Synopsys put it on the 2027, 2028, 2030 and 2032 notes and not on the 2035 and 2055 notes (prospectuses on EDGAR).

Kaz reads the Kroger description of notes and asks whether "special mandatory" is stronger or weaker than ordinary mandatory.

"It's the kind of mandatory," Ines says, "that the issuer and the target can agree to postpone."

The carve-out is a gift. Same issuer, same day, same ranking, same lawyers, and one tranche carries an option the next one does not. Whatever price gap the curve cannot explain belongs to the option, and the option's only underlying is whether the deal dies.

### 15.4.2 Derivation: the implied break probability and the duration jump

**Setup.** An SMR tranche pays coupon $c$ (percent of par, semiannual) and matures at $T$. Collapse the deal's resolution to a single date $T_f$ on which the redemption would settle if the deal failed, and let $\pi_f = \mathbb{Q}(\text{deal fails by } T_f)$. The **fail leg** is the value today of the coupons paid on or before $T_f$ plus the redemption price:

$$
G_f \;=\; \sum_{t_i \le T_f} \tfrac{c}{2}\,e^{-y_f t_i} \;+\; \big(101 + \text{AI}_{T_f}\big)\,e^{-y_f T_f},
\qquad y_f \;=\; y^{\text{TSY}}_{T_f} + s_{\text{acq}}(T_f),
$$

where $\text{AI}_{T_f}$ is interest accrued since the last coupon date (30/360 on US corporates) and $y_f$ is the acquirer's short yield, not the Treasury rate, because without escrow the 101 is the acquirer's credit risk. The **close leg** $P^{\text{close}}$ is what the same cash flows would be worth as an ordinary bond of the acquirer, read off the issuer's non-SMR curve at maturity $T$.

**Assumptions.** (B1) The resolution date is known; a random $T_f$ adds a time integral but changes none of the algebra. (B2) The acquirer's curve is the same whether the deal closes or breaks. (B2) is the weak link and gets its own blow-up below.

**Price and inversion.** Under (B1)–(B2),

$$
P_0 \;=\; \pi_f\,G_f \;+\; (1-\pi_f)\,P^{\text{close}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\hat\pi_f \;=\; \frac{P^{\text{close}} - P_0}{P^{\text{close}} - G_f},\qquad P^{\text{close}} \ne G_f .
$$

**The sign flips with rates.** Differentiate:

$$
\frac{\partial P_0}{\partial \pi_f} \;=\; G_f - P^{\text{close}} .
$$

If rates or the acquirer's spread have risen since issue, $P^{\text{close}} < G_f$ and the SMR is a free put on the deal: the holder is paid 101 for a bond worth less, and roots for the judge. If rates have fallen, $P^{\text{close}} > G_f$ and the holder is short a digital: a break takes away a bond worth more than 101. In between, near $P^{\text{close}} = G_f$, the SMR price carries no information about $\pi_f$ at all and $\hat\pi_f$ explodes. The gauge's resolution is proportional to $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f|$. A thermometer that reads only when it is out of equilibrium is still a thermometer; you just have to know when it is switched off.

**A worked number** *(illustrative)*. Take a three-year 4.60% SMR tranche, the shape of Kroger's 2027s, whose non-SMR-curve value is $P^{\text{close}} = 99.00$. The ruling is expected in a quarter, so $T_f = 0.25$, and $y_f = 4.60\%$. No coupon falls before $T_f$; accrued is $4.60 \times 0.25 = 1.15$; so $G_f = 102.15\,e^{-0.0115} \approx 100.98$. If the tranche trades at 99.40,

$$
\hat\pi_f \;=\; \frac{99.00 - 99.40}{99.00 - 100.98} \;=\; \frac{-0.40}{-1.98} \;\approx\; 0.20 .
$$

The bond market thinks there is a one-in-five chance the judge blocks the deal. That number is free of the takeover premium, the target's standalone value and the equity arbitrageurs' borrow costs, which is why it is cleaner than the one backed out of the target's stock. Figure 15.3 shows the price line, the sign flip and the blind spot.

![Figure 15.3: Left, an SMR tranche's price is a straight line in the break probability whose slope changes sign when rates move; right, the gauge goes blind as the close-leg price approaches the fail-leg price. Illustrative: the three-year 4.60% tranche of Section 15.4.2 (fail-leg value 100.98, resolution in a quarter), with a 0.10-point price error on the right.](assets/figures/ch15_smr_gauge.png)

**Probabilistic duration.** Dollar durations add across the two legs, so the SMR tranche's duration is a value-weighted average of the fail leg's duration, which is about $T_f$, and the close leg's, $D^{\text{close}}$. The weight $w$ on the fail leg is its share of the price, which is close to the break probability itself when both legs trade near par. In the example, $w \approx 0.20$ and $D^{\text{close}} \approx 2.8$, so $D^{\text{SMR}} \approx 2.3$. On ruling day it becomes either 0.25 or 2.8. The hedge ratio does not drift; it jumps, on a date the court has published. Ben Dor documents the same discontinuity for rating changes, where a downgraded IG bond's limit hedge ratio "decreased from 1.00 to 0.45 after the rating change" (Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management pp.212–213). The SMR does by contract what a downgrade does by repricing.

**Measure.** $\hat\pi_f$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$ number. The survival models of §15.5 produce $\mathbb{P}$ numbers. The gap between the two is the deal-break risk premium, and Mitchell & Pulvino (2001) tell you which way it points.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.267:* "Demand and supply would quickly act to create a price discrepancy, which would represent the premium that investors are willing to pay for the early redemption option. The size of the premium would implicitly reflect the probability that investors placed on the refinancing."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer's example is two senior issues of the same distressed company with different maturities, where a refinancing would take out one of them early: the price gap between them is the market's probability of the refinancing, expressed in points. Replace "refinancing" with "deal break" and you have the SMR gauge, down to the insistence that the comparison be made within one issuer's capital structure so that credit and ranking cancel. Where the 2026 desk departs: Moyer's investor eyeballs the gap and forms a view. The systematic desk inverts it with a fitted issuer curve, guards the denominator, marks $\hat\pi_f$ every day as a time series, and compares it with a physical-measure hazard model, so that the trade is the spread between two probabilities rather than an opinion about one.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** (1) *The control group is contaminated.* Assumption (B2) says the non-SMR tranches price the same in both states. They do not. On the day Kroger terminated it also announced a \$7.5bn buyback including a \$5bn accelerated share repurchase (Kroger release, Dec. 11, 2024, EDGAR), which is not nothing to the holders of the 2034s. If a break moves the acquirer's curve, $P^{\text{close}}$ is itself a mixture, and $\hat\pi_f$ is biased toward whatever the long bonds were pricing. (2) *Extrapolation.* Kroger's SMR tranches were the short ones and its new non-SMR tranches all matured in 2034 or later; curve-matching the 2027s means extrapolating to the short end unless you add the issuer's legacy short bonds as nodes. The code flags it. (3) *The outside date moves.* With a mutually extendable date, the gauge prices "fails by $T_f$," not "fails ever." (4) *Accrued.* Use the indenture's convention to the redemption date; a quarter's accrued on a 4.6% coupon is more than a point, which is most of the signal. (5) *The switched-off thermometer.* When $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f|$ is a few cents, report "no information," not 0.97.

#### The Full Derivation: probabilistic duration

Dollar durations add across the two legs, so with $D_f \approx T_f$ the fail leg's duration and $D^{\text{close}}$ the close leg's,

$$
P_0\,D^{\text{SMR}} \;=\; \pi_f\,G_f\,D_f + (1-\pi_f)\,P^{\text{close}}\,D^{\text{close}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
D^{\text{SMR}} \;=\; w\,D_f + (1-w)\,D^{\text{close}},\quad w = \frac{\pi_f\,G_f}{P_0},
$$

which collapses to the probability-weighted $\pi_f D_f + (1-\pi_f) D^{\text{close}}$ when both legs are near par. The weight is exact; the probability-weighted shortcut is an approximation that is good to a few hundredths of a year in the worked example, where $G_f/P_0 \approx 1.016$.

Doing this once for Kroger is a good afternoon with a legal pad and a Bloomberg screen. The gauge needs three things, and none of them is on the screen. It needs $P^{\text{close}}$, a price for the SMR bond that does not exist, because the only market for it is the one that includes the option, so it has to be built from the issuer's other bonds. It needs $G_f$, which is arithmetic but arithmetic with accrued interest, and accrued interest is where afternoons go to die. And it needs a denominator that the desk is afraid of. A systematic desk wants the mark every morning for every pending deal with an SMR, and the failure it fears is not a crash. It is a curve that works on Monday and goes quietly wrong on Thursday because someone added a bond to the fit by hand and the SMR tranche, the very thing being measured, helped price its own control group.

Listing 15.3 is a small pipeline of seven functions, easiest to read in the order the data flows. `z_spread` uses Brent root-finding on `bond_price` to find the constant spread over the Treasury zero curve that reprices a bond to its observed full price. `curve_match_close_price` takes one issuer's tranche table, leaves the SMR bonds out of the fit, converts the rest to Z-spreads, interpolates them across maturity with a shape-preserving PCHIP curve, and reprices each SMR tranche at its interpolated spread. That repriced figure is $P^{\text{close}}$, what the same cash flows would trade at without the option. Beyond the first and last node the spread is held flat and the row is flagged `extrapolated`. `smr_fail_leg` computes $G_f$: coupons paid on or before the resolution date plus 101 and accrued, discounted at the flat yield you pass in, which should be the Treasury zero plus the acquirer's short spread. `smr_price` and `smr_implied_break_prob` are the mixture and its inversion; the inversion returns the raw ratio, the clipped probability, and a flag for whether clipping happened. `smr_duration` weights the fail leg's duration by its share of value, and `spread_duration` supplies the close leg's by bumping the spread one basis point each way.

The checks follow the derivation. The worked $G_f$ of 102.15 discounted for a quarter. A round trip from probability to price and back, over eleven probabilities. The sign flip when rates have fallen and the holder is short the digital. And a synthetic issuer with a linear Z-spread curve, on which PCHIP must recover the line exactly. The last block makes two SMR tranches trade a few tenths rich to the curve and prints their implied break probabilities and a probabilistic duration.

Use it to put a daily mark on every pending deal with an SMR carve-out, and to set that $\mathbb{Q}$ number beside the survival models of §15.5. Do not use it when the non-SMR bonds are themselves moved by a break, which is assumption (B2) failing. The edge case that bites is the denominator guard. The function refuses only when $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f|$ is below `tol`, which defaults to 1e-6 points, a rounding guard and not an economic one. With the two legs three cents apart, a two-cent change in the observed price moves the answer from about one percent to about two-thirds, and nothing in the output objects. Set `tol` to the price noise you are prepared to ignore.

### 15.5.2 Derivation: Kaplan–Meier to competing risks

Put $t=0$ at signing (or at HSR filing; say which). Each deal ends in one of three causes, $k=c$ (close), $a$ (abandon), $b$ (litigated block), or is right-censored at the cutoff. Let $t_1 < t_2 < \dots$ be the distinct event times, $n_i$ the number of deals pending just before $t_i$, $d_{k,i}$ the number ending in cause $k$ at $t_i$, and $d_i = \sum_k d_{k,i}$.

**All-cause survival.** The Kaplan–Meier estimator walks through the event dates in order and, at each one, multiplies in the fraction of still-pending deals that did not end that day:

$$
\hat S(t) \;=\; \prod_{t_i \le t}\Big(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\Big),
$$

and the Nelson–Aalen cumulative hazard adds the same fractions instead of multiplying their complements, $\hat\Lambda(t) = \sum_{t_i\le t} d_i/n_i$. (We write $\Lambda$ for a cumulative deal hazard only inside this section; $\lambda$ stays reserved for default intensity.) Greenwood's formula puts a standard error around $\hat S$, and it grows as the number still at risk shrinks; it is written out in "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section.

**Cause-specific hazards with covariates.** For each cause, $h_k(t\mid x) = h_{0,k}(t)\,e^{x'\beta_k}$. Bohn and Stein state the restriction exactly: "the shape of the hazard function (and the corresponding survival function) is arbitrary, but the impact ratio of the hazards over time is constant" (Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.233). The coefficients are fit by Breslow's partial likelihood, which never estimates the baseline at all: at each date on which a deal ended in cause $k$, it asks how surprising it was that *this* deal ended rather than any of the others still pending, given their covariates, and chooses $\beta_k$ to make the realized sequence of endings least surprising. A Second Request coefficient of $-1.2$, for instance, says a deal under a Second Request closes at $e^{-1.2} \approx 0.30$ times the rate of an otherwise identical deal. Deals ending in other causes are censored for cause $k$; that is correct for cause-specific hazards and wrong for anything else. The partial likelihood and its gradient are in "The Full Derivation."

**Cumulative incidence.** The probability of ending in cause $k$ by $t$ is not $1 - e^{-\Lambda_k(t)}$, because a deal that is blocked cannot later close. The Aalen–Johansen estimator respects that: at each event date it takes the probability of still being pending just before it and multiplies by the fraction of pending deals that ended in cause $k$ on that date, then adds those pieces up. Because every deal that ends must end through exactly one door, the three incidences sum exactly to the probability of having ended at all, $\sum_k \hat F_k(t) = 1 - \hat S(t)$, which makes that identity the best unit test in the chapter. The estimator itself is in "The Full Derivation." Figure 15.4 shows the three incidences on the Listing 15.4 cohort, and how far the cause-specific shortcut overstates closing.

![Figure 15.4: By about 1.5 years the cause-specific shortcut (dashed) says 90% of deals have closed, while the competing-risks estimate says 72%, because 14% were abandoned and 9% blocked, and the three incidences sum exactly to total exits. Illustrative: Aalen–Johansen estimates from the seeded 600-deal synthetic cohort of Listing 15.4 (Weibull cause-specific hazards with planted coefficients, independent censoring), stopped where fewer than 20 deals remain at risk.](assets/figures/ch15_competing_risks_incidence.png)

**From clocks to PnL.** For a position whose price jumps by $\Pi_k(t)$ on outcome $k$ at $t$, under $\mathbb{P}$,

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\text{PnL}] \;=\; \int_0^{\infty} e^{-rt}\,S(t)\,\big[h_c(t)\Pi_c(t) + h_a(t)\Pi_a(t) + h_b(t)\Pi_b(t)\big]\,dt \;+\; \text{carry}.
$$

**Silence is data.** Each milestone that passes quietly (the 30-day wait expires with no Second Request; Phase I ends without Phase II; a complaint deadline passes) updates the deal probability by Bayes' rule, with $q_d$ and $q_b$ the probabilities of a quiet milestone under "deal" and "break," conditional on quiet so far:

$$
\pi_d^{+} \;=\; \frac{\pi_d\, q_d}{\pi_d\, q_d + (1-\pi_d)\, q_b},
$$

which rises whenever $q_d > q_b$. Giglio & Shue (2014) show that equity markets underreact to the information in the passage of time during pending mergers. If the credit market does the same, a target bond's spread should drift tighter through silent milestones; that is Chapter 10's trend signal with a legal calendar for a clock.

What it means for the desk: the baseline $h_{0,c}(t)$ in the first 30 days is not stationary across administrations, so never pool FY2016–2019 clocks with FY2022–2024 clocks without a regime covariate, and expect the first month of every filing in 2025–26 to carry procedural variance that had nothing to do with antitrust.

#### The Full Derivation: Greenwood, Breslow and Aalen–Johansen

With $t_i$, $n_i$, $d_i$ and $d_{k,i}$ as defined at the start of §15.5.2, the Kaplan–Meier estimator and Greenwood's variance are

$$
\hat S(t) \;=\; \prod_{t_i \le t}\Big(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\Big),
\qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}\big(\hat S(t)\big) \;=\; \hat S(t)^2 \sum_{t_i \le t} \frac{d_i}{n_i(n_i - d_i)} .
$$

For the cause-specific Cox model, with $R_i = \{j: t_j \ge t_i\}$ the risk set, the Breslow partial log-likelihood for cause $k$ is

$$
\ell_k(\beta) \;=\; \sum_{i:\,\text{cause}_i = k}\Big[x_i'\beta - \log\!\sum_{j\in R_i} e^{x_j'\beta}\Big],
\qquad
\nabla \ell_k \;=\; \sum_{i:\,\text{cause}_i = k}\big(x_i - \bar x_w(t_i)\big),
$$

with $\bar x_w(t_i)$ the $e^{x'\beta}$-weighted mean of the covariates in $R_i$. The gradient reads as the sum, over the deals that ended in cause $k$, of how far each one's covariates sat from the risk-weighted average of those still pending; the maximum is where those gaps cancel. Deals ending in other causes are censored for cause $k$.

The Aalen–Johansen estimator of cumulative incidence is

$$
\hat F_k(t) \;=\; \sum_{t_i \le t} \hat S(t_{i}^{-})\,\frac{d_{k,i}}{n_i},
\qquad
\sum_k \hat F_k(t) \;=\; 1 - \hat S(t),
$$

and the identity on the right is exact: summing over $k$ replaces $d_{k,i}$ by $d_i$, and $\hat S(t_i^-)\,d_i/n_i$ is precisely the drop in $\hat S$ at $t_i$, so the sum telescopes to $1 - \hat S(t)$.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**What ROBO built from the vague version.** "Estimate how long the deal will take." ROBO averaged signing-to-close times over closed deals and returned seven months. Every deal that had been blocked, abandoned or was still pending had been dropped as "missing data." Survivorship bias, delivered with confidence.

*The precise spec for Listing 15.4 (estimators, array shapes and return values) is in [The Engine: Full Specifications](#ch15-engine-specs) before §15.9's Spec You Hand the Robot.*

ROBO's seven months was an honest average of a dishonest sample. The deals that closed fast were in it; the deals that were blocked, abandoned or still pending had been quietly dropped, so the answer described the winners and nothing else. The fix is textbook statistics, and Theo's renamed 13-week columns are already most of the way there. The trouble is that each estimator is ten lines long and fails without a sound when the bookkeeping slips: a tied date, a pending deal counted as an event, a competing cause treated as if it never happened. A desk that will mark hundreds of deal clocks every morning cannot eyeball those. So it writes the estimators itself, bolts each to an identity or to an answer planted in advance, and only then points them at real deals.

Listing 15.4 builds the toolkit from scratch, then proves it on data whose answers are planted. `synthetic_deal_cohort` draws 600 deals with six covariates, generates a latent clock for each of the three causes from a Weibull proportional-hazards model with known coefficients, keeps the earliest as the outcome, and censors at an independent cutoff between half a year and four years; cause 0 means still pending. `_risk_table` does the bookkeeping everything else relies on: the distinct event times, the number at risk just before each, and the number of events, with ties kept in the risk set. `kaplan_meier` adds Greenwood bands (plain Wald intervals, clipped to the unit interval) and `nelson_aalen` the cumulative hazard. `aalen_johansen` computes all-cause survival and then, for each cause, the running sum of the previous step's survival times that cause's events over the number at risk. `cox_ph` maximizes the Breslow partial likelihood for one cause, treating the others as censored, with an analytic gradient and Hessian; it returns coefficients, standard errors, a baseline cumulative hazard, a convergence flag, and the correlation of Schoenfeld residuals with time as a proportional-hazards smell test. `bayes_deal_prob_path` applies the silence update one milestone at a time, taking the quiet probabilities as inputs.

The checks are identities wherever identities exist. With no censoring, Kaplan–Meier equals one minus the empirical distribution function. The three cumulative incidences sum to one minus survival to twelve decimals, and that survival matches the Kaplan–Meier curve. The close-hazard coefficients land within four standard errors of the planted ones. The Bayes path starts at 0.80 and rises through three quiet milestones.

Use it to build intuition and to audit any library you adopt later by running it on the same cohort. Do not read its output on real deals without asking why they are censored. This cohort censors independently by construction, while real outside dates are extended when a deal is in trouble, so every check passes in a world real data will not give you. The edge case that bites is a constant column. Fit a sample drawn from a single regulatory regime and `regime` never varies, the information matrix is singular, and `cox_ph` raises a `LinAlgError` instead of returning a standard error. Drop the column.

## 15.6 Target vs. Acquirer: Spread Compression, Acquirer Widening, and Event Momentum

The academic base rates are short and worth memorizing. Billett, King & Mauer (2004) find that target bondholders gain on average, with the gains concentrated in below-investment-grade targets bought by better-rated acquirers, while acquirer bondholders do not share in them. Furfine & Rosen (2011) find that mergers raise the acquirer's default risk on average. Acharya & Johnson (2010) find that the equity and CDS of LBO targets move before the announcement, and more so when more insiders are involved, which is Ines's hand history in academic form: somebody at this table knew more than you.

The trade the chapter's machinery suggests is event momentum. Run `bayes_deal_prob_path` on each pending deal; go long target bonds whose $\pi_d(t)$ is rising through silent milestones faster than their spread is tightening toward the acquirer's curve; size by Paleologo's rule that a shorter runway to the event means a smaller position, because "there is less time to build it" (Paleologo_Advanced_Portfolio_Management p.152). Then, before anyone says the word alpha, strip the acquirer's own spread change, the rates leg, carry and the market factor from the target's return. Most of what a target bond does after announcement is the acquirer's beta wearing the target's CUSIP. IDO or it didn't happen.

## 15.7 When the Deal Dies: The Merger as the Credit

For a weak target, the bond price before resolution is a mixture of two curves:

$$
P^{\text{tgt}}_t \;=\; \pi_d(t)\,P^{\text{acq}}_t \;+\; \big(1-\pi_d(t)\big)\,P^{\text{sa}}_t,
\qquad
\Delta P^{\text{tgt}}\big|_{\text{block}} \;=\; -\,\pi_d(t^-)\,\big(P^{\text{acq}}_t - P^{\text{sa}}_t\big),
$$

where $P^{\text{sa}}$ is the standalone value. A block is a jump of known sign and estimable size on a date the court publishes, which a diffusion VaR will understate every time (Chapter 5's mixtures; Chapter 14's distress dynamics).

Spirit Airlines is the template. On January 16, 2024 the District of Massachusetts enjoined the JetBlue merger; three days later Spirit reported \$1.3bn of liquidity at year-end and sale-leasebacks of 25 aircraft. On March 4, 2024 the parties terminated by mutual agreement; JetBlue paid Spirit \$69mm, and Spirit's stockholders had already received about \$425mm of prepayments while the agreement was in effect. On November 18, 2024 Spirit filed a prearranged Chapter 11 with a restructuring support agreement signed by a supermajority of its loyalty-program and convertible noteholders: a \$350mm backstopped equity investment, equitization of \$795mm of funded debt, and a \$300mm DIP from existing bondholders (Spirit 8-Ks and releases, EDGAR). Signing to block took about 18 months; block to bankruptcy took about ten.

Mags reads the prepayment line twice. "The ticking money went to the equity. The ticking clock went to the bonds."

## 15.8 Case Study: Warner Bros. Discovery 2025–26, the Covenant Strip and the Ticking Fee

**The strip came first.** On June 9, 2025, before any buyer had signed, WBD launched tender offers and consent solicitations for substantially all the notes of Discovery Communications, WarnerMedia Holdings, Warner Media LLC and Historic TW. Requisite consents arrived on June 16, and the supplemental indentures "eliminate substantially all of the restrictive covenants" and certain events of default once operative (WBD 8-K, June 13, 2025, EDGAR). Which protections survived in each series, the change-of-control offer included, must be checked supplemental indenture by supplemental indenture. The structural lesson does not wait for that check: the issuer exercised its counter-option *before* the put could be in the money, when consent was cheap.

**The loan said what it was.** A \$15bn bridge was documented as the "Non-Investment Grade Leveraged Bridge Loan Agreement" (June 26, 2025). Kaz: "They named the loan after its rating?" Theo: "It's the only honest title in the data room." It was refinanced on June 4, 2026 with a \$13bn term loan B at SOFR+250 and a €1,717mm term loan B at EURIBOR+250 at Discovery Global Holdings, maturing June 4, 2033, whose change of control, "such as the consummation of the previously disclosed proposed acquisition of the Company by Paramount Skydance Corporation," permits acceleration (WBD 8-K, June 2026). Entry 5 of the §15.1 table, with the trigger named in advance.

**Three suitors, one clock.** WBD signed with Netflix in early December 2025, amended on January 19, 2026 to \$27.75 a share in all cash, and then signed with Paramount Skydance on February 27, 2026 at \$31.00 in cash plus "Ticking Consideration" of \$0.00277778 per share per day after September 30, 2026, capped at \$0.25 per 90 days. Stockholders approved on April 23, 2026. The deal closed on October 6, 2026 at \$31.01666668 a share: exactly six days of ticking ($6 \times 0.00277778$), or \$41,886,975.78 in aggregate. At closing WBD repaid the June 2026 term loans and its October 2024 revolver, and WBD subsidiaries guaranteed Skydance's new notes and credit agreement (WBD 8-Ks, EDGAR). Figure 15.5 puts every date on one axis.

![Figure 15.5: The covenant strip on the legacy notes came eight months before the winning bidder signed and sixteen months before the deal closed. **Pricing the ticking fee.** Annualized, $0.00277778 \times 365 \approx \$1.014$, about 3.3% a year on \$31. That compensates shareholders for time, not for break risk, which they still carry. A survival model prices the rest: the expected ticking payment is $0.00277778 \times \mathbb{E}\big[(\tau_c - t^{*})^{+}\big]$ in days, with $t^{*}$ = September 30, 2026, and the competing-risks curve of §15.5 supplies the law of $\tau_c$.

**What the desk should have flagged.** For the legacy bondholders, the decisive date was June 16, 2025, eight months before the buyer was chosen. Our reading, to be checked against the guarantee documents: once WBD subsidiaries guarantee acquisition debt, the new money has claims at the same entities as the legacy notes, and stripped indentures no longer police it. The strip, not the merger, was the credit event.

## 15.9 Desk Debrief

- **Every deal clause is a payoff function.** Write it as (outcome, trigger, price at trigger), and store the issuer's counter-options (consent strip, defeasance, exchange offer) next to it in the security master.
- **CoC moneyness is coupon vintage.** The put cares about $101 - P$, not the credit story. The IG double trigger needs both inspectors, and for LBOs the trigger and the payoff covary ($M > 1$).
- **Portability is a short digital on a number the sponsor chooses.** Every point of permitted add-back lowers the odds that your put survives.
- **The SMR is a $\mathbb{Q}$ break gauge inside the acquirer's curve.** Its resolution is $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f|$, its control group is contaminated, and its duration jumps on ruling day.
- **Regulatory clocks are competing-risks survival data.** Censoring is informative, regimes shift $h_0$, and $\sum_k F_k = 1 - S$ is your unit test.
- **Silence is data, and beta is not alpha.** Update $\pi_d$ through quiet milestones, then strip the acquirer's beta before claiming the event. IDO or it didn't happen.
- **The most expensive three words have a cousin:** "defease certain obligations."

### The Engine: Full Specifications {#ch15-engine-specs}

The three listings of this chapter were explained where the story needed them. Their precise specifications, the part ROBO actually reads, are collected here so that they stop interrupting the derivations. Each is the contract the corresponding listing in Appendix F was written against.

**Listing 15.1, the change-of-control put ([Appendix F](#code-15-1)).** Implement `price_coc_put` with the payoff conditional on deal × trigger, the post-close bond priced on the post-close curve, the trigger allowed to depend on the post-close spread (to capture $M>1$), and an optional issuer counter-option (defeasance or strip) that may depend on how far in the money the put is.

- **Inputs.** `deal_prob` $\pi_d\in[0,1]$ (state the measure); `trigger_prob_given_deal`, a float or a callable mapping spreads (decimal, shape `(n,)`) to per-path probabilities; `post_spread_sampler(rng, n)` returning decimal spreads of shape `(n,)`; `coupon` in percent of par; `maturity_years` and `t_close` in years (ACT/365 clock; coupons 30/360 semiannual, valued on a coupon date so accrued is zero); `defeasance_prob`, a float or a callable of intrinsic value.
- **Outputs.** A dict with `value` (points per 100 face at signing), `stderr`, `moneyness_ratio` $M$, and `mean_intrinsic`.
- **Edge cases.** `deal_prob == 0`, a scalar trigger probability of 0, or a scalar defeasance probability of 1 returns exactly 0. A bond maturing before `t_close` returns 0 with a warning. Spreads are clipped at 0. $M$ is `nan` when intrinsic value is zero everywhere.
- **Variance reduction.** Integrate the deal and trigger Bernoullis analytically (Rao–Blackwellization): simulate only the spread.

**Listing 15.3, the SMR break gauge ([Appendix F](#code-15-3)).** Fit a Z-spread curve through the issuer's non-SMR tranches by maturity (PCHIP, flat beyond the last node, with an `extrapolated` flag), price each SMR tranche on that curve to get $P^{\text{close}}$, compute the fail leg $G_f$ with accrued interest and the acquirer's short yield, invert for $\hat\pi_f$ with a denominator guard and clipping report, and return the value-weighted probabilistic duration. Schema: a DataFrame with columns `tranche_id` (str), `coupon` (percent), `maturity` (years, on the half-year grid so that `bond_price` values on a coupon date), `price` (full price per 100), `is_smr` (bool). Raise if fewer than two non-SMR tranches or duplicate non-SMR maturities.

**Listing 15.4, the deal clock ([Appendix F](#code-15-4)).** From-scratch estimators (no `lifelines`): `kaplan_meier` with Greenwood bands, `nelson_aalen`, `aalen_johansen` for cumulative incidence, `cox_ph` by Breslow partial likelihood with analytic gradient and Hessian through `scipy.optimize`, a seeded synthetic cohort with known cause-specific Weibull hazards, and the Bayesian milestone path. Shapes: `times (n,)` in years ACT/365, `event (n,)` in $\{0,1\}$, `cause (n,)` in $\{0,1,2,3\}$ with 0 = pending, `X (n,p)`. Cox returns `beta (p,)`, `se (p,)`, baseline cumulative hazard `(m,2)` at $x=0$, and Schoenfeld-residual time correlations (reported, not asserted). Working-day calendars are converted to years by the caller, never inferred.

**The Spec You Hand the Robot.** "Build `event_credit_engine.py` (numpy, scipy, pandas; seeded). (1) `price_coc_put`: Monte Carlo over post-close spreads (decimal), deal × trigger × issuer-counter-option probabilities integrated analytically, bond priced on the post-close curve, return value in points per 100, stderr and $M$; exact zeros for degenerate inputs. (2) `curve_match_close_price`, `smr_fail_leg`, `smr_implied_break_prob`, `smr_duration`: PCHIP Z-spread curve over non-SMR tranches with an extrapolation flag; accrued to the redemption date; raise when $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f| < 10^{-6}$; clip and report. (3) `kaplan_meier`, `nelson_aalen`, `aalen_johansen`, `cox_ph` (Breslow, log-sum-exp, analytic gradient and Hessian), `bayes_deal_prob_path`. Assert: closed-form match for a constant spread; SMR round trip to $10^{-10}$; KM equals $1-$ECDF without censoring; CIFs sum to $1-\hat S$ within $10^{-12}$; Cox recovers planted $\beta$ within four standard errors. Units: years ACT/365 for clocks, 30/360 accrued, spreads decimal, prices per 100 face. Never pool regimes without a regime covariate. Never mix $\mathbb{P}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ without saying so."
