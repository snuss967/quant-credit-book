## Cold Open: Friday the Thirteenth, Aisle Four

*A replay. The Kroger, Albertsons and Tapestry filings, dates, amounts and court events are real and cited; the desk's 2024 client flow, its prices marked (illustrative), its positions and all dialogue are invented.*

Friday, December 13, 2024, 7:40 a.m. An EDGAR alert fires on an 8-K from The Kroger Co., Item 2.04: *Triggering Events That Accelerate or Increase a Direct Financial Obligation.* Kroger has delivered a notice of special mandatory redemption on four tranches of the notes it sold in August to pay for Albertsons, \$4.7 billion in all, to be redeemed on December 18 at 101% of principal plus accrued interest. Three days earlier, a federal court in Oregon had enjoined the merger.

Ines deals that page first on purpose. It is Friday, September 25, 2026, Theo's fourth day on the desk and his first replay: a real file, cut off at dated points and traded blind, with what the desk actually did at the time held face down until the end. The alert is the last page. She makes him start at the first.

**Tuesday, August 27, 2024.** Kroger prices \$10.5 billion of senior notes in seven tranches, from a 2026 out to a 2064, to pay for a grocer it agreed to buy on October 13, 2022 and that the Federal Trade Commission sued to keep it from buying on February 26. Theo has the Fiftieth Supplemental Indenture open in one window and the pricing supplement in the other. The four shortest tranches, the 2026s, 2027s, 2029s and 2031s, \$4.7 billion together, carry a special mandatory redemption; the 2034s, 2054s and 2064s, \$5.8 billion, do not. If the merger agreement is terminated, or the deal has not closed by an outside date, Kroger must redeem the four short tranches at 101% plus accrued, on a date "no earlier than the third business day and no later than 30 days following the date of such notice" (Kroger 8-K, Exhibit 4.3, Aug. 27, 2024, EDGAR).

In his old job this was a page of the description of notes that the associates proofread at two in the morning. On this desk it is a payoff.

"Why a hundred and one?" Kaz asks. "Why not a hundred?"

"Because nobody wants to lend a company money for three years and get it handed back in three months at exactly what they paid," Theo says. "You've done the credit work, filled the slot in the portfolio, and now you have cash and a hole. The point is an apology for the hole."

"Is 'special mandatory' stronger or weaker than 'mandatory'?"

"Weaker. I think. Mandatory happens on a schedule whether anyone likes it or not. Special mandatory happens only if something goes wrong."

"So it is a mandatory that is optional."

"Ask me again when the judge rules," Ines says, without looking up.

Mags is more interested in which tranches got the clause. "If the deal dies, Kroger gives back the short money and keeps the long money. Thirty- and forty-year paper is the hard part of any financing, and they wanted it whether or not they ever bought a single Safeway." That is the desk's reading, not Kroger's stated reason. For now it means that on one Tuesday the same issuer, with the same lawyers and the same ranking, sold seven bonds of which four had a deal-break option stapled to them and three did not.

**Thursday, November 14 to Tuesday, November 26, 2024.** The next cut opens on somebody else's broken deal. Tapestry and Capri, sued by the FTC in April, called off their merger on the thirteenth, and Tapestry's 8-K says it will redeem about \$6.1 billion of its own special-mandatory-redemption notes at 101 on or about November 25, from cash on hand, with a \$61.1 million premium to be recognized in income (Tapestry 8-K, Nov. 14, 2024, EDGAR). Theo prints it and tapes it to the side of his screen. "One point on six billion. That's the whole option, in one line of an 8-K."

The Kroger hearing in Portland closed in mid-September after three weeks (Grocery Dive, Sept. 17, 2024). Since then there has been nothing: no ruling, no leak, just a judge with a record and a calendar nobody else can see. "Somebody is getting a ruling for Christmas," Mags says.

Ines turns up a slice of the 2024 blotter: a run of client sells in the Kroger 2027s, offered a few cents under par, around 99.90 *(illustrative price)*. The desk is a market maker; when clients sell, it buys, and whether it keeps what it bought is the hand.

"The price already contains the judge," Kaz says, the way other people say the sun comes up.

"Then tell me what the judge is worth," Theo says.

Kaz writes three numbers on a sticky note. If the deal closes, the 2027s are an ordinary three-year Kroger bond, and the issuer's other bonds put that at about 99 *(illustrative)*. If the deal breaks, they are worth 101 plus a few months of accrued interest, discounted for a few weeks, about 101.5 *(illustrative)*. "Ninety-nine-ninety is nine-tenths of a point up a two-and-a-half-point ruler. Call it 36% that the judge blocks it." Section 15.4 turns that sticky note into an estimator, with all the ways it can lie. Kaz is pleased with it for about an hour.

Ines is not. "That's the pot and the odds. Now who at this table knows more than you? Those sellers are either telling us something about the judge, or telling us they need cash before year-end and don't care about a point."

The risk system is no help. It shows the 2027s with a spread duration of about 2.8, like any other three-year Kroger bond, because the security master has a field for coupon, a field for maturity and a field for call schedule, and no field for a judge. Theo asks the risk manager whether it could show two durations, one for each ruling. The risk manager asks which one should go in the morning report. Theo says both. The risk manager says the morning report has one column. It is the most important conversation of the afternoon and it takes ninety seconds by the coffee machine.

Ines sizes it out loud, which is how she teaches. "Block, and I make about a point and a half a bond in a few weeks. Close, and I lose about ninety cents. At Kaz's 36% those cancel. That's what an implied probability *means*." Theo sees that the bond is three bets, not one: the judge; the Kroger curve, since a closed deal leaves him owning a company that has just borrowed for Albertsons; and Treasuries, since the close leg is a three-year bond, the fail leg matures in a month, and a rally makes the close leg worth more than the redemption, quietly turning the holder from long the deal-break into short it. "The judge is the only one you get paid for knowing," Ines says. "The other two you survive. Size it for a bad month in Treasuries."

#### Decision point: the Kroger 2027s, Tuesday 26 November 2024

Write three things on the card: whether you buy the 2027s, how big, and which of the three bets you are making, the judge, the Kroger curve or Treasuries.

**Tuesday, December 10 to Thursday, December 12, 2024.** The court grants the preliminary injunction on Tuesday, and Albertsons terminates the same day. On Wednesday Kroger delivers its own termination notice and announces a \$7.5 billion share buyback, including a \$5 billion accelerated share repurchase (Kroger release, Dec. 11, 2024, EDGAR). On Thursday it sends the redemption notice, for Wednesday, December 18: the fourth business day, one longer than the shortest clock the indenture allows.

Theo notices something in the Wednesday cut that he will think about for a long time. He had been using the 2034s, the long bonds without the clause, as the control group against which the 2027s' option was measured, and a control group is clean only if nothing happens to it when the treatment arrives. A \$7.5 billion buyback, announced the day the option paid, is not neutral for holders of long unsecured paper. Whether the 2034s moved is a question for prints the replay does not deal; they had every reason to, and none of the reasons was a judge.

Then Ines turns the blotter over. In late November 2024 the desk had kept a little under half of what clients sold it, which she describes, depending on who is asking, as either disciplined or cowardly. Kaz's sticky note had said 36%. The judge had said 100%. Neither number had been available on November 26 at any price, which is the point of a probability.

"So it's a refund with a tip," Kaz says.

"Price is what you pay," Mags says. "A hundred and one is what you get, eventually, after the judge."

Ines circles the late-November sells on the hand history. "Somebody sold below par two weeks before a ruling that made these a 101 claim. Knew the court's calendar, or needed the cash more than the point. The markouts will say which."

Theo plugs in the USB stick with his thirteen-week cash flow model, for emotional support. It has a row labelled "Regulatory" that has been blank for six years. He types "Dec 10: PI granted" into it and feels, for the first time, that the row has a purpose. ROBO, asked to "price the redemption," returns 101.000. It is not wrong. It is just eleven days late. Twenty-six months from signing to ruling, four business days from notice to money.

"Everything in this file is written as if the holder owns an option," he says, mostly to himself. "The redemption, the 101, the put in the old Albertsons bonds. But Kroger chose which tranches got the option. Kroger and Albertsons could have agreed to move the date. Kroger decided what to do with the money the morning after. And the only person who decided *when* any of it happened was a judge in Portland who has never heard of us." He writes it on a sticky note and puts it next to the Tapestry 8-K: *Who actually owns this option, and who owns the clock?*

The rest of the chapter is the answer, in three movements. The 101 change-of-control put looks like the holder's option, but its moneyness is an accident of the year the bond was printed and its survival is the issuer's choice (§15.2–15.3). The special mandatory redemption prices the clock honestly, until it goes blind (§15.4). And the clock is a survival problem in which the deals still waiting are not a random sample of the deals that started (§15.5). Kroger, Electronic Arts and Warner Bros. Discovery tell one lesson three times: the credit event was the paragraph nobody read, not the merger.

**The question this chapter answers:** *how do you price a cash flow whose trigger is a federal judge, whose strike was written into an indenture months before anyone knew how the judge would rule, and whose clock is set by a regulator who counts in working days?*

## 15.1 The Event-Driven Credit Map: Six Cash Flows That Fire on a Deal

### 15.1.1 Desk framing: the merger is the underlying, the regulator is the clock

An equity merger-arbitrage desk sees one number: the gap between the target's share price and the offer. A credit desk sees a cluster of contractual cash flows, each written by a different lawyer in a different year, each switched on by a different event, and each owned by a different kind of holder. The RSSG reader already reads these documents better than anyone on the floor. The new skill is to stop reading them as prose and start reading them as payoff functions.

Here is the one-line version of the whole chapter. Let $\kappa$ be the terminal state of a pending deal (close, abandon, litigated block) and $\tau$ the date it resolves. Every M&A-sensitive instrument has a price of the form

$$
P_0 \;=\; \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\, \sum_{k \in \{c,a,b\}} \mathbf{1}_{\{\kappa = k\}}\; e^{-\int_0^{\tau} r_u\,du}\; \Pi_k(\tau) \,\right] \;+\; \text{carry to } \tau,
$$

where $\Pi_k(\tau)$ is the value of the instrument immediately after outcome $k$ arrives at time $\tau$. Two hard problems hide in that expectation. The first is $\Pi_k$: what the bond, loan or convert is worth in each terminal state, which is where option theory, covenants and the acquirer's curve live (§15.2–15.4). The second is the joint law of $(\kappa,\tau)$: which outcome, and when, which is where HSR waiting periods, Second Requests, European Phase II, CFIUS, the Surface Transportation Board and district-court calendars live (§15.5). Practitioners spend their time on the first and guess the second. The second is where the money is, because the market prices the payoffs well and the clock badly (§15.6).

> **Math, Gently.** *Three light switches and a discount factor.* The formula is simpler than its notation. The *indicator* $\mathbf{1}_{\{\kappa = k\}}$ is a light switch that reads 1 if the deal ends in outcome $k$ and 0 otherwise; the sum over $\{c,a,b\}$ is a panel of three switches of which exactly one ends up on, so it picks out the payoff of whatever happens. The *discount factor* $e^{-\int_0^{\tau} r_u\,du}$ says a dollar arriving at $\tau$ is worth less than a dollar today by the interest you could have earned in between; with a constant rate it is $e^{-r\tau}$. And $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}$ averages with the market's odds, the ones that reproduce today's prices, rather than with your own (Chapter 6 owns the distinction). Now by hand, ignoring coupons. Give close, abandon and block probabilities of 0.70, 0.20 and 0.10, and say the bond is worth 100, 101 and 85 the moment each arrives: $0.70 \times 100 + 0.20 \times 101 + 0.10 \times 85 = 98.7$. Resolution half a year away at 4.4% gives a discount factor of $e^{-0.044 \times 0.5} \approx 0.978$, and a price of about 96.6. Which measure belongs in which slot comes back in §15.2.2 Step 6.

Ines puts it in poker terms. "The payoff is the pot. The clock is how many streets are left. Most people at the table can count the pot. Almost nobody can tell you the odds that the river gets dealt before the outside date."

### 15.1.2 The six contingent cash flows

Three of the chapter's instruments fall into the table's rows before it is drawn. The Electronic Arts 1.850% notes due 2031, printed in February 2021, sit in row 1: an investment-grade change-of-control put at 101 that pays only if the deal closes *and* the agencies take the notes below investment grade inside a window. Had EA's new owners simply retired them, they would have moved to row 3, the make-whole call, the greater of par and the remaining payments discounted at Treasuries plus a small spread; for a 1.85% coupon in a 4% world that is exactly 100, a par call with extra paperwork. What the owners actually did belongs to neither row (§15.2). The Kroger 2027s live in row 2: the holder of a row-2 bond is long the break, the holder of a row-1 bond long the close, and the same desk can own both in one week and root for opposite outcomes in the same courtroom. And Warner Bros. Discovery's 2026 term loan B is row 5 written with unusual candor, since its change-of-control clause names the deal that would fire it (§15.8). Rows 4 and 6 wait for later chapters and for the forced-flow view below.

Kaz reads row 3 and gets there first. "Make-whole. Not insurance. I remember."

"It's what bondholders are paid to feel better about leaving early," Theo says. "Whether they get paid depends on what state the issuer is in."

He is only half joking. Row 3 looks like the tidiest entry in the table: the issuer's option, a formula, a Treasury rate. Outside bankruptcy it usually is, which is why a strategic acquirer refinancing target debt after closing simply pays it. The trouble starts when a deal fails and the target ends up in Chapter 11, as Spirit Airlines did (§15.7), or when a solvent company uses bankruptcy to repay expensive bonds early. Then whether a make-whole is owed at all turns on three things the formula does not show: the wording (does the premium attach only to an "optional redemption," or also to repayment after acceleration?), the circuit, and whether the debtor is solvent. The four decisions that a desk most often hears cited make the point:

| Case | Court | Setting | Outcome on the make-whole |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| *Energy Future Holdings* (*Delaware Trust Co. v. EFIH*) | 3d Cir., 2016 | Repayment in Chapter 11 of notes whose indenture required a premium on optional redemption | Owed: the in-bankruptcy refinancing was an optional redemption, so the express premium applied |
| *Momentive* (*In re MPM Silicones*) | 2d Cir., 2017 | Notes accelerated automatically by the filing | Not owed: repayment after acceleration was not an optional redemption under that indenture |
| *Ultra Petroleum* | 5th Cir., 2022 | Solvent debtor | The make-whole is the economic equivalent of unmatured interest, disallowed in the ordinary case; owed here under the solvent-debtor exception |
| *Hertz* | 3d Cir., 2024 | Solvent debtor, unimpaired noteholders | Same characterization; a solvent debtor must pay post-petition interest at the contract rate, make-whole included (Chapter 16, §16.6) |

Read the table as a scoreboard, not as settled law: one practitioner tracker in June 2026 told its readers to treat the alignment as "current, not settled" (Nelson Mullins, *Red Zone* blog, "Make-Whole and Solvent-Debtor Circuit Scoreboard," June 17, 2026). What it means for the security master is that row 3 needs two fields, not one: the make-whole formula, and whether the indenture expressly attaches the premium to acceleration. The second field is worth nothing in a successful merger and a great deal in a failed one.

Two further entries are not cash flows at all but *counter-options held by the issuer or acquirer*: the consent solicitation that strips covenants before the trigger, and legal or covenant defeasance, which replaces the issuer's promises with a box of Treasuries. Both kill entry 1. Both appear in the case studies (§15.2, §15.8). An RSSG banker has drafted all eight. A systematic desk has to store all eight as fields in its security master, because a CoC put without its defeasance article is a payoff without its knock-out barrier. ### 15.1.3 The forced-flow view

Chapter 1 drew the credit ecosystem as a graph of who is a forced seller to whom. M&A adds edges to that graph on dates the deal calendar announces in advance, which is the rare case where a forced flow is both large and predictable. The easiest way to see the four edges is to let them all land in the same week. Take Wednesday, December 18, 2024, the day Kroger's redemption settled, and surround it with three composites of the kind any December calendar carries *(the last three are illustrative, not events of that week)*.

At nine in the morning the SMR edge pays. Kroger's \$4.7 billion arrives as cash in the accounts of the investment-grade funds that held the 2026s, 2027s, 2029s and 2031s, three weeks after Tapestry had repaid about \$6.1 billion the same way in November 2024. None of those holders planned to have the cash. Most of them are benchmarked to an index that just lost four bonds, so the cash has to go somewhere in the short end of the IG market by the end of the month, in a size that moves the short end of an issuer curve and of the index itself.

By lunchtime the desk is talking about the fallen-angel edge, because a sponsor buyout of an investment-grade company is due to close before month-end and the agencies have said where the ratings will land. When an IG target bought by a sponsor is downgraded to high yield at or near close, every IG-index-constrained holder becomes a seller within days, and HY funds become buyers only after the bond enters their index at a month-end rebalance. Dynkin's evidence that fallen angels outperform after the downgrade "due to price pressures around the downgrade event" (Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.411) is the price of that gap between the two calendars. If the bond also carries a CoC put struck at 101 and trades in the 80s, the edge is short-circuited: holders do not sell to HY funds, they put the bond back to the issuer. Unless the issuer finds a cheaper door (§15.8).

In the afternoon a cash merger closes on a company with a convertible outstanding, and the convert edge fires. A cash merger collapses a convertible's equity delta to zero on the close date and turns the bond into a par-plus-make-whole claim. The arbitrageur who was long the convert and short the stock must buy the stock back, and the stock-borrow fees that subsidized the trade disappear on the same day.

And somewhere in the loan market a borrower with a change-of-control event of default is bought, and the CLO edge fires. A loan repaid at par on a change of control hands CLO managers cash to reinvest and removes a loan from the collateral pool, which matters most for loans trading below par or bucketed in CCC (Chapter 21).

> **View From Another Seat.** *The CLO manager.* To a CLO manager the merger is barely the story. A loan bought at 99 and repaid at par on a change of control is a point of par build, which is welcome, and a reinvestment problem, which is not: in a tight market the replacement loan pays less spread or carries a worse rating, and the cash sits earning nothing while the manager looks. What the manager actually watches is the test sheet. A typical CLO limits loans rated CCC/Caa1 or below to 7.5% of the pool, marks the excess at the lowest market value in the CCC bucket, and runs within a few percentage points of its tightest overcollateralization test (Kundu, ESRB Working Paper 141, March 2023). So a merger that takes a CCC loan out at par is a gift that loosens the bucket, and a merger that leaves a B3 borrower with two more turns of debt is a downgrade waiting to land in it. The same deal that an equity arbitrageur prices as a spread, and a credit desk prices as a put, the CLO manager prices as a change in two ratios on a monthly trustee report.

Each edge is a known flow on an unknown date. The rest of the chapter is about estimating the date.

> **Trend Watch.** *The 2023–2026 mega-deal and IG take-private wave.* The aggregate value of transactions reported under the Hart–Scott–Rodino (HSR) Act rose from about \$1.6 trillion in FY2023 *(approx.; FY2023 report, not rechecked)* to \$2.1 trillion in FY2024 and \$2.5 trillion in FY2025, while the number of reported transactions stayed roughly flat (1,805, 2,031 and 2,006); transactions above \$1 billion were "approximately one-fourth" of reported transactions in FY2024 and 31.8% in FY2025 (FTC/DOJ *Hart-Scott-Rodino Annual Reports*, FY2023–FY2025, Appendix A). Fewer, bigger deals means more of the market's event risk sits in IG capital structures. The marquee examples are all 2025–26: Electronic Arts agreed on September 29, 2025 to be taken private by PIF, Silver Lake and Affinity Partners at \$210 per share, about \$55bn of enterprise value, described in the announcement as "the largest all-cash sponsor take-private investment in history" (EA press release, Sept. 29, 2025, EDGAR); Union Pacific agreed on July 29, 2025 to acquire Norfolk Southern at about \$85bn of enterprise value, filing an application of roughly 7,000 pages with the Surface Transportation Board on December 19, 2025, which the Board rejected as incomplete on January 16, 2026; the railroads refiled on April 30, 2026, and on May 28, 2026 the Board found the revised application complete but asked for supplemental information (Surface Transportation Board decisions; Union Pacific releases, EDGAR); and Warner Bros. Discovery signed with Netflix in early December 2025 before signing with Paramount Skydance on February 27, 2026 at \$31.00 per share in cash plus a per-diem ticking fee, closing October 6, 2026 (WBD 8-Ks, EDGAR). What it means for the desk: a sponsor buying an IG company with 2020–21 vintage low-coupon bonds outstanding creates a deep-in-the-money CoC put on an IG security, which the IG market had not had to price at scale for a generation, and the issuer's response (tender, consent strip, defeasance) is now the main determinant of what that put pays.

A generation is long enough for a market to forget how its smoke detectors work, and the IG market had last tested this one in earnest when RJR Nabisco's bondholders learned what an LBO does to an investment-grade coupon. The next section takes the 101 put apart, starting with the question those bondholders learned to ask too late: who has to agree that there is a fire, and who gets to unplug the alarm?

## 15.2 Anatomy and Pricing of the 101 Change-of-Control Put

### 15.2.1 Desk framing: a smoke detector with two licensed inspectors

A high-yield change-of-control put is a smoke detector. When someone acquires control of the issuer (typically more than 50% of the voting stock, or a sale of substantially all of the assets), the issuer must offer to buy back every note at 101% of principal plus accrued and unpaid interest. The offer goes out within a fixed number of days after the event, often 30, and stays open for a window usually measured as 30 to 60 days; then the repurchase settles. Each holder chooses: tender at 101, or keep the bond and live with the new owner.

The investment-grade version is a smoke detector that goes off only if there is a fire *and* two separately licensed inspectors agree, within sixty days, that it is a fire. The defined term is usually "Change of Control Repurchase Event" or "Change of Control Triggering Event," and the EDGAR counts in §15.3 show that one or the other appears in hundreds of registered IG prospectuses a year. The trigger needs a change of control *and* a "Rating Event": the notes are rated below investment grade by each of the named agencies (sometimes all three, sometimes two of three) within a window that typically opens at the earlier of the change of control and its public announcement, runs for about 60 days after the change of control, and stays open while any agency has the notes publicly under review for downgrade. That drafting is typical, not universal. Read the definition in the document you actually own, because the gap between "each of" and "any two of" is a probability you are about to estimate.

Why 101? Because the drafters of the late 1980s wanted the holder to be worth at least par after a leveraged buyout, and a point on top made the tender offer clean. The premium is the tip. The put is the *par*. Remember this the next time someone tells you the 101 is "only a point": on a bond trading at 77, it is twenty-four points.

Mags was not there; she started in 1996. But she learned it from people who were, and tells it as if she had been. "In 1988 RJR Nabisco taught investment-grade bondholders they were lunch. Then the market spent two years inventing the poison put." (The measured damage is in Asquith & Wizman (1990) and Warga & Welch (1993); the covenant response is in Crabbe (1991), Lehn & Poulsen (1991) and Cook & Easterwood (1994).)

She tells the rest whenever someone on the desk says "investment grade" as if it were a promise. In the autumn of 1988 a management group and then KKR bid for RJR Nabisco with money borrowed against RJR itself, and its existing bonds, paper insurers had bought to sleep at night, were suddenly the junior claims on a company that would carry several times the debt. "Nobody had lied to them," Mags says. "That was the worst part. The indenture didn't say the company couldn't borrow twenty-odd billion dollars. It just never occurred to anyone that it would." Theo's question is the one he brought from PJT. "But what does the indenture say? Not the summary. The definitions." Every term in the payoff below is a defined term, and each defined term is a parameter.

### 15.2.2 Derivation: the put as a compound option

**Notation for this chapter.** The style bible reserves $\tau$ for default time. The deal-resolution date from §15.1 is the first event this chapter cares about, so we write $\tau_c$ for the closing date when the deal closes, and keep $\tau$ for default where it appears. Leverage collides with loss given default $L = 1-R$, so pro-forma leverage is $\ell_{pf}$, defined as debt over EBITDA (or over $\text{EV}$ where stated). Deal-clock hazards are $h_k(t)$, never $\lambda$, which stays reserved for default intensity.

**Setup.** A bond pays an annual coupon $c$ (percent of par, paid semiannually) and matures at $T$. A deal is signed at $t_0$; if it closes, it closes at a random time $\tau_c$. Let $\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{deal}\}}$ indicate that it closes and $\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}}$ that the contractual trigger fires (the change of control alone in HY; the change of control *and* the Rating Event in IG). The strike is $K = 101$ plus accrued interest. Under $\mathbb{Q}$ with short rate $r_u$, the put is worth

$$
V_{\text{CoC}}(t_0) \;=\; \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\, e^{-\int_{t_0}^{\tau_c} r_u\,du}\; \mathbf{1}_{\{\text{deal}\}}\,\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}}\,\big(K - P^{\text{post}}_{\tau_c}\big)^{+} \,\right],
$$

where $P^{\text{post}}_{\tau_c}$ is the price the bond would trade at immediately after closing if nobody tendered. It is a compound option: an option (the deal closes and triggers) on an option-like payoff (the put is worth something only if the post-close bond trades below the strike).

> **Math, Gently.** *A raffle ticket that enters you in a second raffle, and an umbrella that gets wetter than you think.* Buy a ticket in the first draw and you win nothing directly; you win a ticket in the second draw, where the prize is. The change-of-control put works that way. The first draw is the deal: it has to close, and in investment grade the agencies have to agree it is a fire. Winning hands you the second ticket, an ordinary put struck at 101 on whatever the bond is worth after closing, and that ticket can still be a blank: if the post-close bond trades at 104, it pays nothing. A 60% chance of the first draw and a second ticket worth 10 points on average gives $0.6 \times 10 = 6$ points before discounting *(illustrative)*, *if* the two draws are unrelated.
>
> In an LBO they are not, and that is the second half of the picture. The bar in $\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid \text{deal}]$ reads "given": average only over the states in which the deal closes. The trap is multiplying two averages that are secretly related. "The probability I carry an umbrella" times "the average rainfall" understates how wet the umbrella gets, because you carry it when it rains; the two have a positive *covariance*. The IG ratings trigger is the umbrella. It fires when post-close spreads are wide, which is when the bond is cheap and the put deep in the money. Say the trigger fires in half the states of the world, where the put is worth 24 points, and the put is worth 0 in the other half. The honest average of "trigger times intrinsic" is $0.5 \times 24 = 12$. The shortcut, "probability of trigger" times "average intrinsic," is $0.5 \times (0.5 \times 24 + 0.5 \times 0) = 6$, off by a factor of two. That factor is the $M$ of Step 2.

**Step 1: factor what can be factored.** Assume (A1) the closing time is independent of rates, so the discount factor can be pulled out at the expected close $\bar\tau_c$ as $B(t_0,\bar\tau_c)$, and condition on the deal:

$$
V_{\text{CoC}} \;\approx\; \pi_d\, B(t_0,\bar\tau_c)\; \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}}\,(K - P^{\text{post}})^{+} \,\middle|\, \text{deal}\right],
\qquad \pi_d = \mathbb{Q}(\text{deal}).
$$

**Step 2: do not factor the trigger.** It is tempting to write the inner expectation as $\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\cdot\mathbb{E}[(K-P^{\text{post}})^+]$. That is assumption (A2), independence of trigger and payoff, and it is false in exactly the deals you care about: the umbrella is carried when it rains. Package the correction as one multiplier, the **conditional moneyness ratio** $M$, the put's average intrinsic value in the states where the trigger fires divided by its average across all states ($M = 2$ in the box). Then $V_{\text{CoC}} \approx \pi_d\,\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\,B\,M\,\mathbb{E}[(K-P^{\text{post}})^+]$. For a sponsor LBO, $M>1$ and the independent approximation understates the put. For a strategic IG acquirer, $\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\approx 0$, because the Rating Event cannot fire when the parent is a stronger credit, and the put is worthless however far below 101 the bond trades: Microsoft buying Activision is the textbook case, a AAA parent whose second inspector never shows up. The covariance identity is written out in "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section.

**Step 3: price the bond at the trigger.** With $t_i$ the remaining coupon times and $y^{\text{TSY}}_t$ the Treasury zero curve,

$$
P^{\text{post}} \;=\; \sum_{i} \tfrac{c}{2}\,e^{-(y^{\text{TSY}}_{t_i} + s^{\text{post}})\,t_i} \;+\; 100\,e^{-(y^{\text{TSY}}_{T} + s^{\text{post}})\,T},
\qquad s^{\text{post}} \;=\; s_{\text{acq}} + \Delta_{\text{struct}} + \Delta_{\text{lev}}(\ell_{pf}),
$$

where $s_{\text{acq}}$ is the acquirer's (or new capital structure's) spread at the relevant tenor, $\Delta_{\text{struct}}$ the penalty for legacy notes left structurally subordinated at an operating company or holding company, and $\Delta_{\text{lev}}$ a Merton-style map from pro-forma leverage to spread (Chapter 6). Discount on the post-close curve, never on the target's pre-deal curve: after the trigger the target's pre-deal curve is a historical document.

**Step 4: coupon vintage dominates.** Differentiate $P^{\text{post}}$ in the coupon:

$$
\frac{\partial P^{\text{post}}}{\partial c} \;=\; \tfrac12\sum_i e^{-(y^{\text{TSY}}_{t_i}+s^{\text{post}})t_i} \;\equiv\; A(s^{\text{post}}),
$$

the annuity factor per point of annual coupon. The derivative is only a slope: price is coupon times $A$ plus a principal that does not depend on the coupon, so every extra point of coupon adds about $A$ points of price and removes the same from the put (present value and annuities are Chapter 4-B's). Price is linear in coupon with slope $A$, so the intrinsic value $(K - P^{\text{post}})^+$ is a hockey stick in $c$ with a kink at the **break-even coupon**

$$
c^{*}(s^{\text{post}}) \;=\; \frac{K - 100\,e^{-(y^{\text{TSY}}_T+s^{\text{post}})T}}{A(s^{\text{post}})}.
$$

Any bond with $c < c^*$ has an in-the-money put if it triggers. Plug in a remaining life of 4.5 years and a flat Treasury zero rate of 4.4% *(illustrative throughout)*. Into a strategic acquirer at 60bp, $y = 5.0\%$, $A \approx 3.98$, $100e^{-yT}\approx 79.9$ and $c^*\approx 5.3\%$. Into a sponsor LBO at 350bp, $y=7.9\%$, $A\approx 3.71$ and $100e^{-yT}\approx 70.1$. On the 1.85% bond the nine half-yearly coupons of 0.925 add about 6.9 to that principal, a price near 77.0 and 24 points of put; 24 points divided by 3.71 points per point of coupon is 6.5 more points of coupon before the put dies, so $c^*\approx 1.85\% + 6.5\% \approx 8.3\%$. The full grid:

| Bond (4.5 years remaining, *illustrative*) | Coupon | $P^{\text{post}}$, strategic buyer (60bp) | $P^{\text{post}}$, sponsor LBO (350bp) | Intrinsic if triggered, LBO |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| 2021 vintage | 1.85% | ≈87.2 | ≈77.0 | ≈24.0 |
| 2023 vintage | 5.00% | ≈99.7 | ≈88.7 | ≈12.3 |
| 2009-style high coupon | 6.875% | ≈107.2 | ≈95.6 | ≈5.4 |

Same issuer, same acquirer, same trigger: a four-to-one spread in what the put is worth, driven entirely by the year the bond was printed. The put does not care about the credit story. It cares about $101 - P$. This is aha number one of the chapter: **moneyness is a coupon-vintage accident**, and the 2020–21 vintage of sub-3% IG coupons turned every one of those bonds into a deep-in-the-money LBO put the day rates went to 4%. Kaz looks at the table. "So the most valuable covenant in the IG market was written by a bond that nobody wanted to buy in 2021."

"Nobody wanted to buy it at 1.85% either," Mags says. "They bought it anyway. Now they get paid for their regret."

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Schmid_Credit_Risk_Pricing_Models p.153:* "Credit options offer protection against changing default free interest rates and changing credit spreads. Hence, they allow hedging of mark-to-market exposure to fluctuations in spreads and interest rates: hedging long positions with puts, and short positions with calls."
>
> **Annotation.** Schmid's credit put is a traded instrument whose strike is a spread. The change-of-control put is a credit put embedded in the bond, with a strike fixed in price (101), a trigger written by a lawyer, and an exercise window set by an indenture. The table above shows why the embedded version is also a *rates* option: most of the intrinsic value on the 1.85% notes comes from Treasury yields, not from the LBO spread. A desk that hedges an LBO-candidate portfolio with CDS index protection is hedging the spread leg and staying unhedged on the leg that drives moneyness. Where the modern desk departs: the embedded put is not tradeable on its own, so its value shows up only as a cross-sectional spread anomaly across coupon vintages of the same issuer (exercise 3).

**Step 5: the trigger probability.** The ratings leg is a classification problem. A serviceable model is a logistic

$$
\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d} \;=\; \sigma\!\left(\alpha + \beta_{\ell}\,\ell_{pf} + \beta_{s}\,\mathbf{1}_{\text{sponsor}} + \beta_{r}\,\text{notch}_{\text{acq}}\right),\qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
$$

fit on past deals, with $\text{notch}_{\text{acq}}$ the acquirer's rating in notches below AAA. The logistic is a dimmer switch: it turns any sum of evidence $z$ into a number between 0 and 1, reads 0.5 at $z=0$, about 0.88 at $z=2$ and 0.12 at $z=-2$, and moves fastest in the middle, where its slope $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ peaks at a quarter (§7-B.3 fits and calibrates it on small samples). A sponsor LBO that adds five or more turns of debt pins it at "on," and an IG strategic pins it at "off"; neither needs a model. The interesting cases are in the middle: an IG acquirer that levers up for a large deal and promises to deleverage, where the agencies' "under review" language holds the window open and the answer depends on whether the deleveraging plan is believed.

**Step 6: say which measure.** A $\pi_d$ backed out of the equity merger-arbitrage spread is a risk-adjusted, $\mathbb{Q}$-like number; a hazard estimated from historical deal outcomes (§15.5) is $\mathbb{P}$. Merger arbitrage is the insurance business for deals. If the equity spread on a pending deal implies a 25% chance of failure while comparable deals have historically failed 15% of the time *(illustrative)*, the ten points between them are the premium arbitrageurs charge for a risk that arrives in bad markets: Mitchell & Pulvino (2001) show that risk-arbitrage returns behave like a short position in index puts, so market-implied failure probabilities exceed physical ones, especially when markets fall. The 25% belongs in a pricing formula such as $V_{\text{CoC}}$, the 15% in an expected-PnL calculation such as the integral in §15.5.2, and the trade, if there is one, is the gap. Chapter 6 and §6-B.6 own the change of measure.

**Step 7: bound the Bermudan error.** The holder actually has a window of 30–60 days to tender, so the put is Bermudan-ish, not European. Treat it as European at $\tau_c$ and bound the error by the value of waiting. Spreads wander like a random walk over short horizons, so their risk grows with the square root of time (§6.1.1 draws the picture): 150bp of annual spread volatility over a 45-day window is $150 \times \sqrt{45/365} \approx 53$bp, and at a spread duration near 3.7 the four-and-a-half-year bond can move about 2 points while the window is open. That is small against twenty-four points of intrinsic and large against half a point; the bound tells you when to care.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** (1) *The covenant is not the cash flow.* A consent solicitation can strip the change-of-control covenant before the trigger (Warner Bros. Discovery, June 2025, §15.8), and covenant defeasance can release the issuer from it after the trigger (Electronic Arts, August 2026, below). Model the issuer's counter-option or your put is a payoff without its knock-out barrier. (2) *Accrued interest and record dates.* The repurchase price is 101 plus interest accrued to, but excluding, the repurchase date; if that date falls after a record date, the coupon goes to the record holder and the repurchase price drops accordingly. Off-by-one-coupon errors are worth 1–3 points on a high-coupon bond. (3) *Structural subordination.* Tapestry's 2023 prospectus states that the new notes would be "structurally subordinated to certain indebtedness assumed in connection with the Capri Acquisition"; legacy target notes left at an operating subsidiary can be senior *or* junior to the new money, and $\Delta_{\text{struct}}$ can have either sign. (4) *The wrong curve.* Discounting at the target's pre-deal spread overstates $P^{\text{post}}$ for LBOs and understates it for strategic buyers. (5) *The definition you did not read.* "Each of" versus "any two of" the agencies; a window that ends 60 days after the change of control versus 60 days after its announcement. Each is a different $\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}$.

Kaz reads item (1) twice. "So the issuer is also playing."

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Veronesi_Fixed_Income_Securities p.470:* "The key to the dynamic replication is that the issuer behaves as the model prescribes it should. However, exercising an American option optimally is not mandatory, and it is perfectly reasonable that the issuer may exercise sub-optimally."
>
> **Annotation.** Veronesi is writing about callable bonds, where the issuer's option is to call early and the model assumes it does so optimally. The change-of-control put inverts the roles but keeps the lesson: the *holder* owns the put, and the *issuer* owns a set of counter-options (tender below 101, strip the covenant by consent, defease, refinance through an exchange offer) that the textbook payoff omits. A 2026 desk prices the put net of those counter-options, and the issuer's choice among them is not a random variable with a nice distribution; it is a cost-minimization problem the sponsor solves with expensive lawyers. Where the modern desk departs from Veronesi: we do not assume the counterparty is sub-optimal by accident. We assume it is optimal for a payoff we forgot to write down, and we go find the clause.

#### The Full Derivation: the trigger covariance and $M$

Step 2 replaced the independence shortcut with the identity

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}}(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right]
\;=\; \pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right] \;+\; \operatorname{Cov}\!\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}},\,(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right).
$$

The ratings leg fires when the post-close spread $s^{\text{post}}$ is high, and a high spread is precisely when $P^{\text{post}}$ is low and the put is deep in the money. The covariance is positive. Define the **conditional moneyness ratio**

$$
M \;=\; \frac{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\mid \text{trig}\right]}{\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right]} \;=\; 1 + \frac{\operatorname{Cov}\!\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}},(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right)}{\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[(K-P^{\text{post}})^{+}\right]},
$$

so that $V_{\text{CoC}} \approx \pi_d\,\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\,B\,M\,\mathbb{E}[(K-P^{\text{post}})^+]$, the expression used in Step 2. The second equality for $M$ follows by dividing the identity by $\pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\,\mathbb{E}[(K-P^{\text{post}})^+]$ and using $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{trig}\}}X] = \pi_{\text{trig}\mid d}\,\mathbb{E}[X\mid\text{trig}]$ for $X = (K-P^{\text{post}})^+$. When trigger and payoff are independent the covariance vanishes and $M = 1$; Listing 15.1's third check measures $M$ by repricing with the trigger probability replaced by its path average.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**What ROBO built from the vague version.** Theo typed "price the CoC put on the U.S. Steel 6.875% 2029s." ROBO priced a vanilla European put struck at 101 on the bond using the target's pre-deal spread volatility, ignored the acquirer, ignored the trigger, and returned a healthy number for a bond that was about to migrate onto a stronger parent's curve and trade above 101. It did exactly what it was told.

*The precise spec for Listing 15.1 (inputs, outputs, edge cases and the variance-reduction rule) is collected with the chapter's other two in [The Engine: Full Specifications](#ch15-engine-specs), just before The Spec You Hand the Robot in §15.9.*

Theo's request had been one bond. By the afternoon Kaz wants every coupon vintage of one LBO candidate, repriced whenever Treasuries move fifteen basis points or someone asks what happens if the sponsor defeases, and the tangle from Step 2, a trigger that fires exactly when the put is deepest, is not something a spreadsheet column survives. So the desk writes the pricer once, tests it against cases whose answers are known in advance, and lets ROBO call it.

Listing 15.1 is three small functions and a page of checks. `tsy_zero_curve` is a fixed Nelson–Siegel shape for continuously compounded Treasury zero rates, labeled illustrative because it is; any function from tenor to rate can replace it. `bond_price` counts coupons backward from maturity, builds a matrix of discount factors at the Treasury zero plus a spread for every tenor, and multiplies it by the cash flows, so a vector of spreads comes back as a vector of prices in one pass. `price_coc_put` does the derivation's work. It draws post-close spreads from the sampler you supply, clips them at zero, reprices the bond at its life remaining after `t_close`, and takes the shortfall against 101 as intrinsic value. The deal and trigger events are never simulated. Each path carries `deal_prob`, the discount factor, the trigger probability, one minus the defeasance probability, and intrinsic value, multiplied together, and the average is the answer. Because the trigger probability can be a function of that path's spread, the covariance from Step 2 survives, and the reported `moneyness_ratio` is the $M$ of the derivation.

The four checks do real work. The first collapses the sampler to one spread and matches the closed form to ten decimals. The second shows that a 6.875% bond priced into a tight acquirer curve is exactly worthless. The third builds an LBO from a mean-preserving lognormal spread and a logistic ratings leg (a lognormal keeps spreads positive and moves them in percentages, and "mean-preserving" means shifting the log-mean down by $\tfrac12\sigma^2$, so that with $\sigma = 0.5$ the median draw is $e^{-0.125} \approx 88\%$ of a 350bp mean; Chapter 5 owns the lognormal, and Chapter 6 the same half-sigma-squared in Itô's lemma), then reprices with the trigger probability replaced by its path average. The seed is fixed, so both runs see the same draws and the gap is the covariance, not noise. The fourth lets the sponsor defease whenever intrinsic value exceeds five points.

The bug the desk actually hit was the coupon grid. Kaz's first rerun set `t_close` to 0.8 years, off the half-year grid; `bond_price` returned a dirty price without complaint, the code compared it with a flat 101, and the put came out short by the accrued interest, up to half a coupon. The pricer also treats the tender window as a single date, discounts at a known rate, prices the post-close bond off today's zero curve, and knows nothing about a consent strip unless the trigger probability is set to zero by hand.

*The full listing is [Listing 15.1 in Appendix F](#code-15-1).*

Under these illustrative inputs the 1.85% bond's put comes out at eighteen to nineteen points at signing, most of the gap between where an LBO candidate's low- and high-coupon bonds trade. Check (4) sends it to roughly zero: a sponsor that defeases whenever defeasing is cheaper than paying 101 leaves the holder only the scheduled cash flows. That is not a hypothetical.

### Case Study: Electronic Arts 2025–26, the In-the-Money Put That Got Defeased

On his second Friday Ines deals it to him in three cuts, with the desk's own cards from the time face down.

## 15.3 Portability: The Short Option You Did Not Know You Sold

### 15.3.1 Desk framing

Kaz hears the word in a morning meeting and brightens. "Portability. Like a phone number? Bondholders keep their coupon when they switch carriers?"

"Close," Ines says. "The issuer keeps the number. You get dropped."

A portability clause, usually drafted as a "Specified Change of Control Event" or a "Permitted Change of Control" carve-out, says that a change of control which would otherwise trigger the 101 put does *not* trigger it if conditions hold. Typical conditions, in the order a covenant analyst checks them: the change of control happens within a window after issue (two or three years is common); pro-forma leverage on the closing date is at or below a test, often the leverage at issuance or a fixed multiple; the carve-out can be used once; and sometimes the acquirer must meet a size or experience test. The details vary by deal and the analyst's job is to read them. The pricing job is the same every time: the holder sold the issuer a digital option on leverage, and the sponsor gets to choose the leverage.

Theo meets his first portability clause the way most people do, in a podcast before a document. The *Cloud 9fin* episode "Focus and Foresight" (May 11, 2023), in which Will Caiger-Smith talks to covenant expert James Wallick, asks "when is a change in control not a change of control?" about Focus Financial's \$7bn buyout, financed without refinancing the company's existing debt, and the answer turns on the wording of the credit agreement. (For the definition itself, the portability segment of *Cloud 9fin*'s "Amigo Loans, Portability, Shipping," Sept. 3, 2021, is the covenant explanation; the derivation below is the pricing.) He brings Mags a carve-out from a sponsor-backed bond on the desk's watch list *(illustrative)* and starts on the add-back definition. She stops him at the cover page. "When was it printed?" Four years ago, with a two-year window. "Then it's a history lesson. Read it on the train." The next one is fourteen months old, its likely buyer is a sponsor that would rather keep cheap existing debt than raise new, and its add-backs run to a page and a half. "That one," Mags says, "is a short option. Price it." A strategic buyer that will refinance the target's debt anyway ports nothing; nobody ports a capital structure they are about to retire.

### 15.3.2 Derivation: a short digital on a number the counterparty chooses

**Exogenous version.** If pro-forma leverage at the change of control, $\ell_{\tau_c}$, were an exogenous random variable, the holder's put would survive portability only when the test fails:

$$
V^{\text{port}}_{\text{CoC}} \;=\; V_{\text{CoC}}\cdot \mathbb{Q}\!\left(\ell_{\tau_c} > \bar\ell \,\middle|\, \text{deal},\text{trig}\right),
$$

and the issuer's digital is worth $V_{\text{CoC}}\cdot\mathbb{Q}(\ell_{\tau_c}\le\bar\ell\mid\cdot)$. With $\ln \ell_{\tau_c}\sim\mathcal{N}(\ln \ell_0 + \mu\tau_c,\ \sigma_\ell^2\tau_c)$, the survival probability is $\Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln\ell_0 + \mu\tau_c - \ln\bar\ell}{\sigma_\ell\sqrt{\tau_c}}\right)$, a barrier problem with a known answer. It is also the wrong model.

> **Math, Gently.** *The bell curve's running total, and a coin that pays one or nothing.* Picture a histogram of surprises: for a few thousand closed deals, how far EBITDA at closing landed from the plan, in standard deviations. Most pile up near zero and a handful land two or three out; the outline is the bell, with height $\varphi(z)$ at $z$. Walk along the bottom from the far left and keep a tally of how much of the pile you have passed. That tally is $\Phi(z)$: half at the middle, $\Phi(0) = 0.5$; about 84% one standard deviation to the right, 16% one to the left, 97.7% two to the right. Because the bars are tallest in the middle, a small shift in a line near the middle moves the tally far more than the same shift out in a tail. A *digital option* is a coin that pays one or nothing: one if a quantity finishes on the agreed side of a line. Before discounting it is worth the probability of that side, so a digital on a normal quantity is one $\Phi$. Row 2 of the §15.1.2 table is one. Portability is another, sold the other way: the issuer holds a coin that pays "no put" whenever leverage clears the test.
>
> Tiny number, with an EBITDA log-volatility of 0.25. A sponsor needs 6.05 turns of signing EBITDA to finance the purchase and the test is 5.0x. With no add-backs, the holder keeps the put when EBITDA at closing lands below $6.05/5.0 = 1.21$ times its signing level, a barrier $(\ln 1.21 + 0.031)/0.25 \approx 0.89$ standard deviations above the median, and $\Phi(0.89) \approx 0.81$: the put survives about four times in five. Allow 20% add-backs and the barrier drops to $6.05/6.0 \approx 1.01$, about 0.16 standard deviations above the median, and $\Phi(0.16) \approx 0.56$. One defined term moved the line toward the middle of the bell, where $\varphi$ is tallest, and took a quarter of the probability with it.

**Endogenous version.** The acquiring sponsor sets new debt $F_{\text{new}}$ *after* reading $\bar\ell$. Let $E^{\text{rep}}$ be reported EBITDA at closing, $a\ge 0$ the add-back ratio the definition permits, so that the test is computed on $E^{\text{adj}} = (1+a)E^{\text{rep}}$, and let $F_{\min}$ be the least debt that finances the purchase given the largest equity cheque the sponsor will write. The sponsor can pass the test whenever $F_{\min} \le \bar\ell\,(1+a)\,E^{\text{rep}}$, and it will pass whenever the cost of the 101 offer exceeds the value of the turns of leverage it gives up. Take the limiting case in which the put is always more expensive than the forgone leverage (true for the deep-in-the-money puts in §15.2). The put survives only on the event

$$
\mathcal{A} \;=\; \left\{ E^{\text{rep}} < \frac{F_{\min}}{\bar\ell\,(1+a)} \right\}.
$$

Put a lognormal on reported EBITDA, normalized so that its mean is one at signing, and the probability that the holder keeps the put is the share of that distribution lying below the barrier $F_{\min}/(\bar\ell(1+a))$. Add-backs move the barrier to the left: the box's 1.21 becomes 1.01 at 20%, from comfortably above the expected outcome to almost exactly on it. The closed form, a normal probability, and its derivative in $a$ are in "The Full Derivation" at the end of §15.3.

Every point of permitted add-back lowers the probability that the holder keeps the put, and the derivative is largest exactly where the test is close to binding; Mags has been "deeply suspicious of anything called adjusted" for thirty years, and here is the derivative that justifies her. The put that does survive lives on $\mathcal{A}$, the states in which EBITDA came in low, which are the states where the holder most wants it, so the residual is not worthless. What the holder sold is the put's value in the *good* states, where the bond would have traded a few points below 101 and the sponsor would otherwise have had to pay up. This is a contract-design argument under $\mathbb{P}$; pricing the residual put is under $\mathbb{Q}$ and uses the §15.2 engine restricted to $\mathcal{A}$.

Ines turns it into a hand. "You are not playing against the deck. You are playing against a player who sees the test before choosing the bet. If he can make the bet that passes, he will. You only get paid when he cannot."

Ines's hand is easy to say and annoying to size. A covenant analyst can read the add-back definition and report that it is generous; she cannot tell you what a generous definition does to the probability that your put exists at all. The closed form above answers that for one lognormal and one set of inputs. A desk screening a stack of portability clauses wants the answer across a grid of add-back ratios, a proof that the machinery agrees with the formula, and a look at which states of the world the surviving puts live in. That is a dozen lines of code, and a dozen lines are cheap to verify.

`portability_put_survival` is the endogenous version from §15.3.2 with the sponsor's decision made mechanical. It draws reported EBITDA at close as a lognormal with mean one, so signing EBITDA is the unit of account. It computes `f_min`, the least debt that finances the purchase, as one minus the maximum equity share, times the enterprise value multiple, in turns of signing EBITDA. Then it asks, path by path, whether even that minimum debt exceeds the leverage test applied to adjusted EBITDA, which is `lev_test` times one plus the add-back times the draw. If it does, the sponsor cannot port and the put survives. The function returns three numbers: the share of paths on which the put survives, the mean EBITDA on those paths, and the mean on the paths where the deal ports. The last two carry the lesson, because they show the surviving put living in the weak states and the ported deals in the strong ones. It prices nothing. The residual put's value comes from running the §15.2 engine on the surviving states.

Three checks guard it. Survival never rises as the add-back grows across a five-point grid; because the seed is fixed, every grid point sees the same draws, so noise cannot break that. At a 20% add-back the simulated frequency sits within four binomial standard errors of the closed-form normal probability from the derivation. And mean EBITDA given survival is below mean EBITDA given porting.

The function assumes the sponsor ports whenever it can, so a sponsor who would sooner pay 101 than give up turns of debt is outside it, and it tests one gross multiple of reported EBITDA at close, so a test on net debt, on coverage or on a base fixed at issuance needs different code. The first time Kaz ran it across the desk's clause file, the table's average survival came back as `nan`. One clause had a test so far from binding that every path survived, the mean EBITDA on the ported paths was the mean of nothing, and the `nan` had travelled quietly into the average. The fix was a flag for empty sides before any average across clauses.

Across the listing's five-point grid of add-backs, with the box's inputs *(illustrative)*, the survival probability falls from about 81% to about a third. Same bond, same sponsor, same test; the only thing that moved was the word "adjusted."

#### The Full Derivation: the put's survival probability under portability

With $\ln E^{\text{rep}}\sim\mathcal{N}(-\tfrac12\sigma_E^2,\ \sigma_E^2)$ normalized so that $\mathbb{E}[E^{\text{rep}}]=1$ at signing,

$$
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}) \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln\!\big(F_{\min}/(\bar\ell(1+a))\big) + \tfrac12\sigma_E^2}{\sigma_E}\right),
\qquad \frac{\partial\,\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})}{\partial a} \;=\; -\frac{\varphi(\cdot)}{\sigma_E\,(1+a)} \;<\; 0 .
$$

The probability is a standard normal CDF evaluated at the log-distance between the barrier and the median of $E^{\text{rep}}$, measured in units of $\sigma_E$; the $+\tfrac12\sigma_E^2$ is the mean-preserving shift of Listing 15.1's lognormal sampler (§15.2). The derivative follows from the chain rule through $-\ln(1+a)$ in the argument, and its magnitude is largest where $\varphi$ peaks, at an argument of zero, which is the case in which the test is close to binding.

What it means for the desk: portability is a document feature with a price, it is spreading into the documents a systematic desk can parse, and Chapter 25's extraction pipeline should pull the leverage test, the window, the one-time-use flag and the add-back definition as four separate fields, because each one moves $\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A})$.


## 15.4 Special Mandatory Redemptions: The Deal-Break Gauge in the Acquirer's Curve

### 15.4.1 Desk framing: a bond with a deal-break digital stapled to it

An acquirer that has signed a deal but not closed it has a timing problem. It wants its financing locked now, because a committed bridge loan is expensive and the bond market can shut in a week. It does not want to own \$5bn of debt and no target if the regulator says no. The special mandatory redemption solves both. The acquirer sells bonds today; if the merger agreement is terminated, or the deal has not closed by an outside date, it must redeem them, usually at 101 plus accrued, within days of a notice. Kroger's window, three business days to 30 days after notice, is in the cold open.

Three drafting details decide the price, and Theo learns them the way he learned credit agreements, on paper. *(The documents are real and cited; the evening and its dialogue are invented.)* On the evening of the Kroger replay he prints three descriptions of notes and lays them side by side on the empty desk next to his, a highlighter in each hand. All three are obituaries. Tapestry's SMR notes were redeemed in November 2024, when the Capri deal died; Kroger's short tranches went in December, after the judge; Synopsys's, printed in March 2025 for its Ansys purchase, expired unused when that deal closed in July 2025. Theo's theory, carried over from restructuring, is that whatever drafting mattered in the dead deals will be in the next live one too, slightly reworded, waiting for someone to price it.

He starts with the question every restructuring banker asks first: where is the money while we wait? In the Synopsys prospectus the answer takes one sentence: "The proceeds from this offering will not be deposited into an escrow account" (Synopsys 424B5, Mar. 2025, EDGAR). Theo highlights it and writes in the margin what it means. The 101 is an unsecured promise by the acquirer. Between issue and redemption the holder does not own a pile of cash in a locked box; the holder is long the acquirer's credit, with a deal-break option attached. Anything bad enough to hurt the acquirer badly while the deal is pending hurts the promise too.

The second document is Tapestry's, from November 2023. Its SMR fired if Capri was not acquired by February 10, 2025, and then, inside parentheses, came a phrase that a proofreading associate would have skimmed: "(or such later date mutually agreed between us and Capri)" (Tapestry 424B5, Nov. 2023, EDGAR). Theo reads it three times. An outside date is the one fixed point in the whole option, the date by which the holder's patience is guaranteed to end one way or the other. This one could move, and the two parties allowed to move it were the two parties who most wanted the deal to live. The bondholder had no vote. Tapestry never needed the parenthesis, because the deal died in court before the date arrived, but a gauge built on February 10, 2025 would have been reading a clock whose hands the issuer and the target could hold still.

The third detail is not a sentence at all. It is a pattern, and it only shows up when the documents sit next to each other. Kroger put the SMR on its four short tranches (2026, 2027, 2029, 2031; \$4.7bn of \$10.5bn) and not on the 2034, 2054 and 2064 bonds. HPE, which Theo pulls up on his screen as a fourth exhibit, did the reverse for Juniper: SMR on the 2029, 2031, 2034 and 2054 notes, none on the 2026 and 2027 notes. Synopsys put it on the 2027, 2028, 2030 and 2032 notes and not on the 2035 and 2055 notes (prospectuses on EDGAR). There is no market convention hiding in that list. Each issuer chose, tranche by tranche, which money it would hand back if the deal died. Mags's reading of Kroger in the cold open, keep the hard-to-raise long paper either way, is one rationale; HPE's mirror-image choice says it is not the only one.

Kaz wanders over, reads the Kroger page upside down, and asks the question they asked in the replay that afternoon as though for the first time: is "special mandatory" stronger or weaker than ordinary mandatory?

"It's the kind of mandatory," Ines says, "that the issuer and the target can agree to postpone."

Theo caps his highlighters and sums up for nobody in particular. The escrow sentence tells him whose credit he is long while he waits. The parenthesis tells him whether the wait has a guaranteed end. The carve-out tells him something more useful than either.

The carve-out is a gift. Same issuer, same day, same ranking, same lawyers, and one tranche carries an option the next one does not. Whatever price gap the curve cannot explain belongs to the option, and the option's only underlying is whether the deal dies.

### 15.4.2 Derivation: the implied break probability and the duration jump

**Setup.** An SMR tranche pays coupon $c$ (percent of par, semiannual) and matures at $T$. Collapse the deal's resolution to a single date $T_f$ on which the redemption would settle if the deal failed, and let $\pi_f = \mathbb{Q}(\text{deal fails by } T_f)$. The **fail leg** is the value today of the coupons paid on or before $T_f$ plus the redemption price:

$$
G_f \;=\; \sum_{t_i \le T_f} \tfrac{c}{2}\,e^{-y_f t_i} \;+\; \big(101 + \text{AI}_{T_f}\big)\,e^{-y_f T_f},
\qquad y_f \;=\; y^{\text{TSY}}_{T_f} + s_{\text{acq}}(T_f),
$$

where $\text{AI}_{T_f}$ is interest accrued since the last coupon date (30/360 on US corporates) and $y_f$ is the acquirer's short yield, not the Treasury rate, because without escrow the 101 is the acquirer's credit risk. The **close leg** $P^{\text{close}}$ is what the same cash flows would be worth as an ordinary bond of the acquirer, read off the issuer's non-SMR curve at maturity $T$.

**Assumptions.** (B1) The resolution date is known; a random $T_f$ adds a time integral but changes none of the algebra. (B2) The acquirer's curve is the same whether the deal closes or breaks. (B2) is the weak link and gets its own blow-up below.

**Price and inversion.** Under (B1)–(B2),

$$
P_0 \;=\; \pi_f\,G_f \;+\; (1-\pi_f)\,P^{\text{close}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\hat\pi_f \;=\; \frac{P^{\text{close}} - P_0}{P^{\text{close}} - G_f},\qquad P^{\text{close}} \ne G_f .
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *A price between two prices is a ruler, and the probability is where the price sits on it.* Kaz's sticky note in the cold open was this formula in disguise. Lay a ruler on the table with two marks on it. One mark is the close leg, $P^{\text{close}}$, what the bond is worth if the deal goes through. The other is the fail leg, $G_f$, what it is worth if the deal dies. A price that is a probability-weighted average of the two legs has to sit somewhere between the marks, and how far along the ruler it sits, measured from the close mark toward the fail mark, *is* the probability of failure. The inversion is nothing more than distance travelled divided by the length of the ruler. Tiny number, from the worked example below: the close mark is 99.00 and the fail mark is 100.98, so the ruler is 1.98 points long. The bond trades at 99.40, which is 0.40 of a point along it, and $0.40/1.98 \approx 0.20$: one chance in five that the deal fails. When rates fall far enough that the close leg is worth more than the redemption, the ruler turns around. The fail mark now sits below the close mark, and a higher break probability means a *lower* price; the formula copes without help, because its numerator and denominator change sign together. When the two marks sit on top of each other the ruler has no length, and no price, however precise, can say where along it the market stands. That is the blind spot. The full version, with the sign flip and the error analysis, is the rest of this section.

**The sign flips with rates.** Differentiate:

$$
\frac{\partial P_0}{\partial \pi_f} \;=\; G_f - P^{\text{close}} .
$$

If rates or the acquirer's spread have risen since issue, $P^{\text{close}} < G_f$ and the SMR is a free put on the deal: the holder is paid 101 for a bond worth less, and roots for the judge. If rates have fallen, $P^{\text{close}} > G_f$ and the holder is short a digital: a break takes away a bond worth more than 101. In between, near $P^{\text{close}} = G_f$, the SMR price carries no information about $\pi_f$ at all and $\hat\pi_f$ explodes. The gauge's resolution is proportional to $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f|$. A thermometer that reads only when it is out of equilibrium is still a thermometer; you just have to know when it is switched off.

**A worked number** *(illustrative)*. Take a three-year 4.60% SMR tranche, the shape of Kroger's 2027s, whose non-SMR-curve value is $P^{\text{close}} = 99.00$. The ruling is expected in a quarter, so $T_f = 0.25$, and $y_f = 4.60\%$. No coupon falls before $T_f$; accrued is $4.60 \times 0.25 = 1.15$; so $G_f = 102.15\,e^{-0.0115} \approx 100.98$. If the tranche trades at 99.40,

$$
\hat\pi_f \;=\; \frac{99.00 - 99.40}{99.00 - 100.98} \;=\; \frac{-0.40}{-1.98} \;\approx\; 0.20 .
$$

The bond market thinks there is a one-in-five chance the judge blocks the deal. That number is free of the takeover premium, the target's standalone value and the equity arbitrageurs' borrow costs, which is why it is cleaner than the one backed out of the target's stock. Figure 15.3 shows the price line, the sign flip and the blind spot.

Illustrative: the three-year 4.60% tranche of Section 15.4.2 (fail-leg value 100.98, resolution in a quarter), with a 0.10-point price error on the right.](assets/figures/ch15_smr_gauge.png)

**Probabilistic duration.** Dollar durations add across the two legs, so the SMR tranche's duration is a value-weighted average of the fail leg's duration, which is about $T_f$, and the close leg's, $D^{\text{close}}$. The weight $w$ on the fail leg is its share of the price, which is close to the break probability itself when both legs trade near par. In the example, $w \approx 0.20$ and $D^{\text{close}} \approx 2.8$, so $D^{\text{SMR}} \approx 2.3$. On ruling day it becomes either 0.25 or 2.8. The hedge ratio does not drift; it jumps, on a date the court has published. Ben Dor documents the same discontinuity for rating changes, where a downgraded IG bond's limit hedge ratio "decreased from 1.00 to 0.45 after the rating change" (Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management pp.212–213). The SMR does by contract what a downgrade does by repricing.

> **Math, Gently.** *Duration is a seesaw, and dollar durations add.* A bond's duration answers one question: if yields move by one percentage point, roughly what percentage of its price does it gain or lose? Multiply that percentage by the price and you get the *dollar duration*, the points themselves. Points add in a way that percentages do not. If one leg of a position loses 0.05 of a point when yields rise and the other loses 2.2, the position loses 2.25, whatever the legs' sizes. The SMR tranche is a mixture of a fail leg and a close leg, so its dollar duration is the sum of theirs, and dividing by the price turns that sum back into a duration that is a weighted average. Picture a seesaw. A weight of 0.20 sits at 0.25 years, the fail leg, which matures on ruling day. A weight of 0.80 sits at 2.8 years, the close leg, which is an ordinary three-year bond. The balance point is $0.20 \times 0.25 + 0.80 \times 2.8 \approx 2.3$. That is the kind of number a risk system with a field for the judge would have shown in the cold open, where the real one showed 2.8 for every three-year Kroger bond; at Kaz's 36% the balance point would have been nearer 1.9. On ruling day one of the two weights falls off the seesaw, and the balance point jumps to wherever the remaining weight sits, 0.25 or 2.8. The full version, with the exact weight, is in "The Full Derivation" at the end of §15.4.

**Measure.** $\hat\pi_f$ is a $\mathbb{Q}$ number. The survival models of §15.5 produce $\mathbb{P}$ numbers. The gap between the two is the deal-break risk premium, and Mitchell & Pulvino (2001) tell you which way it points.

**When the gauge reads true, and when it is switched off.** Theo's first instinct after the Kroger week is to run the gauge on every SMR tranche in the market every morning. Ines makes him write down, before he runs anything, what would make the number mean something, and the list that comes back is short. The first item is a ruler with some length. When $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f|$ is a point or more, a ten-cent pricing error moves $\hat\pi_f$ by at most ten percentage points, which is a reading with honest error bars; when the gap is three cents, the same ten cents moves it by more than three hundred, which is noise dressed as a probability. The second is an issuer with bonds of its own at the short end that do not carry the clause, so that the close leg is read off a curve rather than guessed beyond its last point. Kroger's new non-SMR tranches all started in 2034, which made its 2027s an extrapolation problem until someone added the legacy short bonds as nodes. The third is a fixed outside date, because a mutually extendable date turns "fails by $T_f$" into "fails by whatever date two motivated parties agree on." Kroger then taught a fourth condition that nobody on the desk had written down: a non-SMR curve that stays put when the deal dies. On December 11 the buyback gave the 2034s a reason to move, and a gauge that took them as its control group would have been measuring the option against a ruler that could itself slide across the table. The check a seasoned hand runs first is the dull one. Before reading $\hat\pi_f$, read the denominator. If it is a few cents, the gauge is off, and the honest output is "no information."

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.267:* "Demand and supply would quickly act to create a price discrepancy, which would represent the premium that investors are willing to pay for the early redemption option. The size of the premium would implicitly reflect the probability that investors placed on the refinancing."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer's example is two senior issues of the same distressed company with different maturities, where a refinancing would take out one of them early: the price gap between them is the market's probability of the refinancing, expressed in points. Replace "refinancing" with "deal break" and you have the SMR gauge, down to the insistence that the comparison be made within one issuer's capital structure so that credit and ranking cancel. Where the 2026 desk departs: Moyer's investor eyeballs the gap and forms a view. The systematic desk inverts it with a fitted issuer curve, guards the denominator, marks $\hat\pi_f$ every day as a time series, and compares it with a physical-measure hazard model, so that the trade is the spread between two probabilities rather than an opinion about one.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** (1) *The control group is contaminated.* Assumption (B2) says the non-SMR tranches price the same in both states. They do not. On the day Kroger terminated it also announced a \$7.5bn buyback including a \$5bn accelerated share repurchase (Kroger release, Dec. 11, 2024, EDGAR), which is not nothing to the holders of the 2034s. If a break moves the acquirer's curve, $P^{\text{close}}$ is itself a mixture, and $\hat\pi_f$ is biased toward whatever the long bonds were pricing. (2) *Extrapolation.* Kroger's SMR tranches were the short ones and its new non-SMR tranches all matured in 2034 or later; curve-matching the 2027s means extrapolating to the short end unless you add the issuer's legacy short bonds as nodes. The code flags it. (3) *The outside date moves.* With a mutually extendable date, the gauge prices "fails by $T_f$," not "fails ever." (4) *Accrued.* Use the indenture's convention to the redemption date; a quarter's accrued on a 4.6% coupon is more than a point, which is most of the signal. (5) *The switched-off thermometer.* When $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f|$ is a few cents, report "no information," not 0.97.

#### The Full Derivation: probabilistic duration

Dollar durations add across the two legs, so with $D_f \approx T_f$ the fail leg's duration and $D^{\text{close}}$ the close leg's,

$$
P_0\,D^{\text{SMR}} \;=\; \pi_f\,G_f\,D_f + (1-\pi_f)\,P^{\text{close}}\,D^{\text{close}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
D^{\text{SMR}} \;=\; w\,D_f + (1-w)\,D^{\text{close}},\quad w = \frac{\pi_f\,G_f}{P_0},
$$

which collapses to the probability-weighted $\pi_f D_f + (1-\pi_f) D^{\text{close}}$ when both legs are near par. The weight is exact; the probability-weighted shortcut is an approximation that is good to a few hundredths of a year in the worked example, where $G_f/P_0 \approx 1.016$.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**What ROBO built from the vague version.** "Back out the break probability from the Kroger SMR bonds." ROBO divided the SMR tranche's price by 101 and reported 0.98. It was a ratio. It was not a probability. It never complains.

*The precise spec for Listing 15.3 (curve fit, guards and table schema) is in [The Engine: Full Specifications](#ch15-engine-specs) before §15.9's Spec You Hand the Robot.*

Doing this once for Kroger is a good afternoon with a legal pad and a Bloomberg screen. The gauge needs three things, and none of them is on the screen. It needs $P^{\text{close}}$, a price for the SMR bond that does not exist, because the only market for it is the one that includes the option, so it has to be built from the issuer's other bonds. It needs $G_f$, which is arithmetic but arithmetic with accrued interest, and accrued interest is where afternoons go to die. And it needs a denominator that the desk is afraid of. A systematic desk wants the mark every morning for every pending deal with an SMR, and the failure it fears is not a crash. It is a curve that works on Monday and goes quietly wrong on Thursday because someone added a bond to the fit by hand and the SMR tranche, the very thing being measured, helped price its own control group.

Listing 15.3 is a small pipeline of seven functions, easiest to read in the order the data flows. `z_spread` uses Brent root-finding on `bond_price` to find the constant spread over the Treasury zero curve that reprices a bond to its observed full price. `curve_match_close_price` takes one issuer's tranche table, leaves the SMR bonds out of the fit, converts the rest to Z-spreads, interpolates them across maturity with a shape-preserving PCHIP curve, and reprices each SMR tranche at its interpolated spread. That repriced figure is $P^{\text{close}}$, what the same cash flows would trade at without the option. Beyond the first and last node the spread is held flat and the row is flagged `extrapolated`. `smr_fail_leg` computes $G_f$: coupons paid on or before the resolution date plus 101 and accrued, discounted at the flat yield you pass in, which should be the Treasury zero plus the acquirer's short spread. `smr_price` and `smr_implied_break_prob` are the mixture and its inversion; the inversion returns the raw ratio, the clipped probability, and a flag for whether clipping happened. `smr_duration` weights the fail leg's duration by its share of value, and `spread_duration` supplies the close leg's by bumping the spread one basis point each way.

The checks follow the derivation. The worked $G_f$ of 102.15 discounted for a quarter. A round trip from probability to price and back, over eleven probabilities. The sign flip when rates have fallen and the holder is short the digital. And a synthetic issuer with a linear Z-spread curve, on which PCHIP must recover the line exactly. The last block makes two SMR tranches trade a few tenths rich to the curve and prints their implied break probabilities and a probabilistic duration.

Use it to put a daily mark on every pending deal with an SMR carve-out, and to set that $\mathbb{Q}$ number beside the survival models of §15.5. Do not use it when the non-SMR bonds are themselves moved by a break, which is assumption (B2) failing. The edge case that bites is the denominator guard. The function refuses only when $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f|$ is below `tol`, which defaults to 1e-6 points, a rounding guard and not an economic one. With the two legs three cents apart, a two-cent change in the observed price moves the answer from about one percent to about two-thirds, and nothing in the output objects. Set `tol` to the price noise you are prepared to ignore.

*The full listing is [Listing 15.3 in Appendix F](#code-15-3).*

The check that matters most is the round trip, and the check that will save you money is the one you cannot assert: if two SMR tranches of the same issuer imply very different $\hat\pi_f$, the deal has not acquired two probabilities; your curve has a kink, or one tranche is $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f| \approx 0$ and is telling you nothing loudly.

## 15.5 Antitrust and Regulatory Timelines as Survival Analysis

### 15.5.1 Desk framing: the regulator counts in working days

Every payoff in §15.2–15.4 fires on a date. The date is set by a process the RSSG reader knows as a sequence of filings: HSR notification and a 30-day initial waiting period (15 days for a cash tender offer), which a Second Request extends until 30 days after the parties certify "substantial compliance" (15 U.S.C. §18a); a European Phase I of 25 working days and a Phase II of 90 working days, extendable by 15 or 20 (European Commission merger procedures page); CFIUS, the Surface Transportation Board, state attorneys general, and a district judge with a calendar. The desk's job is to turn that sequence into a survival curve.

The base rates are lopsided in the way that makes survival analysis worth doing. Almost every HSR filing clears in the initial period; the event risk lives in a few dozen deals a year.

> **Annotation.** In default modelling a merger is a nuisance: it removes a firm before you learn whether it would have defaulted, so you censor it. In this chapter the roles reverse. #### The clock comes to the desk: ICE–MarketAxess, September 29 to October 18, 2026

The honest adjustment is modest: nudge $\pi_d$ down a little, stretch the expected time to resolution by at least the month that has just been added, and mark October 29 as a milestone that will carry information whichever way it goes. Theo takes the third, because it is a contract question and he has read the contract. The merger agreement carries two termination fees. In the cases where MarketAxess owes one, it owes ICE \$148.8m, about 2.5% of the equity value; if the deal fails on regulatory grounds, ICE owes MarketAxess \$327.4m, about 5.5% (DEFM14A). The second number is more than twice the first, and the proxy adds that ICE has no duty to divest assets to win approval. A reverse regulatory fee is a put the buyer writes to the target's holders on the regulator, and its size is the buyer's own revealed view of where the risk lives. The equity arbitrage spread blends all of this into one number together with financing, market beta and MarketAxess's standalone value. ### 15.5.2 Derivation: Kaplan–Meier to competing risks

Put $t=0$ at signing (or at HSR filing; say which). Each deal ends in one of three causes, $k=c$ (close), $a$ (abandon), $b$ (litigated block), or is right-censored at the cutoff. Let $t_1 < t_2 < \dots$ be the distinct event times, $n_i$ the number of deals pending just before $t_i$, $d_{k,i}$ the number ending in cause $k$ at $t_i$, and $d_i = \sum_k d_{k,i}$.

> **Math, Gently.** *Survival and hazard, counted on your fingers.* Survival analysis asks two questions about a group of things waiting for something to happen. The *survival function* $S(t)$ asks what fraction is still waiting at time $t$. The *hazard* asks: of the ones still waiting right now, what fraction finish in the next short interval? The hazard is a rate for the survivors, not for everyone who started, and that distinction is the whole trick. Take 100 signed deals. In the first month 5 resolve, so the hazard in month one is 5 out of the 100 at risk, 5%, and survival after a month is 0.95. In the second month 10 resolve, but only 95 were still at risk, so the hazard is $10/95 \approx 10.5\%$ and survival after two months is $0.95 \times (1 - 10/95) = 0.95 \times 85/95 = 0.85$. That multiplication, survival so far times the share of today's survivors who get through today, is the Kaplan–Meier estimator below and nothing more. What makes it valuable is that it keeps working when the group at risk shrinks for reasons other than the event, which is where censoring comes in. The full version is the next paragraph, and §7-B.4 derives the same tool for default.

> **Math, Gently.** *Censoring is leaving the stadium before the race ends.* You are timing a marathon, and at the four-hour mark you have to leave to catch a train. Some runners have finished and you know their times. The rest are still running. You do not know their times, but you know each one took *more* than four hours. Throwing them away would make the race look fast, since only the quick finishers would remain; recording them as finishing at four hours would be just as wrong. The honest move is to use them for exactly what you know: they were still at risk for every minute up to the moment you left, and after that they drop out of the count. That is right-censoring, and it is why the $n_i$ in the formula shrinks both when deals end and when they are censored. The trick has one condition. It works only if your leaving had nothing to do with how the runners were doing. If you left early *because your runner was losing*, the runners you stopped watching are not a random sample of the field, and the estimate is biased. That is *informative* censoring, and it is the ICE extension clause of §15.5.1: the deals that drop out of a sample as "still pending" are disproportionately the ones a regulator is holding up. The full version, with Greenwood's error bars, is in "The Full Derivation" at the end of §15.5.

**All-cause survival.** The Kaplan–Meier estimator walks through the event dates in order and, at each one, multiplies in the fraction of still-pending deals that did not end that day:

$$
\hat S(t) \;=\; \prod_{t_i \le t}\Big(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\Big),
$$

and the Nelson–Aalen cumulative hazard adds the same fractions instead of multiplying their complements, $\hat\Lambda(t) = \sum_{t_i\le t} d_i/n_i$. (We write $\Lambda$ for a cumulative deal hazard only inside this section; $\lambda$ stays reserved for default intensity.) Greenwood's formula puts a standard error around $\hat S$, and it grows as the number still at risk shrinks; it is written out in "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section.

> **Math, Gently.** *Four distributions for waiting, and what each one assumes.* A *distribution* is a full description of what a random quantity can do: every value it might take and how likely each one is. For waiting times, four shapes do most of the work on a desk. The *exponential* is the waiting time of something with a constant hazard, a 5% chance of resolving every month, forever. It is memoryless: a deal that has already waited a year is exactly as likely to resolve next month as a deal signed yesterday. That is true of radioactive atoms and false of mergers. The *Weibull* relaxes it with a shape parameter. Shape 1 gives back the exponential; shape above 1 makes the hazard rise with time; shape below 1 makes it fall. With shape 1.6 the hazard climbs as the deal ages, in proportion to time raised to the power 0.6, which is roughly what deals do as they move from filing through review to a decision: they ripen. The *Poisson* is the exponential's counting twin. If events arrive at a constant rate, the number that arrive in a year is Poisson, and it is the natural first model for how many Second Requests the agencies will issue in a year: 41 in FY2025, against 1,944 adjusted filings, about 2.1%. And the *normal*, the bell of §15.2 and §15.3, is the one that does not belong here at all, as Kaz found out in §15.5.1, because waiting times cannot be negative and the bell does not know it. Which shape fits is an empirical question, and Kaplan–Meier is the tool that lets you look before you choose, because it assumes no shape. The full version is in Chapter 5 (the distributions) and §7-B.4 (survival), and in the Cox model below.

**Cause-specific hazards with covariates.** For each cause, $h_k(t\mid x) = h_{0,k}(t)\,e^{x'\beta_k}$. Bohn and Stein state the restriction exactly: "the shape of the hazard function (and the corresponding survival function) is arbitrary, but the impact ratio of the hazards over time is constant" (Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.233). The coefficients are fit by Breslow's partial likelihood, which never estimates the baseline at all: at each date on which a deal ended in cause $k$, it asks how surprising it was that *this* deal ended rather than any of the others still pending, given their covariates, and chooses $\beta_k$ to make the realized sequence of endings least surprising. A Second Request coefficient of $-1.2$, for instance, says a deal under a Second Request closes at $e^{-1.2} \approx 0.30$ times the rate of an otherwise identical deal. Deals ending in other causes are censored for cause $k$; that is correct for cause-specific hazards and wrong for anything else. The partial likelihood and its gradient are in "The Full Derivation."

> **Math, Gently.** *The Cox model is one curve, stretched.* The proportional-hazards idea is that every deal's hazard has the same shape over time, the same rhythm of filing, review and decision, and that a deal's characteristics only scale that shape up or down. A covariate does not change *when* the hazard tends to rise; it multiplies *how high* it is at every moment. The multiplier is $e^{x'\beta}$, which is always positive, so it can shrink a hazard toward zero without ever making it negative. Tiny number: a Second Request coefficient of $-1.2$ multiplies the closing hazard by $e^{-1.2} \approx 0.30$. With a constant hazard, a deal closing at 30% of the rate takes $1/0.30 \approx 3.3$ times as long on average. With a Weibull of shape 1.6 the clock stretches by $0.30^{-1/1.6} \approx 2.1$ times, so a deal whose median close was six months becomes one whose median is about thirteen. Breslow's partial likelihood estimates the multipliers without ever pinning down the shared shape, the way you can tell that one runner is twice as fast as another without knowing the course. The restriction is in the name. If a covariate's effect changes over the life of a deal, say a Second Request matters a great deal in month three and hardly at all in month eighteen, the proportional assumption is false and the coefficient is an average of things that are not alike. The full version is in "The Full Derivation."

> **Math, Gently.** *Competing risks are three doors out of one room.* Every pending deal sits in a waiting room with three doors, marked close, abandon and block. Each door has its own hazard, the rate at which deals walk through it. Once a deal goes through one door it cannot use another: a blocked deal never closes. So the chance of eventually leaving through the close door depends on the other doors too, because every deal that leaves through "block" was not around to close. The tempting shortcut, $1 - e^{-\Lambda_c(t)}$, computes the chance of closing in an imaginary room where the other two doors are bricked up, and it overstates closing. Tiny number: in each month, 10% of waiting deals close and 5% are blocked. Ignore the blocks and after two months $0.10 + 0.90 \times 0.10 = 19\%$ have closed. Count them, and only 85% are left to try again in month two, so $0.10 + 0.85 \times 0.10 = 18.5\%$ have closed. The gap is half a point after two months and compounds every month after that; by a year and a half it is the eighteen points of Figure 15.4. The Aalen–Johansen estimator does the honest bookkeeping, and the fact that the three doors' totals add up to everyone who left the room is its unit test. The full version is in "The Full Derivation."

**Cumulative incidence.** The probability of ending in cause $k$ by $t$ is not $1 - e^{-\Lambda_k(t)}$, because a deal that is blocked cannot later close. The Aalen–Johansen estimator respects that: at each event date it takes the probability of still being pending just before it and multiplies by the fraction of pending deals that ended in cause $k$ on that date, then adds those pieces up. Because every deal that ends must end through exactly one door, the three incidences sum exactly to the probability of having ended at all, $\sum_k \hat F_k(t) = 1 - \hat S(t)$, which makes that identity the best unit test in the chapter. The estimator itself is in "The Full Derivation." Figure 15.4 shows the three incidences on the Listing 15.4 cohort, and how far the cause-specific shortcut overstates closing.

![Figure 15.4: By about 1.5 years the cause-specific shortcut (dashed) says 90% of deals have closed, while the competing-risks estimate says 72%, because 14% were abandoned and 9% blocked, and the three incidences sum exactly to total exits. Illustrative: Aalen–Johansen estimates from the seeded 600-deal synthetic cohort of Listing 15.4 (Weibull cause-specific hazards with planted coefficients, independent censoring), stopped where fewer than 20 deals remain at risk.](assets/figures/ch15_competing_risks_incidence.png)

**From clocks to PnL.** For a position whose price jumps by $\Pi_k(t)$ on outcome $k$ at $t$, under $\mathbb{P}$,

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\text{PnL}] \;=\; \int_0^{\infty} e^{-rt}\,S(t)\,\big[h_c(t)\Pi_c(t) + h_a(t)\Pi_a(t) + h_b(t)\Pi_b(t)\big]\,dt \;+\; \text{carry}.
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *Bayes' rule, or why nothing happening is news.* Bayes' rule is a recipe for updating a probability when evidence arrives: weigh each story by how likely it made the evidence you actually saw. Here the evidence is silence, a milestone passing quietly. Start at $\pi_d = 0.80$ that the deal closes. Suppose a deal that will close passes this milestone quietly 90% of the time ($q_d = 0.90$), and a deal that will break passes it quietly only 60% of the time ($q_b = 0.60$), because dying deals tend to make noise. Picture 100 deals like this one. Eighty will close, and 72 of those pass quietly. Twenty will break, and 12 of those pass quietly. Of the 84 quiet deals, 72 are closers, so the probability rises to $72/84 \approx 0.857$. Nothing happened, and the probability moved by almost six points. The rule has one trap: when $q_d$ and $q_b$ are equal, silence carries no information, and a model that updates anyway is inventing news. The formula follows; Chapter 10 builds a signal on it.

**Silence is data.** Each milestone that passes quietly (the 30-day wait expires with no Second Request; Phase I ends without Phase II; a complaint deadline passes) updates the deal probability by Bayes' rule, with $q_d$ and $q_b$ the probabilities of a quiet milestone under "deal" and "break," conditional on quiet so far:

$$
\pi_d^{+} \;=\; \frac{\pi_d\, q_d}{\pi_d\, q_d + (1-\pi_d)\, q_b},
$$

which rises whenever $q_d > q_b$. Giglio & Shue (2014) show that equity markets underreact to the information in the passage of time during pending mergers. If the credit market does the same, a target bond's spread should drift tighter through silent milestones; that is Chapter 10's trend signal with a legal calendar for a clock.

> **Poker Table.** Ines on the Second Request: "It's a tell, but a cheap one. In FY2024 the agencies issued 59 Second Requests and brought 32 merger enforcement actions, most of them consents and abandonments; the FTC litigated six. A Second Request means the hand goes to the river, not that you lose it. The expensive tell is the one nobody announces: the timing agreement with the agency, which you infer when the parties stop guiding to a close date." Replay the hand: markouts on the target's bonds over the ten days after each Second Request, split by eventual outcome. If the bonds that eventually closed sold off as hard as the ones that were blocked, the market was paying you to know the base rate.

> **Trend Watch.** *Regulatory regime as a shift in the baseline hazard, 2016–2026.* Early terminations of the HSR waiting period, the fast lane for clean deals, ran at 1,102 in FY2016 and 1,220 in FY2017, fell to 417 in FY2021 after the agencies suspended the practice, to 5 in FY2022 and 0 in FY2023, and came back to 265 in FY2025 (FTC/DOJ HSR Annual Reports, Appendix A). The second-request rate meanwhile oscillated between 1.6% and 3.0% of adjusted transactions. In procedure, the new HSR form took effect February 10, 2025; a federal district court vacated it on February 12, 2026 (FTC notice dated February 13, 2026); the Fifth Circuit granted an administrative stay on February 19 and then denied a stay pending appeal on March 19, 2026, and the agencies now accept filings on either form (FTC Premerger Notification Program page). What it means for the desk: the baseline $h_{0,c}(t)$ in the first 30 days is not stationary across administrations, so never pool FY2016–2019 clocks with FY2022–2024 clocks without a regime covariate, and expect the first month of every filing in 2025–26 to carry procedural variance that had nothing to do with antitrust.

**When the survival toolkit earns its keep, and when it quietly lies.** Theo's first Kaplan–Meier curve for the ICE deal is beautiful and, Mags points out, built on a sample that would embarrass a pollster. The tools in this section are honest under three conditions, and the desk's arguments in October were mostly about which of them held. They want dozens of comparable deals at risk at each point on the curve, because Greenwood's error bars widen as the risk set shrinks, and a curve that rests on eleven pending deals in month fourteen is a story told by eleven deals. They want censoring that has nothing to do with the outcome, which the ICE extension clause violates by design: an outside date that extends only when a regulator is still in the way sends the troubled deals into the "still pending" pile, and a naive estimator reads that pile as a random sample of patient deals. And they want a regime that holds still long enough to be measured. The early-termination series, 1,102 in FY2016, zero in FY2023 and 265 in FY2025, is a reminder that the baseline hazard of the first month changed twice in a decade by administrative decision. Pooling across those regimes without a covariate averages two different worlds. Adding the covariate fails in a different way when a sample sits entirely inside one regime: a `regime` column that never varies makes the Cox Hessian singular, and the fitter either crashes or, worse, returns a coefficient that means nothing. Then there is plain scarcity. The agencies issue a few dozen Second Requests a year, 41 in FY2025, and a cause-specific hazard for litigated blocks is fitted on a handful of events, so its coefficients carry error bars wide enough to drive a merger through. The check a seasoned hand runs first is not statistical. For every deal still pending in the sample, ask *why* it is pending: waiting for a scheduled vote, under a timing agreement, extended, in court. If the answers cluster, the censoring is informative, and the curve is optimistic about closing in proportion to how much they cluster.

#### The Full Derivation: Greenwood, Breslow and Aalen–Johansen

With $t_i$, $n_i$, $d_i$ and $d_{k,i}$ as defined at the start of §15.5.2, the Kaplan–Meier estimator and Greenwood's variance are

$$
\hat S(t) \;=\; \prod_{t_i \le t}\Big(1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i}\Big),
\qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}\big(\hat S(t)\big) \;=\; \hat S(t)^2 \sum_{t_i \le t} \frac{d_i}{n_i(n_i - d_i)} .
$$

For the cause-specific Cox model, with $R_i = \{j: t_j \ge t_i\}$ the risk set, the Breslow partial log-likelihood for cause $k$ is

$$
\ell_k(\beta) \;=\; \sum_{i:\,\text{cause}_i = k}\Big[x_i'\beta - \log\!\sum_{j\in R_i} e^{x_j'\beta}\Big],
\qquad
\nabla \ell_k \;=\; \sum_{i:\,\text{cause}_i = k}\big(x_i - \bar x_w(t_i)\big),
$$

with $\bar x_w(t_i)$ the $e^{x'\beta}$-weighted mean of the covariates in $R_i$. The gradient reads as the sum, over the deals that ended in cause $k$, of how far each one's covariates sat from the risk-weighted average of those still pending; the maximum is where those gaps cancel. Deals ending in other causes are censored for cause $k$.

The Aalen–Johansen estimator of cumulative incidence is

$$
\hat F_k(t) \;=\; \sum_{t_i \le t} \hat S(t_{i}^{-})\,\frac{d_{k,i}}{n_i},
\qquad
\sum_k \hat F_k(t) \;=\; 1 - \hat S(t),
$$

and the identity on the right is exact: summing over $k$ replaces $d_{k,i}$ by $d_i$, and $\hat S(t_i^-)\,d_i/n_i$ is precisely the drop in $\hat S$ at $t_i$, so the sum telescopes to $1 - \hat S(t)$.

Listing 15.4 builds the toolkit from scratch, then proves it on data whose answers are planted. `synthetic_deal_cohort` draws 600 deals with six covariates, generates a latent clock for each of the three causes from a Weibull proportional-hazards model with known coefficients, keeps the earliest as the outcome, and censors at an independent cutoff between half a year and four years; cause 0 means still pending. `_risk_table` does the bookkeeping everything else relies on: the distinct event times, the number at risk just before each, and the number of events, with ties kept in the risk set. `kaplan_meier` adds Greenwood bands (plain Wald intervals, clipped to the unit interval) and `nelson_aalen` the cumulative hazard. `aalen_johansen` computes all-cause survival and then, for each cause, the running sum of the previous step's survival times that cause's events over the number at risk. `cox_ph` maximizes the Breslow partial likelihood for one cause, treating the others as censored, with an analytic gradient and Hessian; it returns coefficients, standard errors, a baseline cumulative hazard, a convergence flag, and the correlation of Schoenfeld residuals with time as a proportional-hazards smell test. `bayes_deal_prob_path` applies the silence update one milestone at a time, taking the quiet probabilities as inputs.

The checks are identities wherever identities exist. With no censoring, Kaplan–Meier equals one minus the empirical distribution function. The three cumulative incidences sum to one minus survival to twelve decimals, and that survival matches the Kaplan–Meier curve. The close-hazard coefficients land within four standard errors of the planted ones. The Bayes path starts at 0.80 and rises through three quiet milestones.

## 15.6 Target vs. Acquirer: Spread Compression, Acquirer Widening, and Event Momentum

The morning after a deal is announced, the target's bonds and the acquirer's bonds start telling two different stories, and twenty years of research has checked which story usually comes true. Billett, King & Mauer (2004) find that target bondholders gain on average, with the gains concentrated in below-investment-grade targets bought by better-rated acquirers, while acquirer bondholders do not share in them. Furfine & Rosen (2011) find that mergers raise the acquirer's default risk on average. Anyone who has sat on the sell side of a pitch will recognize the shape: the target is lent the acquirer's balance sheet, and the acquirer pays for the privilege with its own.

Ines tells the third result as a hand she has replayed many times. Acharya & Johnson (2010) asked what the equity and CDS of LBO targets did in the days before anyone announced anything. They moved before the announcement, and they moved more when more insiders were involved, more parties brought inside the deal before it was public. For Ines that is adverse selection reduced to one regression. "Every seat you add to the financing is another player who has seen the cards. The tape doesn't care how the information got out. It shows you that somebody was buying protection, or buying the bonds, before you knew there was a hand." Her replay is the one she runs after every take-private: pull the markouts on the target's bonds and CDS for the ten trading days before the press release, line them up against the desk's own fills, and see which side of each trade the desk was on. If the desk sold protection to a client three days before an LBO, the hand history says so. Her response is never outrage. It is a smaller quote size, the next time, on the next name that looks like a sponsor's target, which is the only lesson the market lets you keep.

Theo builds the trade the chapter's machinery suggests, event momentum, and the building takes a morning. He runs `bayes_deal_prob_path` on each pending deal and looks for target bonds whose $\pi_d(t)$ is rising through silent milestones faster than their spread is tightening toward the acquirer's curve, the deals where the market is slower than Bayes. For sizing he borrows Paleologo's rule that a shorter runway to the event means a smaller position, because "there is less time to build it" (Paleologo_Advanced_Portfolio_Management p.152). The first backtest is glorious. Ines asks it one question: how much of that is the acquirer? Theo strips the acquirer's own spread change, the rates leg, carry and the market factor from the target's return, and most of the glory leaves with them. Most of what a target bond does after announcement is the acquirer's beta wearing the target's CUSIP. What is left is smaller, steadier and real. IDO or it didn't happen.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The multi-strategy relative-value pod (a generic description; no firm is named).* Inside a multi-strategy platform the same event-momentum book lives under two constraints that matter more than the model. The first is factor neutrality. The platform's risk team reads spread beta across hundreds of portfolio managers and treats the sum as a firm exposure, so the PM cannot keep the acquirer's beta even when it is paying; it is hedged with index CDX or with the acquirer's own bonds, and the PM is paid on the residual. The second is the drawdown stop. A PM who is two-thirds of the way to a capital cut sizes down before the risk manager calls, which means a deal that goes quiet for two months, good news under Bayes, can be closed out for budget reasons just as its probability is rising. The pod's edge is speed and hedging discipline; its weakness is that it cannot always afford to be patient with a clock that a regulator sets. And scope is policed: a PM approved for investment-grade event spreads who drifts into CCC targets gets a phone call, whatever the P&L says.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The dealer desk on the other side of the target's bonds (a generic description).* For a bank's credit-trading desk the morning after an announcement is a flow problem before it is a view. Clients who own the target call to sell into the rally or to buy more for the change-of-control put, holders of the acquirer call about the new supply, and the first prints of the day land in the target's CUSIPs, because that is where the news is largest. The trader has to reprice the target off the acquirer's curve before the acquirer's own bonds have finished moving, which is how the dealer sees the acquirer's beta arrive in the target's bonds first. Two things make the seat harder than it looks from the buy side. The bank's own financing arm may be arranging the acquirer's bridge and bonds, so the trading desk can be the one part of the building that is walled off from the deal it is making markets in. And the clients who call first are often the ones who have read the indenture, which makes the morning's first quote the one most likely to show up later in somebody's hand history.

## 15.7 When the Deal Dies: The Merger as the Credit

For a weak target, the bond price before resolution is a mixture of two curves:

$$
P^{\text{tgt}}_t \;=\; \pi_d(t)\,P^{\text{acq}}_t \;+\; \big(1-\pi_d(t)\big)\,P^{\text{sa}}_t,
\qquad
\Delta P^{\text{tgt}}\big|_{\text{block}} \;=\; -\,\pi_d(t^-)\,\big(P^{\text{acq}}_t - P^{\text{sa}}_t\big),
$$

where $P^{\text{sa}}$ is the standalone value. A block is a jump of known sign and estimable size on a date the court publishes, which a diffusion VaR will understate every time (Chapter 5's mixtures; Chapter 14's distress dynamics).

> **Math, Gently.** *A jump is a staircase; a diffusion is a ramp.* Most risk systems model a price as a diffusion: a random walk of small steps, like the man leaving the lamppost in §15.2, in which a big move is just many small moves that happened to line up, and the typical move over a day scales with $\sigma\sqrt{t}$. A jump is a different animal. It is a staircase: nothing for months, then one step down on a single day, and the size of the step has nothing to do with how volatile the price was yesterday. A court ruling is a jump. The mixture formula above gives the size of the step: the probability that was riding on the deal, times the gap between the two legs. Tiny number: a target bond priced at $\pi_d = 0.7$, with a 15-point gap between the acquirer leg and the standalone leg, falls $0.7 \times 15 = 10.5$ points on the day of a block. Over the quiet months before the ruling its daily volatility was small, because nothing was happening, so a one-day 99% VaR might read 1.5 points. The VaR is not wrong about the ramp. It cannot see the stair, because the stair was never in its data. The desks that survive these days carry the jump as its own line in the risk report, sized by the mixture and dated by the court's calendar, instead of waiting for a volatility estimate to learn it the hard way. The full version is Chapter 5's mixtures and Chapter 14's distress dynamics.

Mags tells the Spirit story standing up, in the order it happened, which is how she tells every story that cost somebody money. It is a slow Thursday in October 2026, Theo has asked why the chapter's mixture formula has a minus sign in front of it, and Mags has decided that the answer is an airline.

"July 2022," she begins. "JetBlue agrees to buy Spirit. From that day the Spirit bonds stop being Spirit bonds. Nobody prices an ultra-low-cost carrier with a fleet problem anymore; they price a JetBlue credit with an asterisk, times whatever you think the odds of closing are, plus a little bit of Spirit for the bad case. Nobody bothers to work out that little bit carefully, because nobody needs it. For eighteen months the standalone leg is a number in a footnote." In the chapter's notation, the market was pricing $\pi_d\,P^{\text{acq}}$ with care and $P^{\text{sa}}$ with a shrug, and the shrug was weighted by $1-\pi_d$, which made it look small.

"Meanwhile the company is waiting. A target that has signed a merger agreement is a company in a holding pattern. Merger agreements typically restrict what the target can do while the deal is pending: no big new financings, no dramatic strategy changes, operate in the ordinary course. Which is fine if you're going to be bought. If you're not, you've spent a year and a half not fixing yourself."

Kaz asks the obvious question. "Didn't the stockholders mind waiting?"

"The stockholders were being paid to wait. That's the part I want you to remember." The deal paid Spirit's stockholders prepayments while the agreement was in effect, about \$425mm by the time it ended. Nobody paid the bondholders anything for the same eighteen months.

Then the dates, and Mags slows down for them, because these are the facts and the rest is her opinion.

Spirit Airlines is the template. On January 16, 2024 the District of Massachusetts enjoined the JetBlue merger; three days later Spirit reported \$1.3bn of liquidity at year-end and sale-leasebacks of 25 aircraft. On March 4, 2024 the parties terminated by mutual agreement; JetBlue paid Spirit \$69mm, and Spirit's stockholders had already received about \$425mm of prepayments while the agreement was in effect. On November 18, 2024 Spirit filed a prearranged Chapter 11 with a restructuring support agreement signed by a supermajority of its loyalty-program and convertible noteholders: a \$350mm backstopped equity investment, equitization of \$795mm of funded debt, and a \$300mm DIP from existing bondholders (Spirit 8-Ks and releases, EDGAR). Signing to block took about 18 months; block to bankruptcy took about ten.

Mags reads the prepayment line twice. "The ticking money went to the equity. The ticking clock went to the bonds."

Theo maps the dates onto the formula. On January 15, 2024, the day before the ruling, the bonds sat at $\pi_d P^{\text{acq}} + (1-\pi_d)P^{\text{sa}}$, with the standalone leg priced from a footnote. On January 16 the judge set $\pi_d$ close to zero in a single afternoon, and the price stepped down to $P^{\text{sa}}$, whatever that was. The trouble was that nobody knew what it was, because nobody had needed to. Three days later the company published the inputs: \$1.3bn of liquidity at year-end and 25 aircraft sold and leased back. "That press release," Mags says, "was the standalone leg being priced for the first time in eighteen months. In public. By the company that most needed it to look good."

"So the jump was really two jumps," Kaz says. "One for the probability and one for discovering what the bad state was worth."

"And the second one didn't have a court date."

The ten months after the termination are, in Mags's telling, the part a model handles worst. The deal was dead, so the merger arbitrage community left and took its liquidity with it. The bonds now traded on the standalone airline, a company that had spent eighteen months in a holding pattern, and the restructuring that followed, prearranged and supported by a supermajority of the loyalty-program and convertible noteholders, was the bondholders' answer to a clock they had never been paid for. Chapter 16 picks the story up from there (§16.7): emergence in March 2025, a second filing less than six months later, and a coda that *The Octus Download* covered in its EP 29, "Spirit's Final Flight, AMC Hangs On & The Parent Trap" (May 7, 2026): Spirit ceased operations on May 2, 2026, after a floated federal rescue fell through.

Theo writes the lesson on a sticky note, and for once it is not a rule but a question for the next pending deal. *Who is being paid to wait, and who is waiting for free?* In Spirit the answer was on the face of the merger agreement. The equity had a ticking payment. The bonds had a mixture formula with a minus sign, and a standalone leg that nobody had priced because the probability in front of it had been large. The judgment the case teaches is about when to trust the mixture: it is a good model of the bond on every day that has no ruling, and it carries a hidden assumption on the day that does, that $P^{\text{sa}}$ was known all along. The bigger $\pi_d$ was, the less anyone looked at $P^{\text{sa}}$, and the bigger the surprise when they finally did.

## 15.8 Case Study: Warner Bros. Discovery 2025–26, the Covenant Strip and the Ticking Fee

**Tuesday, June 10, 2025, 8:05 a.m.** The offer documents arrive the way they always do, as a long PDF and a short press release that disagree about what matters. On Monday, WBD launched tender offers and consent solicitations for substantially all the notes of four entities in its family: Discovery Communications, WarnerMedia Holdings, Warner Media LLC and Historic TW. The coverage is about strategy. WBD plans to split itself in two, and its debt is being rearranged to fit (press reports, June 2025). The desk holds a position in the Historic TW notes *(the desk's position is fictional)*, old Time Warner paper that has already survived two mergers and has the documentation to show for it.

Theo reads the consent solicitation first, because that is where restructuring bankers live. "It's an exit consent," he says. "You tender, you get paid, and on your way out of the door you vote to strip the covenants from the bonds that stay behind. Delaware's Court of Chancery blessed the technique in *Katz v. Oak Industries* in 1986. I've worked on three of these."

"Is that legal?" Kaz asks.

"It's been legal for longer than you've been alive. The question is never whether it's legal. The question is whether you'd rather be the holder who tenders or the holder who's left behind."

Ines likes it already. "Threshold game. If enough holders consent, the ones who didn't are left owning a bond with no rules. So everybody consents, including the ones who would rather keep the covenants, because nobody wants to be the last holder of a stripped bond. Section 13.4 has the math. That is the question, and on June 10 nobody on the desk can answer it. The offer documents set out what a consenting holder receives. Mags has seen the move before, in a different neighbourhood. "Back in my day you stripped covenants when you were in trouble. Distressed exchange, exit consents, and the bonds left behind traded at forty. Now an investment-grade company does it on a Monday, with a press release about strategy." She flips through the Historic TW indenture, which is older than some of the desk. "Ask why they want it now. Covenants are cheapest to buy when nobody can see what they'll be needed for."

Kaz reaches for the framing the chapter has been building. "So consenting is selling an option. What's the underlying?"

"Everything the company might do next," Theo says. "That's the problem. I can price the option the moment you tell me the underlying."

**Decision point.** It is June 10, 2025. You hold Historic TW notes. The consent solicitation is open and no buyer exists. Do you consent, and what exactly are you selling?

**The strip came first.** On June 9, 2025, before any buyer had signed, WBD launched tender offers and consent solicitations for substantially all the notes of Discovery Communications, WarnerMedia Holdings, Warner Media LLC and Historic TW. Requisite consents arrived on June 16, and the supplemental indentures "eliminate substantially all of the restrictive covenants" and certain events of default once operative (WBD 8-K, June 13, 2025, EDGAR). Which protections survived in each series, the change-of-control offer included, must be checked supplemental indenture by supplemental indenture. The structural lesson does not wait for that check: the issuer exercised its counter-option *before* the put could be in the money, when consent was cheap.

The same week brought a smaller clause with a long future. The Twenty-Third Supplemental Indenture for the Discovery Communications notes, dated June 13, 2025, carries a section titled "Non-Boycott" that bars holders and their non-screened affiliates from agreements that would restrict them from buying the group's new debt or lending it cash (WBD 8-K, Exhibit 4.1, June 2025, EDGAR). It is a borrower writing a rule against creditor cooperation agreements into its own bonds, the counter-technology to the co-ops of §13.5, and the European Leveraged Finance Association warned publicly that such clauses were risky (Bloomberg Law, "Europe's ELFA Warns Warner Bros. Non-Boycott Clauses Are Risky," 2025).

**The loan said what it was.** A \$15bn bridge was documented as the "Non-Investment Grade Leveraged Bridge Loan Agreement" (June 26, 2025). Kaz: "They named the loan after its rating?" Theo: "It's the only honest title in the data room." It was refinanced on June 4, 2026 with a \$13bn term loan B at SOFR+250 and a €1,717mm term loan B at EURIBOR+250 at Discovery Global Holdings, maturing June 4, 2033, whose change of control, "such as the consummation of the previously disclosed proposed acquisition of the Company by Paramount Skydance Corporation," permits acceleration (WBD 8-K, June 2026). Stockholders approved on April 23, 2026. The deal closed on October 6, 2026 at \$31.01666668 a share: exactly six days of ticking ($6 \times 0.00277778$), or \$41,886,975.78 in aggregate. At closing WBD repaid the June 2026 term loans and its October 2024 revolver, and WBD subsidiaries guaranteed Skydance's new notes and credit agreement (WBD 8-Ks, EDGAR). > **Math, Gently.** *The expected positive part is a parking meter with a grace period.* The ticking consideration works like a parking meter that does not start running until September 30, 2026. Close before then and the meter reads zero. Close after and it charges \$0.00277778 a share for every day past the grace period. The expression $(\tau_c - t^{*})^{+}$, read "the positive part of $\tau_c - t^{*}$," is that meter: the number of days past the grace period if there are any, and zero if there are not. Its expected value is not the meter read at the expected closing date, because the meter never runs backwards. An early close contributes zero, not a refund, so the average reading is at least the reading at the average date, and usually more. Tiny number: suppose the deal is sure to close sometime in the 60 days after September 30, each day equally likely. The meter reads anywhere from 1 to 60 days, about 30 on average, and the expected ticking is about $30 \times 0.00277778 \approx \$0.083$ a share. Now suppose instead that the close is equally likely on any day of the 60 centred on September 30. The expected closing date is September 30 itself, and a careless calculation says zero ticking. But in the half of outcomes that land late, the meter runs 15 days on average, so the expected ticking is about 7.5 days, roughly \$0.021 a share. The real answer replaces these toy windows with the closing-time curve of §15.5; that is exercise 4. The actual deal closed six days past the grace period, for about \$0.0167 a share.

**Pricing the ticking fee.** Annualized, $0.00277778 \times 365 \approx \$1.014$, about 3.3% a year on \$31. That compensates shareholders for time, not for break risk, which they still carry. A survival model prices the rest: the expected ticking payment is $0.00277778 \times \mathbb{E}\big[(\tau_c - t^{*})^{+}\big]$ in days, with $t^{*}$ = September 30, 2026, and the competing-risks curve of §15.5 supplies the law of $\tau_c$.

**Thursday, May 7, 2026.** Theo spends the commute on *Cloud 9fin Syndication Nation*, "The \$81bn HY/IG jigsaw puzzle behind Paramount-WBD" (May 7, 2026), in which William Hoffman and Sunny Oh walk through the financing: about \$32bn of existing bonds and about \$49bn of new syndicated debt, split across asset classes. He arrives with a sketch on the back of a printout, a box for each market, and Ines asks the only question that matters to the desk: "Where does our Historic TW paper sit in your jigsaw?" Theo does not know yet, and says so. The answer will be in the guarantee documents at closing, and he has learned to wait for the document rather than the headline.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The dealer's syndicate desk (a generic description, grounded in the 9fin episode above).* To the banks arranging it, \$49bn of new debt is not a credit view; it is a placement problem. No single market absorbs that much at once, so the syndicate desk cuts it into pieces that each market can digest: secured term loans for the loan market and the CLOs behind it, high-yield bonds for one set of accounts, investment-grade bonds for another, with the rating on each piece deciding which buyers are even allowed to look at it. The desk's daily work in the weeks before launch is boring and decisive: soundings with anchor accounts, a book that fills or does not, price talk that moves, and a running tally of what is left on the banks' own balance sheet if a piece does not clear. The legacy \$32bn is a constraint, not a customer. Every protection the old bonds lost in June 2025 is room the new money can use, and the syndicate desk prices the new pieces knowing exactly how much room there is.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The CLO manager holding the SOFR+250 term loan (a generic description).* For a CLO manager, the June 4, 2026 term loan B at Discovery Global Holdings is an unusual asset: a loan whose change-of-control clause names its own likely trigger, the Paramount Skydance acquisition, in advance. The manager buys it knowing that the probable path is a par repayment at closing, months rather than years later. That is good news for credit risk and a nuisance for everything else: the spread is earned for a short time, the cash comes back at par in whatever market prevails on closing day, and it has to be reinvested inside the portfolio's tests, the spread and rating-factor averages, the CCC bucket and the overcollateralization cushions, which care nothing for the merger. The repayment arrived at closing on October 6, 2026 (WBD 8-K). To the CLO the deal was a reinvestment date.


**Tuesday, October 6, 2026.** The deal closes at \$31.01666668 a share, six days into the meter. Kaz computes the ticking to the penny and is faintly disappointed that the 8-K has already done it. Theo pulls up his notes from June 10, 2025 and answers the decision point out loud, sixteen months late.

"On our reading, we were selling the covenants that would have policed where acquisition debt could sit and which of the issuer's subsidiaries could guarantee it," he says. "We sold them in a week when there was no buyer, which is the week their value was hardest to see. I couldn't price the option because I couldn't see the underlying. The issuer didn't need to see it. It only needed the option gone before anyone could."

"So the consent was the credit event," Kaz says.

"The consent was the credit event. The merger was just the day we found out."

**What the desk missed in June 2025.** For the legacy bondholders, the decisive date was June 16, 2025, eight months before the buyer was chosen. Our reading, to be checked against the guarantee documents: once WBD subsidiaries guarantee acquisition debt, the new money has claims at the same entities as the legacy notes, and stripped indentures no longer police it. The strip, not the merger, was the credit event.


### Quant-Shop Life: The Security Master Has Forty New Columns

*(A fictional composite. The shop, its people, its tickets and its numbers are invented; the market events and the regulatory order cited are real.)*

The systematic asset manager occupies two floors of a converted warehouse, and on Tuesday, December 10, 2024, Priya, a data engineer eight months into the job, spends the whole of it on a ticket titled "Add M&A contingency fields to secmaster." Research has asked for forty new columns. Priya has learned that requests for forty columns are really requests for six, plus thirty-four that someone thought of in the meeting, so she starts with the six: `coc_double_trigger`, `agencies_required`, `defeasance_releases_coc`, `portability_window_end`, `smr_outside_date` and `smr_date_extendable`.

Every one of them starts a definitional argument. `coc_double_trigger` was going to be a yes-or-no flag until a covenant analyst points out that some ratings triggers need a downgrade by two of three agencies and some by all of them, which is how `agencies_required` becomes an integer. `defeasance_releases_coc` is worse. Whether an indenture's covenant-defeasance article releases the issuer from the repurchase covenant is a question for a lawyer, and the shop has one lawyer and three hundred issuers, so the field gets three allowed values, TRUE, FALSE and UNREAD. By lunch UNREAD is the most common value in the table, which Priya considers the first honest thing the security master has ever said.

The backfill runs through the morning. Her scripts pull registered prospectuses from EDGAR for the three hundred issuers on research's list and search them for the defined terms. The double-trigger language comes back clean for most investment-grade names. `portability_window_end` comes back empty almost everywhere, and at first she suspects her parser before realizing that it is telling the truth: investment-grade bonds rarely port, and the high-yield bonds that do are sold under Rule 144A and never filed. A null that means "not applicable" and a null that means "we cannot see it" look identical in a database, so she adds a second column to tell them apart. By three o'clock 212 of the 300 issuers are done, and the column count is up to seven.

At eleven, the point-in-time archive catches a vendor in the act. Overnight, the reference-data feed changed the maturity on one bond in the universe and overwrote the old value, as if it had always been the new date. The archive, which stores what arrived and when rather than what is true now, still holds the old record. Without it, every backtest run from that day on would have used the corrected maturity on dates when nobody knew it, a small leak of the future into the past. Priya files a ticket with the vendor and a note in the change log, and loses forty minutes to a call in which the vendor explains that the correction is a correction.

The worst find of the day is a single hand-keyed date. Someone had typed the European Commission's Phase I deadline for a pending deal as 25 calendar days after notification, instead of 25 working days. The deal-probability model, which treats a deadline that passes quietly as good news, had registered the milestone as passed more than a week early and nudged the deal probability up on a silence that had not yet happened. Nobody traded on it, probably. Priya adds a `clock_basis` column, CAL or WORK or BUS, with a holiday calendar per regulator, and the column count reaches eight.

In the late afternoon the Kroger ruling comes in: the court grants the preliminary injunction. Around six a portfolio manager updates the `ruling_date` and `ruling_outcome` fields on the Kroger 2027s himself, because the ticket queue is slow and the fix is obvious. Across town, on a dealer desk, a vice president with a USB stick is having roughly the same argument about a single column.

At 7:30 the next morning the risk report goes out, and the Kroger 2027s' spread duration has fallen from about 2.3 to 0.25 overnight *(illustrative)*, dragging the portfolio's spread duration with it. The risk committee, three people on a call, asks the question every risk committee asks: why is this number different from yesterday? The answer is that the number is right. The bond is no longer a three-year Kroger bond with a deal attached; it is a 101 claim that will settle within a month. The committee accepts that in two minutes and spends the next twenty on the question it actually cares about: who changed the field, when, and who reviewed it? The change log shows a live field edited by a PM outside the ticket process. That is the meeting's real finding, and it goes in the minutes.

Priya closes the day by sorting the open-ticket queue by age, a habit she picked up from her last job. The oldest is from 2020, four years old, filed by a researcher who has since left: "SMR tranches carry call-schedule duration, not probabilistic duration." It is, she realizes, the bug the risk report just exposed, described in one line four years earlier. A month later the SEC will publish an order against a systematic manager whose employees had identified model vulnerabilities "in or before March 2019" that the firm waited until August 2023 to address (SEC Press Release 2025-15, Jan. 16, 2025). Priya reads it in January and closes her four-year-old ticket the same afternoon.

Her tally for the Tuesday: eight new columns of the forty, 212 issuers backfilled, one vendor correction caught, one hand-keyed clock fixed, one governance finding, and no trades. By the standards of the job it was a very good day.

> **Taped to the Monitor.** *The chapter in seven lines.*
>
> - **Every deal clause is a payoff function.** Write it as (outcome, trigger, price at trigger), and store the issuer's counter-options (consent strip, defeasance, exchange offer) next to it in the security master.
> - **CoC moneyness is coupon vintage.** The put cares about $101 - P$, not the credit story. The IG double trigger needs both inspectors, and for LBOs the trigger and the payoff covary ($M > 1$).
> - **Portability is a short digital on a number the sponsor chooses.** Every point of permitted add-back lowers the odds that your put survives.
> - **The SMR is a $\mathbb{Q}$ break gauge inside the acquirer's curve.** Its resolution is $|P^{\text{close}} - G_f|$, its control group is contaminated, and its duration jumps on ruling day.
> - **Regulatory clocks are competing-risks survival data.** Censoring is informative, regimes shift $h_0$, and $\sum_k F_k = 1 - S$ is your unit test.
> - **Silence is data, and beta is not alpha.** Update $\pi_d$ through quiet milestones, then strip the acquirer's beta before claiming the event. IDO or it didn't happen.
> - **The most expensive three words have a cousin:** "defease certain obligations."

### The Engine: Full Specifications {#ch15-engine-specs}

The three listings of this chapter were explained where the story needed them. Their precise specifications, the part ROBO actually reads, are collected here so that they stop interrupting the derivations. Each is the contract the corresponding listing in Appendix F was written against.

**Listing 15.1, the change-of-control put ([Appendix F](#code-15-1)).** Implement `price_coc_put` with the payoff conditional on deal × trigger, the post-close bond priced on the post-close curve, the trigger allowed to depend on the post-close spread (to capture $M>1$), and an optional issuer counter-option (defeasance or strip) that may depend on how far in the money the put is.

- **Inputs.** `deal_prob` $\pi_d\in[0,1]$ (state the measure); `trigger_prob_given_deal`, a float or a callable mapping spreads (decimal, shape `(n,)`) to per-path probabilities; `post_spread_sampler(rng, n)` returning decimal spreads of shape `(n,)`; `coupon` in percent of par; `maturity_years` and `t_close` in years (ACT/365 clock; coupons 30/360 semiannual, valued on a coupon date so accrued is zero); `defeasance_prob`, a float or a callable of intrinsic value.
- **Outputs.** A dict with `value` (points per 100 face at signing), `stderr`, `moneyness_ratio` $M$, and `mean_intrinsic`.
- **Edge cases.** `deal_prob == 0`, a scalar trigger probability of 0, or a scalar defeasance probability of 1 returns exactly 0. A bond maturing before `t_close` returns 0 with a warning. Spreads are clipped at 0. $M$ is `nan` when intrinsic value is zero everywhere.
- **Variance reduction.** Integrate the deal and trigger Bernoullis analytically (Rao–Blackwellization): simulate only the spread.

**Listing 15.3, the SMR break gauge ([Appendix F](#code-15-3)).** Fit a Z-spread curve through the issuer's non-SMR tranches by maturity (PCHIP, flat beyond the last node, with an `extrapolated` flag), price each SMR tranche on that curve to get $P^{\text{close}}$, compute the fail leg $G_f$ with accrued interest and the acquirer's short yield, invert for $\hat\pi_f$ with a denominator guard and clipping report, and return the value-weighted probabilistic duration. Schema: a DataFrame with columns `tranche_id` (str), `coupon` (percent), `maturity` (years, on the half-year grid so that `bond_price` values on a coupon date), `price` (full price per 100), `is_smr` (bool).
