# Chapter 1-B: A Week in Five Quant Shops

> **Prerequisites.** Chapter 1, and in particular the Halvorsen RFQ that opens it. Nothing else. Every piece of mathematics in this chapter is small, and each one is introduced from scratch in a Math, Gently box that points to the chapter where it grows up. This chapter is an ethnography rather than a curriculum: it walks past the risk pack, the morning explain, the limit e-mail and the restricted list on the way to the coffee machine, and leaves the teaching of them to Chapters 3-B, 8-B and 8-C.

*A note on what is real.* The five people who narrate these days are fictional composites, and so are their firms, their colleagues and every number that is not followed by a source. What is real is the furniture: the regulations that set the clocks, the podcast transcripts and annual reports in which real firms describe their own routines, and the Senate report and SEC orders that recorded what went wrong when the routines failed. Where a real firm or person appears, they say only what a cited public document says they said, and none of them walks into a scene. Halvorsen Building Products, whose 7.25% senior unsecured notes due 2029 cross every desk this week, is fictional too. It is the bond from Chapter 1, rated B3/B−, last seen opening at 78 on the Monday after a lender call.

## Cold Open: Five Alarms

The first alarm goes off at 5:40 a.m. in a one-bedroom in Hoboken, and Hal Brennan, a junior trader on a bank's credit desk, reaches for his phone before his glasses. The night desk in London has left him a Bloomberg chat: two index rolls booked, one client trade with a missing allocation, and the sentence "no drama," which in Hal's experience is the overnight desk's way of saying the drama has been left for New York.

At 5:55 in a Connecticut commuter town, Dov Adler opens a dashboard that shows one number against one line. The number is his book's P&L since the start of the year. The line is the drawdown at which the multi-strategy platform he works for starts taking his capital away. The distance between them is the first thing he sees every morning, and his wife has learned to read his face for it the way farmers read the sky.

At 6:10 in Boston, Ngozi Achebe-Lund, who runs data and research for a systematic credit team inside a large asset manager, reads the overnight load log on her phone in the dark so as not to wake the baby. Forty-one thousand TRACE records loaded, three hundred and twelve corrections, nineteen cancels, and one line in amber that says *as/of volume above threshold*. She puts the phone face down. The amber line will still be there at eight.

At 6:30 in Jersey City, Priya Raman's pager app, which belongs to a non-bank electronic market maker and is set to a tone she chose because nobody else at the firm uses it, is silent. She checks that it is silent on purpose. It is. The silence will last seventy minutes.

At 6:45 on the Upper West Side, Sol Ferreira, an analyst at a distressed fund, opens his firm's restricted list before he opens anything else. He is looking for one name. It is still green. Last Friday, Halvorsen held a lender call; on Sunday night the company's advisers phoned the fund about joining a group of holders; and Sol knows that the green cell is the only thing between him and a week in which he will be allowed to think about Halvorsen but not to do anything about it.

So the only person in five shops who checks anything about Halvorsen before coffee is the one checking whether he is still allowed to look at it. Halvorsen's 7.25s are 78 bid on the composite. Everybody else is about to discover that something far more boring is broken.

By Friday evening you will have spent a day in each of these rooms: a market maker, a multi-strategy pod, a systematic asset manager, a bank's credit-trading desk and a distressed fund. You will have watched five very different businesses touch the same bond, and you will have seen that in all five the senior people spend the week on the same unglamorous question, which is not *what is this worth?* but *who is responsible for this number, and can they prove where it came from?* The models in Parts II to VIII are built on top of the answers.

## 1B.1 Monday. The Market Maker: "Save What You Knew When You Knew It"

*Fictional composite: a non-bank electronic market maker that quotes corporate bonds, credit ETFs and index products. The rhythm of the day is drawn from public accounts by two market makers whose businesses are mostly equities and options, transposed here to credit; the firm, the people and the numbers are invented.*

Priya is on the rota. At her firm, as at most electronic market makers, that does not mean being woken at 3 a.m. It means something closer to what a production engineer at Jane Street described on the firm's podcast: support is "not really the same thing as kind of being on call overnight or on the weekends. This is really like, during the trading day you are present and responding to issues that are popping up live," and "between a quarter and a third of your time you'll actually be on rotation" (*Signals and Threads*, Ep 20, "Solving puzzles in production," 7 Oct 2024). For the other two-thirds Priya builds tools. This week she is the person the traders call when a tool misbehaves, and the person who explains, at 4:30, why it did.

She is at her desk by 7:15. The first quarter of an hour is news, the same fifteen minutes that Optiver's account of a trading intern's day describes as "about 15 minutes going over the news" (Optiver, "Day in the life: quant trader intern"). Then a short standup about how the open will behave: a heavy new-issue calendar, a rally on Friday that the index has not quite believed, one name that held a lender call. Then the scripts run, the ones that check limits and prices before anything is allowed to quote, and at 7:40 one of them goes red.

The firm buys an evaluated-price file from a vendor every night. For the thousands of bonds that have not traded today, or this week, the vendor's number is the engine's starting guess: the prior that a fresh print or a dealer run will then move. Monday's file has arrived, and it has quietly restated Friday's history for 1,900 CUSIPs. Friday's evaluated prices, the ones the engine closed on, are now different numbers, as though Friday had happened twice.

Vendors do this. A Jane Street engineer describing a different market on the same podcast put it with the weariness of long acquaintance: vendors "tell you, whoops, made a mistake" (*Signals and Threads*, Ep 29, "Swapping the engine out of a moving race car," 2 Sep 2026). What saves Priya's morning is the habit the same engineer described next. His firm saves "all the data ... along with the time we received it." Priya's firm does the same, so on her screen Friday's Friday still sits next to Monday's Friday, each stamped with the minute it arrived.

The question in front of her is narrow and practical. The engine is about to seed 1,900 bonds from a number that changed overnight for reasons nobody has explained yet. If she lets it, the open will quote off the restated history; if she blocks it, it will quote off a number the vendor now says was wrong. She does what the archive makes possible: the engine opens on Friday's original file, the restatement goes into quarantine with a ticket to the vendor, and the research team gets a two-line note that 1,900 CUSIPs now have two Fridays. It takes an hour. Without the archive it would have taken a back-test, because a back-test run next spring would see only the restated Friday and would conclude, wrongly, that the engine had known on Friday evening what the vendor only admitted on Monday morning. The engineer on the podcast called the thing the archive prevents "a time machine." Chapter 27 calls it the provenance problem, and Chapter 9-B spends a chapter on the TRACE version of it.

The bond market opens. TRACE dissemination starts at 8:00, prints begin to scroll, and for two hours Priya's job is the job most people imagine a production engineer has, which is watching graphs that are fine.

At 10:52 Halvorsen arrives. A client is selling \$2mm of the 7.25s through an all-to-all RFQ, and Priya's engine sees it along with every other responder. The engine's arithmetic is simple to describe and hard to tune. It starts from its estimate of where the bond would trade between two people who know only what is public, subtracts what it would cost to hold the bonds until somebody else wants them, and subtracts what it would cost to hedge them in the meantime with the high-yield ETF and the CDX HY index. On a Monday after a lender call, that last term is ugly. The hedges see the high-yield market; they are blind to Halvorsen's credit agreement, and the risk that matters this week lives in the agreement. The hedge charge pushes the engine's bid below 77, well out of the competition, and the engine passes. A minute later TRACE shows the clip trading at 78 with somebody else. Nobody on Priya's floor notices, because a trade that did not happen produces no P&L, no break and no page.

At 11:10 a page. The deployment that went out at 9:00 included a new fat-finger band on the quote engine: a check that rejects any outgoing price that looks like a typo. It has just rejected a perfectly legitimate quote of 9, nine cents on the dollar, on a bond whose issuer defaulted in the spring and now trades as a claim on a recovery. The band had been written to reject prices outside 40 to 120, which is where most bonds spend most of their lives and where the person who wrote the ticket had assumed all bonds lived. It was, Priya thinks, a belief about the world that Halvorsen's own bonds had been gently testing since Friday.

The credit version of this bug has a famous cousin. On 20 April 2020 the front-month WTI crude future settled below zero, and the Jane Street engineer on Ep 29 recalled what that did to systems built on assumptions "baked into your trading and risk systems, like this price will always be positive": order entry itself broke. Priya rolls the deployment back by 11:25. The band comes back that afternoon keyed to price bucket, with prices below 10 allowed for paper the security master tags as defaulted or recovery-priced. The rollback also goes into a folder with a calendar attached. The same price and size checks sit in front of the firm's ETF and futures hedges, where they are a legal control: SEC Rule 15c3-5 requires a broker with market access to reject orders that breach "price or size parameters," reviewed "no less frequently than annually," and the chief executive certifies the controls once a year. Nobody at the firm has ever enjoyed the week before that certification, and Priya has just added a page to it.

> **The Spec You Hand the Robot.** *Vague version:* "reject unreasonable prices." *What ROBO built from it:* a band from 40 to 120 on every bond in the security master, because that is where the training data said bond prices were, plus, for symmetry, a rejection of every price on a bond that had not traded in thirty days, since an unobserved price could not be shown to be reasonable. ROBO did exactly what was asked. *The precise version:* bands by price bucket, with widths set from each bucket's own history of one-day moves; sub-10 prices permitted where the security master flags the instrument as defaulted or recovery-priced; a separate, tighter band for exchange-facing ETF and futures orders, which are the ones Rule 15c3-5 governs; and a logged reason for every rejection, so that Wednesday's alert review has something to read (§27.7, §27.8).

At 2:00 the afternoon finds its own small misery. An alert on the CDX HY feed opens, closes on its own forty seconds later, opens again. Nobody acts on it because there is nothing to act on, and it keeps flickering. The production engineer on Ep 20 described exactly this: pages that "open and close and you did nothing to resolve them," which go on "taking up mental space." Her team's response was a tool that asked "What percentage of alerts are we actually actioning?", and Priya's firm built its own version two years ago. She runs it. Over the last month the flickering alert has fired 212 times and been acted on twice *(illustrative)*. She moves its threshold, writes one line in the change log saying why, and the afternoon goes quiet in the particular way that afternoons go quiet when an alert has stopped crying wolf.

At 4:30 the desk holds its end-of-day reflection. Optiver's description of its own version is the closest public account: "close to an hour digging into our trades: what we did, why we did it ... what we didn't do," ending with "a few small but actionable goals." The third clause is the expensive one. Fills arrive with a P&L attached and so review themselves; trades not done arrive with nothing and are dug out of the RFQ log by hand. Priya pulls up the Halvorsen RFQ from 10:52, the one the engine passed, and sets beside it the composite an hour later: 77.25.

> **Math, Gently.** *The markout of a trade you did not do.* A markout is the price path after a decision, read as a P&L. If you buy a bond at $P^{\text{fill}}$ and the mid an hour later is $S_{t+h}$, the one-hour markout on face amount $n$ is
>
> $$\text{markout}_h = \left(S_{t+h} - P^{\text{fill}}\right)\cdot \frac{n}{100}.$$
>
> (Here $n$ is the face amount in dollars and prices are in points per 100 of face.) The trick is that the formula does not care whether *you* did the trade. Halvorsen traded at 78 with someone else, and an hour later the composite was 77.25, so had Priya's engine matched the winning price it would be marked at $(77.25 - 78) \times 2{,}000{,}000/100 = -\$15{,}000$. Passing "earned" 75 cents on \$2mm, which is \$15,000 the desk will not book and for which nobody will thank the engine. One pass proves nothing; the price can move for a hundred reasons. A month of passes, set against a month of fills, tells you whether the engine is passing on the hands it ought to pass on, and that comparison is impossible if nobody logged the passes. The full version, with the sign conventions and the survivorship problem of measuring only the trades you won, is §3.3 and §3.3.5.

The desk writes down its few small, actionable goals. One is a line in the hedge-cost model, which charged Halvorsen as though it were any B3 bond on a rally day rather than a B3 bond whose lender had just been on the phone. The charge was, for once, right for the wrong reason. Ines Okafor would call that the most dangerous kind of right.

> **Poker Table.** That night Priya plays in a monthly game that Ines Okafor, who runs credit market making at a dealer elsewhere in this book, has hosted for years. The street's card games are where people talk about anything except live prices, which is why the compliance departments tolerate them. Priya tells the story of the pass without the name. Ines listens the way she listens to everything, as a hand. "What was the pot, what were the odds, and who at that table knew more than you? You folded. Fine. But you still get to see the river." The passed trade has a price path just like a played one; folding without looking at the river is how a player ends up folding the same winning hand for a year. Marguerite Dunleavy, who comes to the game mainly for the arguments, wants to hear about the vendor file instead. "You kept Friday's Friday?" she says. "Good. We kept it in a drawer." A pause. "The drawer was worse."

> **View From Another Seat.** *The non-bank market maker, in its own published words.* To this seat Halvorsen is not a credit so much as one leg of a basket that also holds the high-yield ETF, a CDX position and possibly the issuer's equity. The seat quotes tight and small when the ETF and portfolio-trading channels offer a cheap hedge and an exit, and pulls back when its models see a jump rather than a diffusion, which is the polite way of saying "a lender call." Jane Street's public pages describe the ambition: the firm says it prices "more than 25,000 bonds" and that its presence in electronic, portfolio and ETF trading "allows us to be a consistent fixed income liquidity provider for clients in all market environments" (janestreet.com, client-offering page, fetched 18 Oct 2026). The other half of the seat's reality is that its credit balance sheet is a decision someone revisits. Flow Traders, an ETP market maker, wrote that it "strategically reduced the capital deployed to fixed income in 2025," and its fixed-income value traded fell to EUR 795 billion from EUR 943 billion (Flow Traders, *Annual Report 2025*, p.18); material changes to its limits need quarterly committee approval (p.31). A market maker's appetite for Halvorsen this Monday is a quarterly committee's opinion about fixed income, expressed through a hedge-cost term. Chapter 4-C does the full anatomy of the seat.

## 1B.2 Tuesday. The Pod: "IDO or It Didn't Happen"

*Fictional composite: a credit portfolio manager on a multi-manager platform. The platform's habits are drawn from public interviews with people who have run risk and books at such firms; the one set of drawdown thresholds quoted below was reported in the press about one firm and describes that firm, not pods in general. Dov Adler, his analyst, his risk manager, his pair, Kestrel Roofing and every number without a source are invented.*

Dov's 5:55 number has not moved overnight, because P&L does not move while you sleep unless your book is in Asia, and Dov's is not. The line has not moved either. What he studies on the train is the distance between them. His book is down \$16 million from its high-water mark on an allocation of \$400 million, and the platform's first line, the one at which his capital is cut in half, sits at \$24 million *(illustrative)*. Two-thirds of the way there. He does the arithmetic the way everyone on the platform does it, in bad days rather than dollars. At his book's typical daily swing of about \$1.6 million, the remaining \$8 million is five bad days, or one very bad one.

At 7:15 he is at his desk in a long room with forty other desks and a sign over the coffee machine that says the coffee machine is being looked into. The first thing on his screen is not the market. It is the x-ray.

Platforms buy factor models and run every pod's book through them overnight. Giuseppe Paleologo, who has spent much of his career in risk at large multi-manager firms, described what a PM receives each morning on *Odd Lots* ("How to Succeed at Multi-Strategy Hedge Funds," 20 May 2024): "It's a little bit like having an x-ray of your body in real time." On the same episode he said that platform books run "purely market neutral and factor neutral portfolios." The reason is commercial rather than modest. The platform can buy high-yield beta for a few basis points through an index or an ETF. What it pays a PM a share of the P&L for is the part it cannot buy.

Dov's x-ray has one line in orange. His book, which is meant to be flat to the high-yield market, is short it. Not dramatically short, but short, and Friday was a rally.

He knows where it comes from before he clicks, because there is only one place it can come from. Since April he has run a pair: long Kestrel Roofing's 6% notes due 2030, short Halvorsen's 7.25s due 2029. Two building-products credits, both single-B, both levered to the same housing cycle, and in Dov's reading of their documents and their maturity walls, one of them was going to be fine and the other was going to need a conversation with its lenders. On Friday afternoon Halvorsen had that conversation. The Halvorsen bonds went from 83 to 78, and Dov's morning explain for Friday, the one the platform's risk system produced overnight, came out like this *(illustrative)*: the pair made \$2.24 million; the market-beta line lost \$0.10 million; the idiosyncratic line made \$2.34 million.

The desk has a motto for that last line, older than Dov's tenure and taped to two monitors: IDO or it didn't happen. Until carry, rates and the market have been stripped out, a gain is a guess about where the market went. Friday's gain was the real thing. Halvorsen fell on its own news while the market rallied, and the hedge had done its job of making the rally nearly irrelevant to the pair. Nearly: the beta line was small and negative, and the x-ray says it will be larger and negative on the next rally. A hedge that works on Friday can be the reason for Tuesday's orange line.

> **Math, Gently.** *Beta, residual, and why the ruler is DTS.* A bond's *beta* to the market answers one question: when the market moves one unit, how many units does this bond move? The *residual* is whatever is left after that, the bond's own news. The factor model's x-ray is just this split, done for every bond in the book. For credit there is a useful empirical regularity about the split: on a broad market day, spreads move roughly in proportion to their level. If the average high-yield spread tightens 4%, a bond at 350 bp tends to tighten about 14 bp and a bond at 1,400 bp about 56 bp. A bond's price, in turn, moves by roughly its spread duration times its spread change. Put the two together and a bond's exposure to the market scales with market value $\times$ spread duration $\times$ spread, which is Duration Times Spread, $\text{DTS}_i = D^{\text{spr}}_i \cdot s_i$, weighted by market value. Two bonds with the same face amount can carry very different amounts of market. In April, \$60 million of Kestrel at 96 (spread 350 bp, spread duration 3.6) had a market-value DTS of $60 \times 0.96 \times 3.6 \times 350 \approx 72{,}600$; \$60 million of Halvorsen at 90 (600 bp, 3.4) had $60 \times 0.90 \times 3.4 \times 600 \approx 110{,}200$. A pair matched on face would have been short half a leg of market from day one. The full version, including why a dollar-neutral spread sort is secretly long beta, is §9.2; the regression that proves the residual is really residual is §8.8.

The reason the hedge has gone stale is that DTS is not a constant. It is a product of things that move, and the idiosyncratic move Dov was paid for has itself changed the ruler. Figure 1-B.2 shows the drift, leg by leg.

> **The Pair.** *Pair Book entry 1B-1, Dov Adler's pod (fictional composite; Kestrel Roofing is invented; Halvorsen is the book's fictional issuer).* **Thesis.** Two single-B building-products credits on the same housing cycle; Kestrel refinanced its revolver early and has room under its covenants, Halvorsen has a 2027 revolver maturity, a sponsor with a history of aggressive documents and leverage drifting up. **Legs.** Long \$60mm Kestrel 6% 2030; short \$39mm Halvorsen 7.25% 2029, the bonds borrowed through the platform's prime broker. **What the hedge removes.** High-yield spread beta, by matching market-value DTS at inception ($\approx 72{,}600$ against $39 \times 0.90 \times 3.4 \times 600 \approx 71{,}600$); most of the building-products sector factor, since both legs load on it; and most of the rate risk, since both are fixed-rate with similar durations. **What idio remains.** Kestrel's credit against Halvorsen's, which is the view: documents, maturity walls, sponsor behaviour. **What leaks.** First, the ruler. On Tuesday morning Kestrel is 97 (335 bp, 3.6) and Halvorsen is 78 (about 1,400 bp, spread duration down to 2.3 as the price fell), so the legs carry $60 \times 0.97 \times 3.6 \times 335 \approx 70{,}200$ against $39 \times 0.78 \times 2.3 \times 1{,}400 \approx 98{,}000$. The pair is now net short about 40% of a leg of market. Second, the jump. Halvorsen's next move is unlikely to be a diffusion: if the lender call ends in an exchange offered to all unsecured holders at a premium, the notes could gap up ten points, and $39 \times 10/100 = \$3.9$mm is half of what is left before Dov's line. No DTS ratio hedges that; it is a bet on the shape of a liability-management transaction, which is not the bet he wrote down in April. Third, the borrow. The bonds are on loan from an asset manager that can recall them at a day's notice, and holders who want a vote in a consent or an exchange tend to want their bonds back exactly when the short most wants to keep them. **What it costs to carry and to short.** The long earns 6% on \$60mm, \$3.6mm a year; the short pays Halvorsen's 7.25% coupon on \$39mm, about \$2.83mm a year, to whoever lent the bonds; financing the long net of the rebate on the short's proceeds costs about \$0.6mm a year at these sizes. In April the borrow fee was 50 bp on the short's market value, about \$0.18mm, and the pair carried roughly flat. This week the fee has been reset to 300 bp, about \$0.91mm a year, and the pair now costs close to \$3,000 a business day to hold *(all figures illustrative)*. The whole change is the borrow fee, which is the market's way of saying that a lot of people now want to be short Halvorsen. The pair's mechanics have a canonical home in Chapter 8; the borrow and the CDS alternative are Chapter 24's; the explain of a pair, line by line, is Chapter 8-C's.

At 9:30 the x-ray is interrupted by something more boring, which on a platform is how it usually goes.

The book holds a CDX HY position, a small one, bought months ago to soak up the beta that Dov's dozen pairs did not quite cancel. The index rolls twice a year, around the 20th of March and of September, into a new series with a refreshed list of names and a maturity six months longer, and this is roll week. Dov rolled the position on Friday afternoon: out of the old series, into the new one, in the platform's order-management system. The clearing broker, the futures commission merchant through which the platform clears its index trades, booked the same roll on Monday. So on Monday night the FCM computed variation margin on an old-series position that Dov's book believes it no longer has, while the book marked a new-series position that the FCM believes it has only just acquired. The two series moved differently on Monday, as old and new series do, and the gap between the margin call and the book's mark is about \$140,000 *(illustrative)*.

In a bank this would be an operations matter until month-end. In a pod it is an unexplained line on the morning explain, and an unexplained line on a pod's explain is a phone call. Dov's operations contact finds it by 11:00, writes it up in four sentences, and attaches it to a ticket titled *auto-sync roll dates between OMS and FCM*. The ticket is fourteen months old. It is, in its way, the platform's most reliable time series: it rises by exactly one day per day.

At 1:15 an email arrives from the prime broker's securities-lending desk. It is polite and has no adjectives. The lender of the Halvorsen bonds has asked whether the bonds can be returned "in the event of a corporate action." Nothing has been recalled. Nothing has been announced. But a short in a bond whose lender is already asking about corporate actions is a short whose exit door has started to stick.

Dov's analyst, Bea Castellanos, has spent the morning in Halvorsen's credit agreement and indenture, which she downloaded in April and has annotated in the margins like a bad novel. Her reading is that the documents leave room for an exchange that rewards whoever participates, and that the company's advisers have every reason to offer it to a group large enough to vote, not to everyone. If it goes to a group, the bonds left outside could fall further, and the short would make money. If it goes to everyone at a premium, the short would gap against him. "So we're not short a deteriorating credit any more," she says. "We're short a guess about who gets invited."

"That's not the trade," Dov says.

"No. It's a different trade wearing the old trade's name."

At 2:40 he does the thing he has been circling since 7:15. He puts \$4 million of the Halvorsen short out for an offer on an all-to-all RFQ, takes 78.75 from someone who is happy to sell Halvorsen to anyone on a lender-call Tuesday, and books the gain on those bonds. The arithmetic comes out tidier than he deserves. Cutting the short to \$35 million brings its DTS down to about $35 \times 0.78 \times 2.3 \times 1{,}400 \approx 87{,}900$, still heavier than the long, so he pairs it with a small sale of CDX HY protection, the cheapest and most liquid way to add back a little market, and the x-ray's orange line goes grey. He keeps most of the short, because most of the view is intact. He has made it smaller, because the part that changed is the part he cannot hedge.

At 3:00 the platform's risk manager calls. His name is Gordon Pike, he has been doing this job for longer than Dov has been a PM, and he opens with the sentence Dov expected: "I see you covered at 2:40."

Paleologo's description of the risk manager's job on *Odd Lots* is three jobs. There are the stop-losses. There is diversification across the whole platform, because small tilts that look harmless in one book add up: "you sum this across 300 PMs, it becomes a big factor exposure." And there is "making sure that people stay in scope." Gordon's call is about the third. "Your mandate is single-name high-yield relative value," he says. "Since Friday you've got a lender-call short, a CCC long that has traded with its equity for two months, and an index position that started life as a hedge. I want to know which of those is the strategy."

Dov has his answer ready because he spent the afternoon writing it. The CCC long is going on the Thursday reduction list; the index sale is sized to the DTS gap and no larger; the Halvorsen short is smaller and will be reviewed if the company files anything, or if the bonds are recalled. Gordon writes it down. The call lasts nine minutes, which on a platform is a good call. What makes it good is the order of events: Dov moved at 2:40 and the platform called at 3:00, and both of them noticed. A stop is a line a PM manages toward. The PM who meets it by surprise usually meets it twice.

> **Math, Gently.** *Why a PM is long a call option, and what the stop buys back.* Paleologo, on the same episode: "a PM is a little bit like somebody who's holding a call option," and the stop-loss is "a sort of a primitive tail insurance." Here is the arithmetic behind the image. Suppose a PM is paid 15% of positive annual P&L and nothing when it is negative *(illustrative; payouts vary by platform)*. Offer the PM two strategies with the same expected P&L of zero. Strategy A makes or loses \$10mm with equal odds; strategy B makes or loses \$30mm. The PM's expected pay is $0.5 \times 0.15 \times 10 = \$0.75$mm under A and $0.5 \times 0.15 \times 30 = \$2.25$mm under B. Same expected P&L, three times the pay, because the PM keeps a share of the upside and hands the downside to the platform. That is a call option, and like every call option its value rises with volatility. Now look at the platform, which receives the other 85% of the upside and all of the downside. Under B, without a stop, its expected P&L is $0.5 \times 25.5 - 0.5 \times 30 = -\$2.25$mm: it has quietly written the PM's option. Add a stop that cuts the book at a \$24mm loss, and in this two-outcome sketch the platform's expected P&L becomes $0.5 \times 25.5 - 0.5 \times 24 = +\$0.75$mm. The stop is the platform buying back part of the variance it gave away. Two caveats do all the work in real life. A stop only truncates losses that arrive gradually; a ten-point gap in a lender-call bond jumps straight through it, which is why Dov cares about Halvorsen's jump more than its beta. And a real stop also kills some paths that would have recovered, which is the premium on the insurance. The full version, with the two-threshold stop priced as an option, is §28.6; the payoff picture itself is Chapter 6-B's.

The reported thresholds that people quote about pods come from one firm, and from the press rather than the firm. eFinancialCareers, citing the *Wall Street Journal*, reported on 5 December 2025 that Millennium PMs "who lose 5% of the capital allocated to them typically have their capital halved. If they lose another 2.5%, they're usually fired"; Millennium declined to comment. Hedgeweek reported a similar shape on 10 March 2025, adding that "exceptions can be made." Dov's platform is not that firm, and his line is his own, but every PM on every platform carries some version of the number around in their head, the way restructuring bankers carry the next coupon date.

From 3:30 to 5:00 nothing happens. Dov reads Kestrel's latest 10-Q, all of it, including the paragraph about warranty reserves that nobody reads, and a sell-side note on building-products inventories with a chart he will think about on Thursday. He does not trade. The platform pays him for the part of his P&L it cannot buy, and some days the most valuable thing he can produce is an afternoon in which the book's residual is left alone to be residual. At 5:10 the x-ray refreshes. The line is grey. The coffee machine is still being looked into.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The multi-strategy relative-value pod (a generic seat; no firm named).* To this seat Halvorsen at 78 is not a price; it is a residual after the CDX HY beta has been stripped out, and the only question is whether 78 is cheap or rich *to the hedge*. The pod does not need Halvorsen to be a good credit. It needs Halvorsen to be mispriced against something it can short or buy against it, and it needs the trade to fit the mandate this week, which after a lender call is a separate question. The seat's whole economics rest on that separation. Rich Falk-Wallace, a former pod PM, put it on *Odd Lots* ("How the Hottest Hedge Funds on Wall Street Really Manage Risk," 25 Jul 2024): "what you're levering is just that residual term." Leverage on beta would be leverage on the thing the platform can buy for a few basis points. Leverage on the residual is leverage on the PM. That is why Dov's x-ray matters more to him than Friday's P&L, and why the pod's view of any bond starts with the hedge and ends with the documents. Chapter 4-C, §4C.3, gives the seat its full anatomy on a different Halvorsen week.

## 1B.3 Wednesday. The Systematic Asset Manager: "The Dataset Is Exhaust"

*Fictional composite: a systematic long-only credit team inside a large asset manager. The working habits are drawn from a published conversation with the co-heads of credit at Man Numeric, from FINRA's reporting rule and from an SEC order; the shop is not Man Group and not Two Sigma, and Ngozi Achebe-Lund, her colleagues and every number without a source are invented.*

The amber line from 6:10 is still there at eight, as promised. *As/of volume above threshold.* Ngozi reads it at her desk with the first coffee and translates it, as she does every morning, from the language of the load log into the language of the week.

TRACE is the public record of other people's trades. A dealer that trades a corporate bond reports it "as soon as practicable, but no later than within 15 minutes of the Time of Execution" (FINRA Rule 6730), and the print appears on the tape. What the tape does next is the part nobody puts in the brochure. Dealers report late, and the late reports arrive flagged *as/of*, carrying a trade date in the past. Dealers report wrongly, and the corrections arrive as cancels of the original print followed by a new one. Large trades are disseminated with their size capped, so a \$2 million trade in a high-yield bond appears to the public as "1MM+". Ngozi's team does not trade off the tape so much as off its own cleaned copy of the tape, which is rebuilt every night from the raw feed and which therefore changes its mind about the past every night as well. Monday's prints arrive, in part, as Wednesday's corrections. A week of cleaning TRACE is not a week; it is a standing condition.

This week the condition has a complication, which is earnings. Paul Kamenski, co-head of credit at Man Numeric, described it in Man's "Extra Credit: Algorithmic Advantage" conversation (recorded March 2024): "a supplier company reporting before a customer company," and then "multiply that by hundreds of issuers as reporting." The supplier's quarter moves the customer's credit signal before the customer has said anything, and the customer's quarter then moves it again. So in earnings weeks Ngozi's signal refresh runs twice a day instead of once, and the exception queue, the list of prints the cleaning filters were not sure about, runs twice as long.

At 11:00 there is the fortnightly model review. Five people, one screen, a PM named Arjun Mehta who asks one question per meeting and has learned to make it the right one. Today's question is the oldest question in systematic investing. "Halvorsen. Forty-first this morning, two hundred and sixth yesterday. Nothing happened to it. Why did it move a hundred and sixty-five places?"

"Something happened to it," Ngozi says. "On Friday. It's the lender-call name."

"Something happened on Friday. Yesterday's rank already knew about Friday. What happened between yesterday and today?"

It takes her until 1:30 to find out, and the answer is that nothing happened to Halvorsen at all. Something happened to the tape. On Monday afternoon a dealer reported a small trade in the 7.25s at 71. On Tuesday evening the same dealer cancelled it and re-reported it as/of at 77; the 71 had been a keying error, the kind of thing a fat-finger band would have caught if the print had been a quote rather than a report. Tuesday morning's model run saw the 71. Wednesday morning's run saw the 77. Nothing in the model changed. The data changed, backward.

> **Math, Gently.** *A return is a ratio, and one of its prices can be retracted.* A daily return is today's price over yesterday's, minus one: $r_t = P_t / P_{t-1} - 1$. Halvorsen closed Friday at 78. On Tuesday morning the tape said Monday's last print was 71, so Monday's return was $71/78 - 1 = -9.0\%$, and when Tuesday's bonds changed hands near 77.50, Tuesday's return was $77.50/71 - 1 = +9.2\%$. One wrong price produced two wrong returns, of opposite sign, in consecutive days, which is a fake reversal: precisely the pattern a short-term reversal signal is built to buy. On Wednesday morning, with the 71 cancelled and replaced by 77, the same two days read $77/78 - 1 = -1.3\%$ and $77.50/77 - 1 = +0.6\%$. The rank moved because a numerator moved. Now imagine a back-test run next spring on the corrected tape. It will see $-1.3\%$ and conclude that on Tuesday the model ranked Halvorsen on a mild decline. It did not; it ranked it on a 9% crash. A shop that keeps only the corrected tape cannot reproduce its own decisions, and a shop that keeps only the raw tape cannot measure what actually happened. So the archive keeps two clocks for every print: when the trade happened, and when the shop learned the version it used. The full version, with the cleaning filters and the four timestamps of a fill, is Chapter 9-B and §27.2; the reason bond-return research is fragile for exactly this reason is Chapter 7.

Arjun listens, nods and asks the second question, which is the one he actually came for. "So which rank do we trade on?"

"Today's. It's built on the better tape." Ngozi pauses. "But yesterday's is the one we'd have traded on, so it's the one the back-test is owed. Monday's portfolio did nothing with Halvorsen because of a typo, and we're going to remember that it did."

At 2:00 the second scene of the day arrives in the form of a researcher named Saoirse Quinn, who has found the filter that let the 71 through. It flags a print as suspect when it deviates from the bond's recent median by more than a fixed number of points, and the number was widened to ten last spring, after a month in which genuine ten-point moves in stressed bonds kept being thrown out as errors. For a bond that has just fallen five points on real news, a seven-point typo looks like more news. Saoirse's proposal is reasonable, quick and exactly the wrong process: "Can I just fix the parameter in prod? It's one number."

The ticket for the proper fix, a threshold scaled by each bond's own volatility plus a check for prints that jump and snap back the next day, has been open since April. Ngozi has commented on it four times. She says no to the one-number fix anyway, and the reason she gives is not a regulation but a story the industry learned from someone else's settlement.

> **Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality.** *Myth:* a quant shop's week is research. *Reality:* a quant shop's week is data custody and change control, with research fitted into the gaps. The public record has a measure of what happens when the change control fails. In January 2025 the SEC announced that Two Sigma would pay a \$90 million penalty and had repaid \$165 million to affected funds and accounts. Employees had found vulnerabilities in models "in or before March 2019"; the firm "waited until August 2023" to fix them; and it "failed to supervise one of its employees who made unauthorized changes to more than a dozen models" (SEC Press Release 2025-15, 16 Jan 2025). The bank regulators' model-risk guidance, which binds banks rather than asset managers but which most large managers have adopted in spirit, describes the opposite habit in one clause: "change control procedures ... all changes are logged and can be audited" (SR 11-7, 4 Apr 2011, p.12). A parameter changed in production by the person who found the problem is a fix with no witness. It might be the right number. Nobody will ever be able to show that it was. §26.8 carries the guidance into the age of model-writing agents.

So the parameter is not fixed in prod. Saoirse writes the change as a pull request, with the Halvorsen case attached as a test, and it goes to Thursday's review. The April ticket gains a fifth comment and a link. Its age, in days, rises by one.

At 2:30 there is a trade to do, and it is not Halvorsen. The rebalance wants to sell \$20 million of a double-B industrial whose rank has slid for three weeks. The head trader, Luis Ortega, has three ways to do it. He can show the whole block to one dealer and get it done in a phone call, at a price that will include the dealer's view of why a systematic manager is selling \$20 million of anything. He can slice it into clips and send them out by RFQ over two days. Or he can fold part of it into the month-end portfolio trade, where it will be one line in a basket of three hundred and its information will be diluted by the other two hundred and ninety-nine.

Robert Lam, Kamenski's co-head, described the same choice in the same Man conversation. Trading a large block through one dealer "might not be the optimal way"; the alternative is "to slice it" and route each piece to "the best venue and the best protocol." Kamenski added that execution includes "venue selection" and "deciding whether you want an order to be high touch or low touch," and that there are "circumstances where the best course of action is to trade voice." Man's own published account, in *The Revolutions in Credit Trading* (Jun 2023), put more than 90% of its credit order flow through electronic venues as of 1 May 2023, the "vast majority" of it by RFQ. Luis does the boring version of the sophisticated thing. He slices \$15 million into clips of \$2 to \$3 million over two days and keeps \$5 million for the portfolio trade. Nobody will thank him for the cost he did not pay. Like Priya's passed RFQ on Monday, it will show up only in a report that somebody chooses to read.

Pinned to the partition behind Luis's screen is a real job advertisement from another firm, printed out because a colleague found it funny that it described his week so exactly. The DWS Group posting for a New York credit trader (7 May 2026) asks for someone to "foster successful relationships and interactions with Operations, Technology, Compliance, Risk and Audit," to take part in a "liquidity dialogue," and to know "Bloomberg, MarketAxess and Tradeweb" and "Python, JavaScript, SQL." It does not mention research.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The systematic credit manager, in public figures.* To this seat Halvorsen is a ranked name with a transaction-cost estimate and a probability of fill. It trades in small clips by RFQ and simply leaves the name alone when the cost model or the liquidity score says the trade is not worth its friction, which this week it does. The seat is the one an ex-banker most expects to be large, and it is the smallest room in this chapter. Systematic strategies are about 20% of global equities but under 2% of the investable fixed-income universe (Brown Brothers Harriman, citing Bloomberg, 2 Feb 2026). At Man Group, whose systematic credit team is the most public in the business, systematic long-only credit sat roughly flat at about \$2.8–3.1 billion between March 2025 and September 2026, while the firm's discretionary long-only credit almost doubled, from \$19.8 billion to \$37.5 billion (Man Group trading statements). The room is small; the exhaust it breathes is everybody's. Chapter 4-C, §4C.2, sits in this seat for a whole Monday.

> **Math, Gently.** *How a tolerance in basis points becomes dollars.* CS01 is the number of dollars a position gains or loses when its credit spread moves one basis point. For a bond it is roughly face $\times$ price/100 $\times$ spread duration $\times$ 0.0001: Hal's \$6mm of Halvorsen at 78, with a spread duration of 2.3, has a CS01 of about $6{,}000{,}000 \times 0.78 \times 2.3 \times 0.0001 \approx \$1{,}080$ per basis point. Marking at 79 instead of the 77.75 mid is 1.25 points, $1.25 \times 6{,}000{,}000/100 = \$75{,}000$, or about 70 bp of spread on this bond. For an index position the arithmetic is the same, and the stakes scale. In 2012 the bank's valuation group let the investment-grade index mark "vary from the midpoint ... by up to 6 basis points" (Senate PSI report, p.112, n.675). On a position with a CS01 of \$50,000 per bp *(illustrative)*, 6 bp is \$300,000 of P&L that depends entirely on who is holding the pen. Summed across a large book, the report records the Controller's conclusion that the book's value "could legitimately fall anywhere between \$719 million and \$1.2 billion" (p.98). A tolerance is a budget for honest disagreement, and the budget can be spent dishonestly one basis point at a time. The full version, with the price-sourcing hierarchy and the true-up, is Chapter 8-C (§8C.4) and the limit sheet in §8B.9.

The rest of the Senate story is what two prices for one position do when a counterparty notices. In 2012 the bank's two units "assigned different values to identical credit derivative holdings"; counterparties objected, collateral disputes peaked at \$690 million, and a senior risk officer resolved them by requiring the first unit to mark as the second did, off "an independent pricing service" (p.6). Mei is not trying to prevent a \$690 million dispute. She is trying to prevent the first email of one. The senior trader grumbles, at length and accurately, that the vendor is behind the market. Hal remarks the bonds at 77.75, the shadow column becomes the official one, and the desk's P&L for the day drops by \$75,000 that it did not really have.

## 1B.5 Friday. The Distressed Fund: "Information Has a Legal State"

*Fictional composite: a distressed-debt fund. The mechanics of confidentiality, restriction and big-boy letters follow Stephen Moyer's* Distressed Debt Analysis *(printed pp.62–64); the sourcing remarks are from a published podcast transcript. Sol Ferreira, his fund, its general counsel and its positions are invented.*

The cell is still green at 6:45. By 11:00 it will be red, and Sol knows it, because on Sunday night Halvorsen's advisers asked his fund to join a group of unsecured noteholders and on Thursday his PM said yes. The fund owns \$25 million of the 7.25s, bought at 88 in the spring *(illustrative)*, so the decision was less about whether to be in the room than about how long to stay out of it.

Moyer's book describes the architecture. Joining a bondholder group that will receive the company's private information means signing a confidentiality agreement, "generally referred to as signing 'confi'," which "can result in the holder becoming 'restricted'" (p.62). To "maximize the time period members remain unrestricted, the receipt and analysis of confidential information initially can be limited to the financial advisors and counsel" (p.62). And once members receive the information they "cannot become unrestricted until the information becomes public, usually through the filing of a Form 8-K, 10-Q, or 10-K, or the information becomes nonmaterial" (p.63). The group's advisers have had Halvorsen's numbers since Tuesday. This morning the company wants the members themselves to see a cash-flow forecast before it proposes anything, and so the members are coming over the wall.

At 10:00 the fund's general counsel holds a call to decide who goes. Sol goes; the PM goes; the trader stays outside and so does everyone else. The general counsel reads the confi aloud in the voice of someone who has read many, and at 10:40 Sol signs. At 10:52 the restricted-list cell for Halvorsen turns red for every security the company has issued, not only the notes. That catches the fund's loan sleeve, which had a standing order to sell \$3 million of Halvorsen's term loan, a position nobody on the call was thinking about. The order can still be done, but now only with a "big-boy letter," which Moyer describes as an acknowledgment by the other side that it knows the seller holds nonpublic information and a waiver of the claims it might otherwise bring, its execution "typically arranged by a broker-dealer intermediary" (p.63). The trader cancels the order instead. Nobody wants to sell a term loan in a lender-call week by announcing that they know something.

A systematic desk would give a great deal for a clean, dated, reasoned record of eighteen passes per two trades. §3.5 calls that record the Edge Ledger.

At 1:00 an email arrives from someone Sol met on a conference panel: Kaz Lind, the model builder on a systematic credit desk across town. Their liquidity score for Halvorsen fell sharply on Thursday and Friday, they write, and nothing in the public record explains it. Does Sol have a view? And, in a postscript, Kaz has heard the phrase "big-boy letter" this week from a distressed PM named Mags and would like to know whether there is a small-boy letter, and which one applies to a fund under a billion dollars.

Sol forwards the email to the general counsel without reading the postscript twice. The general counsel replies to Kaz, from the compliance mailbox, with the fund's standard sentence: *we do not comment on any issuer or security.* Kaz reads it, thinks about it, and writes back to their own desk that Sol's fund is probably restricted on Halvorsen. Then they look at the sentence again and delete the message, because they have just noticed what the general counsel already knew.

> **Math, Gently.** *Conditional probability, told through a silence.* A conditional probability is the chance of one thing given that you have seen another, written $\mathbb{P}(A \mid B)$. Kaz's question is: given that Halvorsen's best-informed holders have gone quiet, how likely is it that they are restricted in private talks? Suppose, across 1,000 high-yield issuers in a year, 50 are in private talks with a holder group *(illustrative)*. In 40 of those 50, the biggest holders stop trading. Among the 950 issuers not in talks, holders go quiet anyway in 95 cases, for holidays, redemptions or boredom. A two-by-two table does the rest. There are $40 + 95 = 135$ quiet names, of which 40 are in talks, so
>
> $$\mathbb{P}(\text{talks} \mid \text{quiet}) = \frac{40}{40 + 95} \approx 30\%.$$
>
> The prior was 5%; the silence multiplied it by six. Now take the compliance reply. If the fund sends the identical sentence about every issuer, restricted or not, then the chance of receiving it is 100% in talks and 100% out of talks, the ratio of the two is one, and the posterior equals the prior. The general counsel's boring sentence is boring on purpose: a reply that is the same in every state of the world carries no information about which state you are in. A fund that answered "no comment" only about restricted names would be publishing its restricted list one email at a time. The full version, with the base-rate trap that catches nearly everyone, is §3.4 and §3.4.3; the sequential detector built on silences is §3.6.2.

Kaz's last email of the day, to their own desk, proposes modelling "restricted" as Gaussian. It is not answered.

From 2:00 to 5:00 nothing trades in Halvorsen at Sol's fund, because nobody who knows anything may trade it, and the people who know nothing have been told not to ask. Sol reads the cash-flow forecast twice and builds his own version of it on a tab he has kept since his first job, a week-by-week runway of cash against the next interest payment. At 3:15 Mags calls him about something else and asks, without asking, how his Friday is going. "Back in my day," she says, "the restricted list was a Post-it on the Bloomberg and the confi was a handshake." A pause. "And that was worse. And I miss it."

The 8-K that would cleanse everyone is expected after the close. At 4:30 there is nothing on EDGAR. At 5:00 there is nothing. At 5:45 the Ad Hoc Group speaks for the only time this week, in unison and through counsel, to ask whether anyone has seen the 8-K. Nobody has. The fund will stay restricted over the weekend, and Sol, who has watched Halvorsen since 88, will spend two days unable to do anything about a bond he now understands better than anyone who can trade it.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The opportunistic buyer, in its own published words.* To the distressed seat Halvorsen is a document first, a price second and a possible restricted interval third, and the interval is part of the price: a fund that joins the group trades liquidity for information, and the trade is priced in weeks of being unable to sell. The other side of that trade is the buyer with patient capital and no obligation to be anywhere in particular. The cleanest public account of the seat comes from structured credit rather than corporate distress. D. E. Shaw's research note *Assessing U.S. Mortgage-Backed Credit Stress in 2020* reports that over March and April 2020 "we purchased in excess of \$400 million in CAS and STACR bonds at a significant discount to par," from sellers who "were put under stress by redemption requests and margin calls." Wednesday's systematic seat sees a liquidity score falling; this seat sees a forced seller's discount and the possibility that somebody else's constraint is about to become its week. Chapter 4-C walks the full set of seats; Chapter 12-B gives the restructuring itself its state machine.

## 1B.7 The Week Log

### Specification for AI Implementation

*The problem it solves, at this point in the story.* Every room this week ran on clocks, and the clocks came in three kinds. Some were set by regulation: the fifteen-minute TRACE report, the quarterly best-execution review, the annual market-access certification. Some were sourced practice, routines that real firms have described in public: the support rota, the morning x-ray, the end-of-day reflection. And some were composite, invented for the story. Each event also had two times, the moment it happened and the moment the shop learned the version it acted on, which is what Monday's vendor file and Wednesday's cancelled print were both about. A desk that wants to learn from its own weeks needs those events in one table, with the clock type and the source attached, so that a later reader can tell a regulation from a habit from a story.

*What it computes, and why it is built this way.* The listing defines one immutable row type, `WeekEvent`, and two functions. Each row carries a logical `event_id` (a correction is a new row with the same id, not an edit), the shop type, a task class, the clock type, `occurred_at`, `known_at`, an optional interval end, an optional ticket-opened date and a `source`. The constructor refuses a regulation or sourced-practice row without a source, which is the chapter's labelling discipline enforced in code. `as_known` returns the latest version of an event that the shop knew at a given time, the bitemporal read of §27.2. `to_calendar_row` emits the row shape that Chapter 3-B's `desk_calendar.py` already ingests (event id, date, known-at date, source), so no new library is needed.

*Inputs and outputs.* In: events typed or parsed from tickets, load logs, compliance calendars and the restricted list. Out: validated rows, a point-in-time view of any event, ticket ages in days, and calendar rows for 3-B's module.

*When to use it, and when not.* It is a record of what happened and when it was known, useful for post-mortems, for the week-in-review, and for back-tests that want to know what the desk knew. It is not a compliance system and it does not decide whether anyone may trade; the restricted list stays the authority for that.

*The edge case that bites.* Two versions stamped with the same `known_at`, as when a cancel and its re-report arrive in the same minute. The listing breaks the tie by order of arrival in the list, which is arbitrary; a production version carries a sequence number from the feed. A second, gentler trap: a restricted interval whose end is unknown. The listing stores it as an open interval, with no end, because "next 8-K" is not a date.

## 1B.8 Desk Debrief

The week's ideas, in the order the rooms taught them. A point-in-time archive is a back-test's alibi: Priya could open on Friday's original file only because her firm had saved it with the minute it arrived. A trade not done still has a markout, and a desk that logs only its fills is grading itself on the hands it chose to play. A hedge ratio built on DTS is right for the bonds a pair used to hold; when the idiosyncratic view pays, the ratio drifts, and a pair left alone turns quietly into a market position, while the jump it was not designed to hedge becomes the risk that matters. A return is a ratio of two prices, either of which the tape can retract, and one bad print makes two fake returns of opposite sign. A mark is a choice inside the bid-ask, and a tolerance is a budget for honest disagreement that can be spent one basis point at a time. Information has a legal state with timestamps, and even a silence carries a likelihood ratio unless someone has made it carry none. Underneath all of it sits one job, the custody of a number, and the one figure that rises reliably every week is the age of the ticket to automate the thing everyone does by hand.

### The Spec You Hand the Robot

*Vague version:* "log what happens each week." *What ROBO built from it:* a table with one column, `what_happened`, populated from every Bloomberg chat on the floor, including Thursday's discussion of where to get lunch, each row timestamped with the moment ROBO read it rather than the moment it happened. ROBO did exactly what was asked. *The precise version:* one immutable row per event version with `event_id`, `shop`, `task_class`, `clock_type` in {regulation, sourced practice, composite}, `occurred_at`, `known_at`, optional `ends_at` (open intervals allowed), optional `ticket_opened`, optional `instrument_id` (a surrogate key, not a CUSIP), and a `source` that is mandatory unless the row is composite; corrections as new rows, not edits; a point-in-time read by `known_at` with a feed sequence number to break ties; export rows in the shape Chapter 3-B's `desk_calendar.py` ingests.

### Exercises

**1. The river on a passed trade.** Priya's engine passes on an RFQ to buy \$3mm of a bond that trades at 80 with someone else; an hour later the composite is 81.50. What is the markout of the trade the engine did not do, and what would you want to see before calling the pass a mistake? *Solution.* Had it bought at 80, the markout would be $(81.50 - 80) \times 3{,}000{,}000/100 = +\$45{,}000$, so the pass cost \$45,000 of opportunity. One pass proves little: the market may have rallied, so the beta-adjusted (IDO) markout of §3.3.3 is the fairer number, and a month of passes set against a month of fills is the real test (§3.3.5).

**2. A pair that keeps working.** Suppose Kestrel's leg stays at a market-value DTS of about 70,200 and Halvorsen falls to 70, with a spread of 1,800 bp and a spread duration of 2.0. What face amount of Halvorsen short keeps the pair DTS-matched? *Solution.* Solve $N \times 0.70 \times 2.0 \times 1{,}800 = 70{,}200$, so $N \approx \$27.9$mm, down from \$39mm. As the short leg's view pays, its DTS rises and the matched size shrinks; an unrebalanced pair becomes a market short. The rematch does nothing about the jump risk of an exchange offer.

**3. The PM's option.** A platform pays 20% of positive P&L. Strategy A makes or loses \$5mm with equal odds; strategy B makes or loses \$20mm. What is the PM's expected pay under each, and the platform's expected P&L under B with and without a stop at a \$12mm loss? *Solution.* PM: $0.5 \times 0.2 \times 5 = \$0.5$mm and $0.5 \times 0.2 \times 20 = \$2.0$mm. Platform under B without a stop: $0.5 \times 16 - 0.5 \times 20 = -\$2.0$mm; with the stop: $0.5 \times 16 - 0.5 \times 12 = +\$2.0$mm, provided the loss arrives gradually enough for the stop to bind.

**4. One bad print.** A bond closes Friday at 95. Monday's last print is reported at 88, Tuesday's at 94.50; Tuesday evening the Monday print is corrected to 94.80. What are Monday's and Tuesday's returns before and after the correction, and which pair belongs in a back-test? *Solution.* Before: $88/95 - 1 = -7.4\%$ and $94.50/88 - 1 = +7.4\%$, a fake reversal. After: $94.80/95 - 1 = -0.2\%$ and $94.50/94.80 - 1 = -0.3\%$. A back-test that reproduces decisions uses the as-known version; a performance measurement uses the corrected one; the archive keeps both (§27.2).

**5. Leaning inside the tolerance.** A desk holds twelve high-yield bonds, \$40mm of face in total, and the valuation group's tolerance is 0.75 points. How much P&L can be moved without breaching a single tolerance, and why does the valuation group look at the signs of the deviations as well as their sizes? *Solution.* Up to $0.75 \times 40{,}000{,}000/100 = \$300{,}000$ if every mark leans the same way. Honest marking errors fall on either side of mid; all twelve on the favourable side has probability $0.5^{12} \approx 0.02\%$ if the errors are honest coin flips, which is a much louder alarm than any single breach.

**6. Kaz's small-boy letter.** Suppose the fund's general counsel used the standard "no comment" reply 90% of the time for restricted names and 60% of the time otherwise. With a 5% prior that a name is in private talks, what does receiving the reply do to the probability? *Solution.* The likelihood ratio is $0.9/0.6 = 1.5$. Prior odds $0.05/0.95 \approx 0.053$ become $0.079$, a posterior of about 7.3%. A reply used for every name, in every state, has a ratio of one and moves nothing; there is, alas, no small-boy letter.
