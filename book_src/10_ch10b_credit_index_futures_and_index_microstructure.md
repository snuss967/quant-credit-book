## Cold Open: Four Pots

At 2:47 on Thursday 1 October 2026, ten days after the September roll, a chat window opened on Theo Marsh's second screen with a request he had not seen before. *IBHY, front month, client buys, 160 lots, block. Can you show?*

He did the conversion first, because it was the only part he was sure of. Cboe's iBoxx high-yield future is \$1,000 times the index, so at a level near 185 one contract is about \$185,000 of high-yield exposure and 160 contracts are about \$30 million *(illustrative level)*. The client, an outsourced investment office for a mid-sized pension, had added a line to the chat that Theo read twice: *we have no ISDA and want none.*

Then he looked for the market, and found four.

On the first screen was CDX HY, the new series that had started trading on Monday 21 September, at a price of about 106.9, which the standard model turned into a spread near 335 bp. On the second was the IBHY order book itself, front month at 185.42 bid, thin. On the third was HYG, the iShares high-yield ETF, at \$80.62, and the fund's own page put its portfolio's option-adjusted spread near 285 bp. On the fourth was last night's close for the Bloomberg US High Yield Very Liquid index, the one CME's and Eurex's high-yield futures follow, with an OAS near 255 bp *(all four illustrative)*.

Three spreads, eighty basis points apart, and a fourth price that was not a spread at all. Theo wrote them on a pad in a column, which is what bankers do when numbers disagree, and the column did not help.

Ines Okafor rolled her chair over with the coffee she had been nursing since lunch. She looked at the pad and then at the chat.

"What's the pot?"

"Thirty million."

"Four pots," she said, tapping the column. "CDX is a hundred names in five-year CDS, equal-weighted, nobody's bonds. HYG is about thirteen hundred bonds weighted by size, in a wrapper that trades at a premium or a discount to its own NAV. The Very Liquid index is a thousand-odd bonds that passed somebody's liquidity screen, with a cap on each issuer. And the future you're being asked to sell settles to an index built from HYG's holdings, at one evaluated close on the first business day of the month. None of them is the market. Which one are you being asked to make?"

Theo had spent eight years at PJT thinking of a price as a claim on a waterfall: who gets paid, in what order, from what assets, under which document. He saw where she was going and did not like it. "So the price is a claim on..."

"A rule," Ines said. "Somebody's rule. Read it before you quote it."

He quoted it as what it was, an ETF portfolio with a settlement date, hedged in HYG, leaning on the close, and showed 185.55. The client paid it at 2:58. The fill made the desk about \$20,000 if the hedge behaved *(illustrative)*, and it raised the question this chapter answers: **which rulebook is this price a claim on, and what does that rulebook do on the days you most need it?**

By the end of this chapter you will be able to explain what a listed credit future is and why a futures-only fund wants one; tell the four index families apart and say what each one's design does to tracking and to forced flow; read volume, open interest, block share and turnover as a diagnostic, and say when volume has been bought; follow a large ticket into the block and basis-at-the-close machinery; build a positioning feature from the CFTC's weekly file without trading on a reclassification; price a CDX roll as a series basis and an index against its constituents as a skew; explain why a client pays more than a dealer for the same index risk; set a pod's index hedge in CDX or in futures and name what each leaks; and read the futures' capacity as a cost curve with a calendar.

## 10B.1 Three Doors Into One Room

*Quarry Brook Trend Partners is a fictional futures-only CTA in Connecticut. Its people, its documents and its numbers are invented.*

Quarry Brook runs about \$4 billion across 140 futures markets *(illustrative)*, and in April 2025 it found out what it did not own. During tariff week, the first days of April, every trend model on the floor turned short risk at once, and Gil Navarro, the firm's head of research, watched credit spreads gap wider on a terminal he could not trade from. Quarry Brook owned rates, equity indices, currencies and commodities. It owned no credit, because every way into credit it had looked at came with a document its compliance officer would not sign.

Gil spent the summer of 2025 trying the doors one at a time, and the order in which they closed is the best short course on why listed credit futures exist.

The first door was the ETF. Quarry Brook could short HYG through its prime broker like any stock, and for six weeks it did, in a small pilot. Then on a Wednesday afternoon the stock-loan desk called. A lender wanted some of the borrowed shares back; the desk could re-borrow most of them at a higher fee, and the rest would be bought in if nobody found more by Friday. Nothing had happened to high yield. Something had happened to the supply of borrowable HYG, which is a market of its own (§8D.2 has the borrow, §20.2 the wrapper). Gil wrote in the pilot's log that the firm was now running a credit signal whose position size was chosen partly by a lender it had never met.

The second door was CDX itself, the standard instrument for credit trend (§10.1.1), unfunded, cleared and deep: the ISDA SwapsInfo review for the second quarter of 2026 puts average daily volume at \$29.3bn in CDX IG and \$7.6bn in CDX HY, and 99.3% of both cleared. Ruth Abernathy, Quarry Brook's general counsel, priced the door in calendar time. An ISDA master agreement and credit support annex with each executing dealer, a clearing agreement with a futures commission merchant that clears swaps, onboarding to a swap execution facility, a swap-dealer relationship review, and new board-approved derivatives policies, because the fund's offering documents said "futures and forwards." She guessed eight weeks if everyone answered e-mail. It was not a no. It was a project, and Quarry Brook's operations team ran 140 markets with five people because every one of those markets used the same plumbing.

The third door was the one Gil had walked past twice: a listed future on a credit index. It cleared through the FCM the firm already used. It margined in the same account as its Treasury futures, and at CME it margined against them, with offsets the exchange computes automatically (CME's launch announcement, 23 April 2024, as reported by Finadium and The DESK). It needed no ISDA, no borrow to short and no new policy language, because it was a future. What it offered was not a better price. It was a different bundle of frictions.

That phrase, a bundle of frictions, is the idea the rest of the chapter keeps returning to. Credit now has three doors into the same room. You can assemble a basket of a thousand bonds, you can sign an ISDA and trade CDX, or you can buy a listed future. The room is roughly the same: an exposure to the spread of a broad set of corporate issuers. What differs is everything around it: whether a document must be negotiated, whether a lender can take the short away, whether the exposure is one leg or two (a total return swap is two, a future is one), whether the margin nets against the rest of the book, and what single print the contract settles to. A futures-only CTA does not care that CDX is thirty times deeper. It cares that CDX has a door it cannot open.

> **Math, Gently.** *What a futures price on a bond index is.* A credit index future is a promise about a number. On a stated date, the exchange will look up the index's official closing level, and the two sides will settle in cash the difference between that level and the price they agreed when they traded, times a multiplier. Nothing is delivered, and no bond changes hands. Cboe's IBHY contract is \$1,000 times the iBoxx index level, the minimum tick is 0.01 (\$10 a contract), and final settlement is at the index close on the first business day of the contract month (Cboe contract specifications). Between today and then, the exchange settles gains and losses in cash every evening, which is variation margin (Appendix D §D.2). Theo's 160 contracts at 185.42 are $160 \times \$1{,}000 \times 185.42 \approx \$29.7$ million of exposure; a fall of one full point in the level costs the holder $160 \times \$1{,}000 = \$160{,}000$, paid that night. There is no coupon leg. Whatever the bonds pay is handled inside the index's own arithmetic and reaches the holder only through the level. That is why the futures price is not the index level. Holding the bonds ties up cash; holding the future does not. So the future's fair price is roughly the index grown at the financing rate to the settlement date, less any income the index level leaves out: $F \approx I\,\big(1 + (r - q)\,T\big)$. For an index that reinvests its coupons, $q$ is zero. With the index at 180.00, three months to settlement and financing at 4%, $F \approx 180 \times (1 + 0.04 \times 0.25) = 181.80$. A trader who compares that future with the index and sees a 1.8-point "premium" has discovered the interest rate. The full margin plumbing is Appendix D §D.2, and §10B.8 turns the financing term into a hedge cost.

Quarry Brook did not discover anything new. Its arrival was forecast, in public, two years earlier. In May 2024 Susquehanna's Andreas McGrath described to The DESK how Eurex had approached the firm in 2021 to make markets in its euro investment-grade future, and how he expected the users to arrive in three phases: asset managers replacing long and short credit beta first, then CTAs, then the CVA and XVA desks of banks hedging under the new trading-book capital rules (The DESK, "The credit futures market," 28 May 2024). Read in October 2026, the first phase is plainly underway; the CFTC data in §10B.5 show asset managers as the large holders of CME's contracts. The second phase is Gil's, and the evidence for it is mostly firms like his asking the question. The third has no public evidence at all yet. A forecast from a market maker with quoting obligations is also a description of the order in which that market maker hopes to be paid, which does not make it wrong.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The futures-only CTA (a generic seat; Quarry Brook is fictional).* To this seat the question "is the future a good credit instrument?" is upside down. The trend model does not want credit for its own sake; it wants one more roughly independent market whose price trends, and it wants that market on the same plumbing as the other 140. A CDX contract that is cheaper by two basis points of bid-offer but needs eight weeks of documents and a new operational process loses to a future that is wider and thinner but settles like a Treasury contract. The seat also sees a risk that the credit specialist misses. Its exposure is to an index close it cannot observe being made, computed from evaluated bond prices, on a contract whose volume is partly paid for by the exchange (§10B.3). A CTA has lived through contracts that died, and it sizes a new one the way a careful guest eats at a new restaurant: a little, and watching the kitchen.

Gil's first allocation, in October 2025, was 2% of the trend book's risk. The memo justifying it did not mention price once. It listed the documents the firm did not have to sign.

## 10B.2 Four Exchanges, Four Index Philosophies

*Ines Okafor's desk, its model review and ROBO are fictional; the index families, their sizes and their launch dates are real and sourced.*

The Monday after the IBHY block, 5 October, the desk's model review had one item: a request Theo had sent ROBO on Thursday evening, while the block was still fresh. *Build a credit future tracking series so we can see how the future moves against what we hedge it with.*

ROBO had built four.

Kaz Lind had put them on the whiteboard as four columns, and stood beside them with the air of someone who had been handed a menu in a language they had studied but never spoken. ROBO had not misunderstood. There is no single credit future to track. There are four exchanges listing credit index futures in the United States and Europe, and each picked a different family of index to settle against, which means each sells a slightly different room through its door.

**Cboe** was first, listing IBHY and IBIG on its futures exchange in September and October 2018 (Cboe contract specifications; first prints in November 2018 in Cboe's daily volume file). Its indices are iBoxx benchmarks intersected with the actual holdings of HYG and LQD, so the future tracks the ETF's portfolio. In effect it is the ETF's basket with a settlement date and no premium or discount, since the index is computed from bond prices rather than from the ETF's trading price. Cboe added an emerging-markets sovereign contract, IEMD, in July 2024.

**CME** launched on 17 June 2024, alongside its Treasury complex, with futures on the Bloomberg US Corporate index (investment grade) and the Bloomberg US High Yield Very Liquid index. **Eurex**, which had listed euro investment-grade credit futures since the third quarter of 2021 and euro high yield since late 2022, added US dollar contracts on the same two Bloomberg indices in September 2024. Its deck gives the size of what those indices hold: the US Corporate index had 8,611 bonds from 1,147 issuers, \$7.3 trillion of debt, an OAS of 0.76% and a duration of 6.88; the HY Very Liquid index had 1,102 bonds from 632 issuers, \$1.0 trillion, an OAS of 2.48%, a duration of 2.89 and a 2% cap on any one issuer (Eurex, "Global Credit Index Futures" presentation, October 2026).

**ICE** came last, in December 2024, with four futures on MSCI MarketAxess Tradable Corporate Bond indices (US dollar and euro, investment grade and high yield), whose bonds are chosen from MSCI's broader indices by MarketAxess's Relative Liquidity Scores, with the stated aim of cutting tracking error (ICE press release, December 2024; The DESK, 13 December 2024). In July 2026 ICE agreed to buy MarketAxess for \$167 a share in cash (ICE Form 8-K, 30 July 2026), so the liquidity scores that pick the bonds in ICE's index belong to a company ICE has agreed to own.

Kaz read the third column twice. "Is 'Very Liquid' a defined term?"

"It's an index name," Theo said.

"So nobody can breach it. Liquidity is a state of the world, but 'Very Liquid' is a state of Bloomberg." Kaz underlined it. "Like 'Material Adverse Effect'. It means exactly what the document says and nothing you would guess."

That was, in fairness, correct. The phrase is defined only in Bloomberg's index methodology, by a set of size and age screens, and a bond that falls out of the screens leaves the index whatever the market thinks of its liquidity that day. It was the second time in a month Kaz had treated a piece of market vocabulary as a legal term, and the first time the instinct had been right.

The desk spent most of the hour on why the four columns matter, and the answer is that **index design is the microstructure**. Each design decides three things a trader feels directly.

The first is tracking. A future on the ETF's own holdings (Cboe) tracks the ETF's portfolio closely and inherits the ETF's choice of bonds; a future on a broad Bloomberg index tracks a wider and less liquid set, which is a better benchmark for an insurer's book and a worse hedge for an ETF trader. A liquidity-scored index (ICE) narrows the set again, by a rule that reacts to trading. None of the exchanges publishes an independent tracking study. CME's own note puts the annualized tracking error of its contracts against their indices between roughly 1.6% and 2.5%, and those are the exchange's numbers, unaudited, in a market where nobody else has measured them.

The second is forced flow. Each index has a calendar on which bonds enter and leave. The Bloomberg and iBoxx indices rebalance monthly; Cboe's indices follow HYG's and LQD's holdings, so they move when the ETFs' portfolios move; CDX swaps names only at the next semiannual series. A fallen angel leaves an investment-grade bond index at its next monthly rebalance, while a downgraded CDX IG name stays in its series and is simply left out of the next one at the roll. The flows around those dates are mechanical and dated, and a reader who spent years watching what happens to a bond between a downgrade and a default can see them coming. The cleanest published estimate is about CDX: Jennie Bai and Or Shachar at the New York Fed found that an issuer added to CDX.NA.IG widened by about 12 basis points, cumulative and abnormal, over the seven days around its entry (Liberty Street Economics, 30 March 2015). One plausible mechanism is the hedge. Dealers who sell index protection to clients buy single-name protection on the constituents to offset it, and a name that has just joined the index acquires a new set of protection buyers who do not care about its credit.

The third is the hedge you did not choose. CME also lists duration-hedged versions of both contracts, DHB and DHY, which follow Bloomberg duration-hedged indices: the parent index plus short positions in Treasury futures (2-, 5-, 10- and 30-year and Ultra), matched by key rate so that the package has zero option-adjusted duration, as CME describes it. A holder is long carry and spread with the rates stripped out, and in August 2026 DHB was about 60% of CME's credit futures volume (The DESK, 17 September 2026). Kaz asked the obvious question.

"Duration-hedged by whom?"

"By the index provider," Ines said. "It's a rule, Kaz. Nobody holds the hedge. The index says what it would have held."

"Then who owns the part the rule gets wrong?"

Nobody had an answer that was not "you," and that was the answer. The duration-hedged index is a hedge ratio chosen by a methodology document, which is precisely the object Chapter 8 spent §8.2 warning about: it removes the rate risk that the index's analytical duration predicts, and leaves the trader holding everything else, including convexity and the regime-dependent link between rates and spreads that Table 8.2 measures. None of the public sources measures that residual. Chapter 8's Trend Watch on listed credit beta (§8.6) has the launch history, and Exercise 4 below asks you to measure the residual yourself.

Theo finished the review by adding a fifth column to the whiteboard, headed *CDX*, and one row underneath all five: *settles to*. Under the futures he wrote *an index close, built from evaluated prices*. Under CDX he wrote *the ISDA standard model, a dealer composite and, on default, an auction*. Kaz looked at the row for a while and said it was the first time anyone had shown them the legal layer of an index on one line.

## 10B.3 Volume Is Not Liquidity

*Lucía Ferrer's employer is the fictional systematic asset manager of §10.1.1; its PM, its overlay and its e-mails are invented. The exchange figures are as reported by The DESK from exchange data, and are labeled by month.*

The e-mail arrived at 3:20 on Friday 9 October, from the PM who had replied "thanks" to Lucía's roll-week ticket in September (§10.1.1). It had a link to a trade-press headline about CME's credit futures volume rising elevenfold in a year, and one line: *Can the overlay's HY beta leg move from CDX to futures now?*

Lucía had learned in September that the first answer to any question about a series is a question about how the series was built, so she did not start with the headline. She started with two numbers that the headline had left out. One was average daily volume, the flow of contracts traded each day. The other was open interest, the stock of contracts still held overnight. She put them side by side for each exchange, for the month the trade press had been most excited about and for the latest month it had reported.

| Exchange | Feb 2026: ADV / OI | Turnover | Aug 2026: ADV / OI | Turnover |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| CME (IQB, HYB, DHB, DHY) | \$522m / \$689m | 0.76 | \$253m / \$1.61bn (average) | 0.16 |
| Cboe (IBHY, IBIG) | \$435m / \$1.99bn | 0.22 | \$407m / \$2.17bn | 0.19 |
| Eurex (euro contracts) | EUR 110m / EUR 3.1bn | about 0.04 | EUR 105m / EUR 3.44bn | about 0.03 |

*Sources: The DESK, 20 March 2026 and 17 September 2026, reporting exchange data. CME's November 2025 ADV of \$458m was the "11x" headline.*

> **Math, Gently.** *Turnover, or the parking garage.* Think of open interest as the cars parked in a garage overnight and daily volume as the cars that drive in during the day. Turnover is the ratio, $\text{ADV}/\text{OI}$, and its inverse is roughly how many days the average parked car stays. CME in February: $522/689 = 0.76$, so a position lasted about $1/0.76 \approx 1.3$ days on average. Cboe: $435/1{,}990 \approx 0.22$, about four and a half days. Eurex: about 0.04, close to a month, which fits a market the exchange itself describes as mostly RFQ and block-sized, where people open a position and hold it to the roll. None of these numbers is good or bad on its own. A high ratio beside a deep, tight order book is an active market. A high ratio beside a thin top of book, where the size quoted at the best price is small, means the same cars are being driven round the block, and somebody is paying for the petrol. The ratio is the cheapest diagnostic in this chapter, and §10B.10's engine computes it by exchange and month.

The February number was the interesting one, and The DESK's reporting of it was unusually direct. Market sources attributed CME's turnover, and the thin top of book beside it, to rebates paid to market makers; CME declined to specify its incentives and had filed a FOIA confidential-treatment request covering them (The DESK, 20 March 2026). By August the picture had changed shape: CME's ADV had halved and its average open interest had risen about 240% on the year. Lucía read that as the healthier of the two months. Fewer cars were driving in, and far more were parked.

Exchanges paying for liquidity is not a scandal. It is how almost every new futures contract has been launched, and the payments are filed with the regulator. The question for a user is narrower: which part of the volume would still be there if the payments stopped?

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *CBOT Submission No. 26-429, Exhibit 1, "Credit Futures APAC Market Maker Program" (filed with the CFTC, 31 August 2026):* "In order to be eligible to receive the incentives, participants must quote two-sided markets in the Products, at predetermined average bid/ask spreads and minimum quote sizes during a certain percentage of time-in-market." ... "Upon meeting the obligations, as determined by the Exchange, participants will receive predetermined incentives."
>
> **Annotation.** Read it as a price list with the prices removed. The filing says what is being bought (quotes on both sides, no wider than some spread, no smaller than some size, for some share of the session) and says that payment is "predetermined", and it does not say how much. The program runs from 1 October 2026 to 31 May 2027, during designated electronic trading hours, covers all of CME's credit futures and their Basis Trade at Index Close, excludes the leveraged-loan contract, and cannot be combined with the existing Credit Futures Market Maker Program, which tells you there is one. For a desk this is useful in two ways. The start date is an event you can study: turnover before and after 1 October, in the hours the program covers and the hours it does not. And the end date is a liquidity forecast with a date in it.

Lucía found one more data point she could not explain, and wrote it down rather than explaining it. Cboe publishes every contract's daily volume and open interest since launch in a plain file (`cfevoloi.csv`, Appendix B). IBHY traded 276,893 contracts in 2022 and 72,429 in 2023, about 26% of the year before; IBIG fell to 49% (computed from the Cboe file). No exchange release, trade-press piece or paper she could find says why. Whatever bought the 2020–2022 volume left in 2023 without a note. Volume recovered to 141,817 IBHY contracts in 2025 and to 168,798 by 15 October 2026, so the contract survived. But a market that can lose three-quarters of its volume in a year for an undocumented reason is a market whose volume is not, by itself, evidence of anything.

So her diagnostic became four numbers instead of one. **Open interest**, because it counts people who chose to hold the risk overnight. **Block share**, because it shows where size actually trades (§10B.4). **Quoted width**, from the exchanges' own sponsored pieces until the desk measures its own: Cboe reported average IBHY and IBIG bid-offers of about 0.05% of price in May 2025, and Susquehanna's 2024 description of the euro IG future put its order book near 10 price basis points against 6.7 for the most liquid euro IG ETF. And **turnover**, as the check on whether the volume was rented.

Her reply went at 5:05. It did not say no. It said the overlay could move a quarter of its HY leg into Cboe IBHY, entering in the week before the quarterly expiry when the contract's volume is thickest (§10B.6), with the desk's own fills measured against the iBoxx close for two quarters before anyone decided about the rest. The PM replied at 5:11: *Why Cboe and not CME, if CME has the volume?* She answered with the table, and the PM did not reply again, which at that firm meant yes.

> **Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality.** *Myth:* Volume up elevenfold means the futures are liquid. *Reality:* Volume is partly a policy variable of the exchange. A turnover of 0.76 beside a thin top of book is flow somebody paid for, and it may leave when the payments do; open interest, block share and quoted width are the market's own vote, and in August 2026 they said the market was real, growing and still small.

> **Trend Watch: Listed Credit Futures, 2018–2026.** *What changed.* Cboe listed IBHY and IBIG in September and October 2018. Eurex listed euro IG futures in the third quarter of 2021 and euro HY in the fourth quarter of 2022. CME launched IG and HY Very Liquid futures on 17 June 2024, with a duration-hedged IG contract (DHB) beside them and a duration-hedged HY contract (DHY) from March 2025. Eurex added US dollar contracts in September 2024, and ICE launched four MSCI MarketAxess contracts in December 2024 (sources in §10B.2). Eurex started a Credit Index Derivatives Partnership Program on 1 August 2025, with Santander, BNP Paribas, Flow Traders, Goldman Sachs, Jane Street, JP Morgan, Morgan Stanley and Susquehanna named as quoters (FOW, 4 September 2025). CBOT's APAC market-maker program runs from 1 October 2026 to 31 May 2027 (CBOT Submission 26-429). *How big.* US dollar listed ADV grew from about \$56m in January 2024 (Cboe alone) to \$398m in August 2025 and about \$660m in August 2026 (Cboe plus CME); open interest from about \$190m to about \$3.8bn, with Eurex adding EUR 3.4–4.0bn (The DESK, monthly reports, exchange data). Part of that growth is new venues arriving rather than the same contracts growing. *What it means for the desk.* Listed credit beta now exists for anyone with a futures account, it is cheap to hold and thin to trade, and every growth figure above was published by, or for, an exchange that earns from it. Appendix C carries the dated entries.

## 10B.4 Where the Size Goes: Blocks, the Close and the Basis

*The bank credit desk below, its traders and its client are a fictional composite; the contract rules and block statistics are real and sourced.*

The bank's credit-trading floor keeps its ETF trader and its credit-futures trader at adjacent desks with one P&L between them, an arrangement that turns every client ticket into a negotiation between two people who sit close enough to hear each other sigh. On Thursday 8 October Yusuf Adeyemi, on the ETF side, took a call from an asset manager who wanted to swap \$100 million of HYG into IBHY and wanted the swap priced at the index close, so that the fund's benchmark and its position would be marked at the same instant.

Clara Lindqvist, on futures, did the arithmetic before Yusuf hung up. At about \$185,000 a contract, \$100 million is some 540 IBHY contracts. In a quiet month IBHY trades between roughly 6,900 and 11,000 contracts in total, a few hundred a day (Cboe daily file), so the ticket was more than a whole quiet day's lit volume. Walking it through the order book would have been a public announcement with a price impact attached.

So it did not go through the order book, and neither does most size in this market. On Cboe's futures exchange it went as an exchange of contract for related position, in which the client hands the bank its HYG and takes 540 futures at an agreed difference. Cboe's rules require the related leg to be an ETF or a total return swap, and a block needs at least 50 contracts (Cboe rules, 2023). CME offers the same service as a Basis Trade at Index Close, in which the two sides agree a basis now and let the index close set the price. Eurex calls its versions Trade at Index Close and EFP-I, an exchange of futures for an ETF or a bond basket. Yusuf's desk would sell or redeem the HYG; Clara's would own the short futures until the basis converged.

The block statistics come almost entirely from exchange-sponsored pieces, and they are worth quoting with that label attached. Cboe reported blocks above \$1 billion a month for the second month running in August 2026, about a third of IBHY volume and just under 40% of IBIG, with IBIG blocks overtaking IBHY for the first time; its own split of the remainder into an "electronic" bucket does not add up to IBHY's \$5.4 billion traded that month, so the bucket is partial (The DESK, sponsored, 2026). Eurex reported 1,604 blocks worth over EUR 41 billion through September 2026, with 47% of euro IG block notional printed within 2.5 cents of mid (Eurex-sponsored piece, The DESK, 12 October 2026). CME's February 2026 blocks were three, under 1.5% of \$4.4 billion of notional, which fits the paid-for order-book turnover of §10B.3.

Then Clara made the point the ticket was really about. Every contract on every exchange settles to one number: the index provider's closing level, built from evaluated bond prices at the end of the day. The swap at the close was priced off that number. So was the fund's benchmark, so was the final settlement of every future, and so, for the bank, was the P&L of the basis it had just taken on. One print, made once a day, from prices that are partly models.

How good is the print? The best evidence is recent. Andreas Rapp, Sean Shin, Xing Zhou and Qifei Zhu ("When Delayed Spotting Meets Delayed Reporting: Distortions in the Corporate Bond Index Close," SSRN, December 2025) study trades whose dollar price is fixed late in the day by "spotting" the Treasury yield an agreed spread was quoted over. Those trades execute at stale prices, about 19 basis points away from trades done at the same time without the delay, and because they are reported late they bias the end-of-day price estimates that index providers' evaluated prices are built on. The distortion grows with intraday volatility and moves together across bonds, which is the worst possible property for an index close: it does not average away inside a basket of a thousand bonds. A desk that settles futures, swaps ETFs and marks a basis to that close holds a small, correlated position in the quality of its construction.

Yusuf priced the swap with a few cents of extra width for exactly that, and the client accepted, which told both traders the client had read the same paper. Chapter 20 has the ETF's own arbitrage band (§20.3) and its monitor (§20.7); the futures corner of that triangle has no public band data at all, and building one starts with the settlement series in §10B.10.

## 10B.5 Reading the CFTC File

*Lucía's firm, Bram Velde, the feature, the backtest and the e-mails are fictional. The CFTC positions quoted are from the public Traders in Financial Futures dataset as queried for this book; the reclassification reading is the book's own inference from those numbers.*

For most of its history the listed credit market had no public positioning data at all. Then, in 2026, the CFTC's weekly Traders in Financial Futures report, the file that splits each contract's open interest into dealers, asset managers, leveraged funds and others, started carrying CME's two outright credit contracts: "Bloomberg HY Credit Futures" (HYB) from the report of 31 March and "Bloomberg IG Credit Futures" (IQB) from 5 May (CFTC public reporting API, dataset `gpe5-46if`). Cboe's iBoxx contracts are not in it under any name, and neither are CME's duration-hedged pair.

Bram Velde, the data engineer who sits beside Lucía, spent the week of 12 October doing what that sentence hides. The report is dated Tuesday and published the following Friday, so a feature built on it is three days stale on the day it exists. The API pages at a thousand rows, and the field names are long enough that Bram kept a dictionary of them taped to his monitor. Credit contracts appear in the file only while enough large traders hold them, and the HY contract simply stops after the report of 15 September, so the feature has a hole where the most recent month should be. Twenty to twenty-five reportable traders sit behind each contract (The DESK counted 24 and 23 on 7 July), which means a single fund can move a category.

What the file shows is a pairing that makes sense once it is said aloud. On 5 May, in IQB, asset managers held 1,369 contracts long and 6,656 short; leveraged funds held 3,006 long and 1,026 short; dealers were net long about 3,400; open interest was 7,924. By 13 October asset managers were 1,734 long and 4,296 short and leveraged funds 4,874 long and 2,835 short, with dealers about flat. In HYB the May pairing (asset managers net short about 3,200, leveraged funds net long 3,054) had unwound by September, with leveraged funds net short from mid-July and dealers holding a small net long (all from the CFTC dataset).

Bram's feature was the asset managers' net short as a share of open interest, and his first backtest, on the twenty-three weekly observations IQB had produced, came back with a correlation to the next week's index return that made him walk the laptop over to Lucía's desk. She asked to see the best week first, because the best week is where a backtest keeps its secrets.

It was 14 July. In that report about 4,400 contracts of IQB net short left the asset-manager bucket and appeared in the dealer bucket, while open interest barely moved. Nobody had sold anything. A trader, or a few, had been reclassified: the CFTC assigns each reportable trader to one category by its predominant business, and when the assignment changes, the whole position walks across the table in one week. The feature had read a change of label as a change of mind, and the following week's return had happened to cooperate.

The fix was a structural-break filter. A week in which one category's position jumps by more than a set share of open interest, an opposite jump of similar size appears in another category, and open interest itself moves by less than a few percent is flagged as a probable reclassification, and the feature is spliced across it rather than differenced through it. Lucía recognised the shape at once. It was her September roll-date phantom (§10.1.1) in a different file: two things with the same label on either side of a date, and a pipeline that subtracted one from the other.

At 4:40 she wrote the e-mail.

*Positioning feature from the CFTC file (IQB, HYB): not ready. The backtest's best week, 14 July, is a reclassification of about 4,400 contracts from asset managers to dealers with open interest unchanged, not trading. With a break filter the sample correlation falls to roughly zero on 23 weeks, which is also what 23 weeks can tell us. HYB has no reports after 15 September. Cboe's contracts are not in the file. Proposal: keep collecting, filter breaks, revisit at 52 weeks. No position affected.*

The PM wrote back "thanks," which was progress: in September the reply had come with a question.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The insurer or asset manager that is net short in the file (a generic seat).* A crowding model that reads "asset managers net short 6,656 IQB" as a bearish bet has the sign of the motive backwards. An insurer or a long-only manager holding \$5 billion of investment-grade bonds who sells futures is hedging what it already owns, often for a quarter, often around a cash inflow it has not yet invested or a liability event it has. Its short is a temporary reduction of a long. When the hedge comes off, it buys futures, and that buying says nothing about its view of credit either. The leveraged-fund longs on the other side may be directional, may be one leg of a futures-against-CDX or futures-against-ETF basis, or may be relative value; the file cannot tell them apart. A positioning signal in this market is a signal about who is hedging, which is real information about future flow and almost none about future value.

That afternoon Theo showed Mags Dunleavy the IQB chart on his phone, because he thought she would enjoy the reclassification. She did.

"Back in my day positioning was what a salesman told you at dinner," she said, "and he was lying, and you knew he was lying, and you still paid for dinner." She scrolled to the dealer bucket. "This one lies once a week, in public, with a date on it, and you can catch it." She handed the phone back. "That's an improvement. Don't tell anyone I said so."

> **Ways This Blows Up.** *(1) The index close as a single point of failure.* Every futures contract, every swap at the close and every basis the desk marks to it settles to one evaluated print a day; a correlated distortion in that print (about 19 bp on the delayed-spot trades Rapp, Shin, Zhou and Zhu study) is a loss shared by every position the desk thought was hedged. *(2) The duration-hedged residual nobody measures.* DHB and DHY remove the rate risk their methodology predicts; convexity and the moving rate-spread correlation of §8.2 stay with the holder, and no public source sizes them. *(3) The reclassification jump.* A 4,400-contract move between categories with open interest unchanged is a label, not a trade; a pipeline that differences through it will find its best signal on the day the regulator tidied its records.

## 10B.6 The Roll: Twice a Year the Index Changes Identity

*The dealer bank, its index desk and Rafael Quist are a fictional composite; the roll dates, clearing volumes and research findings are real and sourced. Theo's ROBO is canon.*

On Monday 21 September 2026 Rafael Quist got to the dealer's index desk at 6:10, because roll day starts before the market does. The index administrator, S&P Dow Jones Indices, had dated the September roll for Monday, the 20th having fallen on a Sunday, and the new series of CDX IG and CDX HY, the forty-seventh of each, started trading that morning (S&P DJI, CDX roll notices for September 2026).

A protection buyer who wants to stay on the run has to sell the old series and buy the new one, and so does every other holder on the same morning. Rafael's job was to quote that switch as a single price, the roll, and absorb whatever the clients did not net out among themselves. On a normal day his scarce input is attention. On roll day it is balance sheet: every client roll he takes leaves him long one series and short the other until somebody takes the other side, and the clearing house charges initial margin on both legs while he waits.

The roll is a price, not a formality, because the two series are different instruments. The old series, launched in March, matures on 20 June 2031; the new one on 20 December 2031. That makes it six months longer, on a credit curve that normally slopes upward. And its list of names is different: issuers that were downgraded, merged or defaulted are left out and replacements come in, chosen by the administrator's rules from dealer liquidity polls. So the new series trades at a different spread from the old one on the same morning, and the difference has a name, the **series basis**.

> **Math, Gently.** *The series basis: six months of curve plus a change of guests.* Picture the index as a dinner table with a hundred seats, with a reservation that ends on a fixed date. On roll day a new table is set: the reservation runs six months longer, and a few guests have been replaced. The new table costs more for two reasons, and you can price each one. First, the curve. If five-year protection on the average high-yield name costs about 15 bp more for each extra half-year of maturity *(illustrative)*, the new series is about 15 bp wider on that account alone. Second, the guests. Suppose one name trading at 1,200 bp leaves and a replacement at 350 bp joins, each with a one-hundredth weight. The index loses $(1{,}200 - 350)/100 = 8.5$ bp of spread. The new series is about $15 - 8.5 = 6.5$ bp wider than the old *(illustrative)*. In investment grade the same arithmetic is smaller: perhaps 3 bp of curve, and a 250 bp name replaced by an 80 bp one at a weight of 1/125 for $-1.4$ bp, a series basis near $+1.6$ bp. The roll quote is this number plus the dealer's charge for absorbing the imbalance. The full treatment of the index's legs and annuity is §24.2.

By 7:30 Rafael's screens showed what the clearing data later confirmed. Cleared index volume comes in waves around the roll: in 2025, Clarus found cleared USD CDX volume peaking on 22 September, five days after the 17 September IMM date (Clarus, "CRD volumes at the end of Q3 2025," 5 November 2025). The largest single week on record is still the September 2021 roll: ICE reported that ICE Clear Credit cleared over \$3 trillion of notional, counting both sides, in the week ending 24 September 2021, \$1.3 trillion of it for clients; set against the \$9.2 trillion ICE reported for the whole quarter, one week looks like about a third, though the two figures may not count the same things (ICE, CDS clearing 3Q 2021 update). Treat "about a third" as a shape, not a statistic. Chapter 10 §10.2.3 cites a range of 25–50% of quarterly notional for the roll window; we could not find a source for that range, and the ICE week is the number that should stand in its place.

The listed futures have a calendar of their own, and in September 2026 it touched the swaps' calendar. Cboe's quarterly IBHY contract settles on the first business day of its month, and its volume piles into the last week before that: the week of 24 August 2026 was its busiest of the summer, as were 26–27 May and 24–25 November 2025 (Cboe daily file). Eurex's credit futures settle at the index close on the third Friday, which in September 2026 was the 18th, the last business day before the CDX roll, and Eurex's roll months run about three times its other months' volume (Eurex deck). A trader looking for the cheapest week to move credit beta in size finds that the cheap weeks are the same weeks everyone else is rolling, and that the dealer balance sheet is the one input all of them share.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The dealer's index desk in roll week (a generic seat).* The client sees the roll as a chore with a bid-offer. The dealer sees a week in which every client wants the same trade in the same direction at the same time, and the dealer's capital, its CCP margin and its risk limits are what make that possible. The roll price therefore carries a charge for balance sheet that has nothing to do with credit, and it is largest when clients are most one-sided, for example after a sell-off, when protection buyers roll and few sellers do. A dealer that quotes the roll tight on Monday to win the flow is betting it can lay the imbalance off by Wednesday. Some years it can.

Old-series positions do not disappear at the roll; they become quieter. Liquidity moves to the new series within days, the old series goes off the run, and its marks start to come from fewer quotes. Chapter 8-C's stale-series morning (§8C.6) is what that looks like on the explain: a Friday mark republished on Monday because nobody quoted the old series. Lucía's spliced history (§10.1.1) is what it looks like in a backtest: a series basis booked as a market move.

ROBO supplied the third version, on Theo's desk, the week after the cold open. Theo asked it to rebuild the desk's CDX HY hedge P&L over the last ten rolls and, at each roll date, to "roll the position." ROBO closed the old series and opened the same notional in the new series at the same spread, because the spec said nothing about the spread being different. Every roll produced a profit or a loss equal to the series basis times the risky duration, for a cumulative \$410,000 of P&L that had never existed *(illustrative)*. The precise spec that replaced it states the series identifier on every row, the roll date from the administrator's notice, the roll executed at the observed old and new prices, and a separate *series basis* line on roll day that is neither a market move nor a trading gain (§8C.2 has the line).

Under the series basis sits an older and more interesting gap: the one between the index and its own ingredients. An index CDS is economically a portfolio of single-name contracts, so its fair spread can be rebuilt from theirs.

> **Math, Gently.** *Intrinsic value, and the skew.* If the index is a portfolio of names, its fair spread is an average of their spreads, but not a simple one. A name trading at 400 bp has a shorter risky duration than one at 60 bp (it is more likely to stop paying early), so each basis point of its spread is worth less. Weight each spread by its risky duration $D_i$: $s^{\text{intr}} = \sum_i D_i\,s_i \big/ \sum_i D_i$. Take a five-name toy index with equal notionals, spreads 60, 80, 100, 150 and 400 bp and risky durations 4.6, 4.5, 4.4, 4.2 and 3.6. The products are 276, 360, 440, 630 and 1,440, which sum to 3,146; the durations sum to 21.3; so $s^{\text{intr}} = 3{,}146/21.3 \approx 147.7$ bp, below the simple average of 158 bp, because the widest name carries the least duration. If the index itself is quoted at 143 bp, the **skew** is $143 - 147.7 = -4.7$ bp: the index is tighter than its ingredients. A desk that sells index protection and buys protection on all five names at their own spreads would lock that gap in, minus the cost of five bid-offers, five margin calls and the possibility that the gap widens before it closes. The engine in §10B.10 computes it from constituent spreads; §24.3 places it among the other meanings of basis.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Bai and Shachar, "The Effects of Entering and Exiting a Credit Default Swap Index," Liberty Street Economics, Federal Reserve Bank of New York (30 March 2015):* "the value of a replicated portfolio will be the duration-weighted average of the constituents' spreads" ... "the basis usually teeters around zero" ... "the difference reached 60 basis points in absolute value" ... "Yet the spread of the index 'jumps' at the time of the roll."
>
> **Annotation.** Three findings in four fragments, and each is a desk fact. The skew is near zero most of the time, because index arbitrage keeps it there when dealers have balance sheet. It reached 60 bp in 2008–09, because dealers did not. And the roll is a discontinuity in the generic index series, which is why a return series must be built inside one series (§10.2.3). What a 2026 desk adds is the futures. A bond-index future has its own "intrinsic," the index close, and its own skew to CDX, and nobody publishes either.

Mags had a view on all of this, as she does on most things that arrive with a methodology document. "Back in my day," she told Theo when he showed her the roll-day screens, "the index was a fax from a dealer with the names crossed out in pen. At least you knew who crossed them out." She looked at the S&P notice for a while. "This is better. It's still somebody crossing out names."

The most famous skew of the last fifteen years belonged to a single position. In the spring of 2012 the financial press reported that one bank's trading in an old investment-grade series had grown large enough to pull that series away from its constituents, and that hedge funds were trading the gap against it. The U.S. Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations later wrote the case history (*JPMorgan Chase Whale Trades*, 15 March 2013), and §8C.7 reads it as an explain. The skew is usually a few basis points of plumbing. When it is not, somebody's balance sheet is the reason, and the size of the skew is a rough measure of how large that balance sheet has become relative to the market's capacity to arbitrage it.
