## Cold Open: Twelve Folders and a Blind That Does Not Close

*Friday, 16 October 2026, 5:35 p.m. The desk, its cast and ROBO are fictional. Every company in the folders is real, and every date on a tab comes from a court record, a filing or a named report.*

The small room with the blind that does not close is where the desk takes things apart on Fridays. The blind has been stuck at forty degrees since before Theo arrived, so the low October sun lays a stripe across the table, and tonight the stripe falls across twelve manila folders.

Mags Dunleavy squared the stack with both hands. Each tab carried a company name and, under it in her handwriting, a date: Revlon, Boardriders, Envision, Incora, Robertshaw, Lumen, Trinseo, Saks, Altice France, Thames Water, Zayo, Pluralsight. "House rule," she said. "Nobody reads past the date on the tab. Each folder stops where the market stopped knowing."

Theo had been given one job that week, and it had gone wrong in a way he was still deciding how to describe. On Monday he had asked ROBO to "build a docket feed for the LMT cases." ROBO had built one. For Incora's adversary proceeding alone it returned 4,100 entries: every motion to appear pro hac vice, every certificate of service, every notice of appearance and every amended exhibit list, each parsed into case number, entry number, date and a searchable description. Theo searched it. He could find the day each lawyer in Houston had joined the case. He could not find out who had won.

"It did exactly what I asked," he said. "There's no field for which way it went."

"There never is," said Mags. "A docket is the court's diary. It doesn't know it's your P&L."

Ines Okafor set her coffee down like a chip. "What's the pot, and who at this table read the clause?" She tapped the stack. "Twelve hands, and we know how every one of them ended. That's the trouble with autopsies. Everyone is brilliant about the dead. So we play them blind, up to the tab, and at each entry we write two things: what the score would have said that morning, and what it cost the holder who was left on the wrong side of the door."

Kaz Lind pulled the Incora folder across the table, opened it to the first page, and found a list of law firms. "Is it always this many lawyers?"

"In the good ones," said Mags.

Theo wrote the question at the top of his pad, because Ines would ask it again on Monday: *can a ruling be a feature, and can an exclusion be priced on the day the deal closes?*

By the end of this chapter you will be able to read a docket as a time series rather than a story; label a ruling by its direction for the excluded holder and give it a log-odds jump; say, for twelve real cases from 2020 to 2026, which Priming Risk Score features would have fired and what the excluded holder's markout was; see why a pod's pair inside each case earns exactly that markout; and write the litigation map as a Markov chain whose transitions jump on ruling dates. You will also see why all of it is less certain than it looks.

## 13B.1 How to Read a Docket

Theo pulled ROBO's 4,100 rows up on the wall screen and scrolled, slowly, so that the size of the thing could do the explaining. Kaz watched the descriptions go past.

"'Motion for admission pro hac vice,'" Kaz read. "'For this vice.' Which vice? Leverage?"

"'For this occasion,'" said Theo. "A lawyer from out of state, admitted for one case. Incora has dozens of them. Each one files a motion, gets an order, and somebody files a certificate saying everyone was served with both." He stopped scrolling. "Most of a docket is the court proving to itself that it told everyone."

The twelve folders came from four kinds of place. Federal cases, every US bankruptcy here included, sit on PACER, which charges by the page. New York's commercial cases sit on the state's own filing system. English judgments are published by the National Archives as Find Case Law. And Altice France's most important "rulings" came from no court at all, but from the EMEA Credit Derivatives Determinations Committee, which posts its decisions as PDFs.

Then Theo showed Kaz that an entry has four clocks on it. It is *filed* when paper reaches the court, *entered* when the clerk dockets it, *argued* on the hearing day, and *decided* when the judge says or signs the decision. For Incora, decided meant two days: an oral ruling from the bench on 10 July 2024 and a written report and recommendation on 17 January 2025. Serta had the same split: a bench ruling for the borrower on 28 March 2023, a written opinion after trial that June.

"Which one is the event?" said Kaz.

"The first moment the market could know," said Theo. "Sometimes that's a reporter in the courtroom. Sometimes it's a law firm's client alert the next morning. Almost never is it the entry date."

Then he added the column ROBO had not built: *direction*. A ruling scores $+1$ if it favours the excluded or non-consenting holders, $-1$ if it favours the majority, the debtor or the people being released, and $0$ if it is mixed. Judge Isgur's 10 July 2024 ruling for the Incora minority is $+1$; the district court's reversal of 8 December 2025 is $-1$.

"So the label is one number per ruling," said Kaz. "Then what's the feature?"

"The feature is what the ruling does to the odds," said Ines, from the door.

> **Math, Gently.** *Odds, log-odds, and a ruling as a jump.* A probability of 0.5 is odds of 1 to 1; 0.8 is odds of 4 to 1. The log-odds, or *logit*, is the natural log of the odds: $\operatorname{logit}(p) = \ln\big(p/(1-p)\big)$. At 0.5 it is 0; at 0.8 it is about 1.39; it runs from minus infinity to plus infinity while $p$ stays between 0 and 1. So the desk adds a ruling's effect on this scale: any number added to a log-odds converts back, through $p = 1/(1+e^{-\text{logit}})$, to a legal probability, whereas 0.3 added to a probability of 0.9 is nonsense. Picture a dial with no end stops wired to a needle that moves only between 0 and 1: a click moves the needle a lot near the middle and barely at all near the ends. *A tiny worked number.* Before the Fifth Circuit's *Serta* opinion of 31 December 2024, a reader gives excluded lenders in an "open market purchase" dispute an even chance: log-odds 0. Having read the opinion, the reader assigns it a jump of $+1.0$ for the excluded side *(illustrative)*. The new log-odds is 1.0 and the new probability is $1/(1+e^{-1}) \approx 0.73$. The same click from a starting point of 0.9 lands at about 0.96. Written for the desk's variable $\theta_w$, the chance that a priming deal under wording $w$ *survives* challenge, the same ruling is a jump of $-1.0$, from 0.5 to 0.27. The full version, with one jump per ruling and per wording class, is §14.6.1.

The jump is a judgment, and the folder records whose and on what day. Nineteen rulings are too few to fit anything (§13B.7) and enough to keep a disciplined diary.

That left the two lines Ines wanted at the bottom of every folder. The first is *PRS flags*: which features of Chapter 13's Priming Risk Score would have fired, and when. Some are in the §13.2.1 vector already (`loan_px`, `runway_months`, `holder_hhi`, `coop_reported`, `unsub_capacity_pct_ev`, `buyback_exception`, `integration_gap`); the folders propose new ones as they go. The second is *Excluded markout*: what happened to the price of the tranche left outside the door, against the tranche that went through it.

> **Math, Gently.** *A markout is an average of what happened next, given who you were.* Ines calls a markout "the hand history": the price some time after a trade minus the price at the trade, averaged over many trades. Here the "trade" is a liability-management deal, and what sorts the hands is participation status. Write $P_{t_0}$ for the excluded tranche's price on the last day before the deal became knowable and $P_{t_0+h}$ for its price $h$ later. The excluded markout is the conditional expectation $M^{\text{excl}}(h) = \mathbb{E}\big[P_{t_0+h} - P_{t_0} \mid \text{excluded}\big]$, and the participant's is the same with the condition flipped. *A tiny worked number.* Two holders own the same first-lien loan at 70 *(illustrative)*. One is invited, swaps into super-priority paper and marks 90 a month later; the other is not, and marks 40. Same loan, same lien, same company: markouts of $+20$ and $-30$, a coalition gap of 50 points. Twelve folders give no expectation, only a dozen dated points over different horizons, mostly vendor quotes rather than prints. The full version, for dealer trades, is Chapter 2-B and Chapter 18.

"One more reason to care," said Ines. "Dov's pod across town runs these as pairs." Each folder names the pair a pod would have put on: long the paper likely inside the deal, short the paper likely left out, hedged so that default and the sector cancel. What the hedge leaves is participation status plus a judge, so the pair's P&L is the excluded leg's markout with a sign on it.

Used in / builds on: §12B.4 (the menu of out-of-court moves, here as observed transitions), §13.2.1 and §13.8 (the PRS vector these flags come from), §14.6.1 (the log-odds jump, in full), Chapter 25 §25.6 (the docket as an event stream, where the feed ROBO built gets rebuilt properly), Appendix E (the court is a random variable with timestamps).

## 13B.2 The US Diaries, in Deal Order

Mags dealt the US folders in the order the deals were done, which is the order the market learned. Each gets a diary of dated entries and three lines in Theo's hand. Ines read the entries aloud and stopped at each date to ask what the room would have done with only that much paper.

> **Docket Diary.** *Revlon BrandCo, 7 May 2020 to 3 April 2023.* **7 May 2020:** Revlon signs a BrandCo credit agreement with Jefferies as agent: \$815mm of new-money term loans and a \$950mm roll-up commitment that lets lenders in the 2016 term loan, a \$1.8bn facility with Citibank as agent, swap at par into new 2025 paper. Elizabeth Arden and other brand intellectual property move to BrandCo subsidiaries that do not guarantee the 2016 loan; by month-end \$846mm of 2016 loans have rolled up. **August 2020:** the 2016 loan trades at "20 to 30" cents, in the Second Circuit's later words. **11 August 2020:** Citibank, as agent, wires about \$894mm of principal to 2016 lenders by mistake (Chapter 27's Long Scene "The default field"). **12 August 2020:** excluded 2016 lenders authorize UMB Bank to sue, alleging insolvency "by as much as \$1.71 billion"; the complaint is never served and is dismissed without prejudice on 6 November. **15 June 2022:** Chapter 11 in the Southern District of New York. **3 April 2023:** plan confirmed. As summarized by debtor's counsel and press reports, BrandCo B-2 lenders take 82% of the equity and of a \$670mm rights offering, 2016 and B-3 lenders 18%, and non-electing 2016 and B-3 lenders share a \$56mm cash pool; the 2016 litigation is resolved "conclusively and consensually." *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel: "We note for the record that we were not wired anything. We note it with regret."*
>
> *PRS flags:* capacity to move intellectual property outside the guarantor group (`unsub_capacity_pct_ev`, the basket triangle of Chapter 25 §25.3) and room for a par roll-up offered to a subset, both readable in the 2016 agreement before May 2020; `loan_px` only afterwards. *Excluded markout:* the 2016 loan at 20–30 within three months of the deal, behind roll-up paper exchanged at par; in the plan, a minority share of equity and rights alongside B-3 lenders, or a slice of a \$56mm pool. *Pod pair:* long BrandCo paper, short the 2016 loan. Nobody lends a term loan, so the short is a forward sale re-lit every settlement cycle, and what it isolates is one question: where did the brands go?

Kaz read the flags line twice. "The capacity was in the document for four years. Why did the score wait?"

"It didn't have to," said Theo. "`unsub_capacity_pct_ev` was there the day the agreement was signed. What changed in 2020 was `runway_months`. A basket is an option the sponsor holds. The runway is what tells you it's about to be exercised."

Mags grunted, which in her register is agreement. "Back in my day we found out about the basket when the lawyer for the other side read it to us at the closing. Then we argued about it for three years, and the company paid both of us." She tapped the folder. "This is better. Now the closing reads it to you in May, and you argue with a spreadsheet."

> **View From Another Seat.** *The dealer as agent.* In the Revlon folder dealers appear twice, neither time as traders: Jefferies as agent on the new BrandCo facility, Citibank as agent on the 2016 loan whose collateral walked. An agent earns a fee for the mechanics (notices, payments, the register of who holds what), while a dealer desk at the same firm sees the LMT as a financing event: new paper to place, old paper whose holders are about to want bids. Jefferies recurs in the Saks folder as initial purchaser of the notes a later exchange subordinated. For the systematic desk the agent's seat is a source of timestamps, and Robertshaw shows what happens when an amendment is never posted.

> **Docket Diary.** *Boardriders, August 2020 to September 2023.* **April 2018:** credit agreement; about \$450mm of first-lien debt, held partly by Oaktree, also the sponsor. **August 2020:** \$110mm of new super-priority money; \$321mm of participants' loans rolled up at par into equal super-priority debt, \$431mm in all. Majority amendments strip the debt covenant and the requirement that any intercreditor agreement be junior or pari passu, and the roll-up is papered as "open market purchase" agreements. Non-consenting lenders, about \$85mm, go to the back. Their loans trade at a 40–50% discount. **Late September 2020:** S&P raises the rating from SD to CCC and says the deal was "tantamount to default." **October 2020:** ICG, York and others sue in New York Supreme Court. **17 October 2022:** Justice Masley denies dismissal of the breach and implied-covenant claims (the "open market" exception is ambiguous) and dismisses tortious interference against Oaktree. **August 2023:** a settlement tied to Authentic Brands' purchase of Boardriders repays non-participants in full; dismissal with prejudice follows in September. Authentic closed around 1 September at an undisclosed price (Moody's cited \$1.25bn, Bloomberg about \$1.3bn). *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel: "We were paid par. We would like it known that we always believed in the brand."*
>
> *PRS flags:* `buyback_exception` (an undefined "open market purchase" in the pro rata sharing carve-out), a sponsor that is also a lender (`holder_hhi`, with the sponsor's affiliate inside the majority), and the rating tell, SD then CCC, which Chapter 13's vector does not yet carry. *Excluded markout:* about 50–60 at the deal; par on the sale, three years later. *Pod pair:* long the super-priority paper, short the excluded loan: everybody's trade on deal day, and it lost the excluded leg's 40-to-50-point recovery.

"'Open market purchase,'" said Mags. "The most expensive three words in finance. Here they were the cheapest. A judge said she couldn't tell what they meant, and not knowing was worth par."

"So everybody's trade was a short of something that went to par," said Theo.

"A short of a lawsuit and a sale process," said Ines. "They didn't win a ruling. They won the right to keep arguing, and a buyer arrived who preferred paying them to finding out." She wrote on the whiteboard: *exclusion is a bet on a sale or a ruling, not a stable recovery*. TriMark, a month later in 2020, settled in January 2022 by moving its excluded lenders into the super-priority tranche at par. Serta's non-participants, with neither a buyer nor a settlement, received 0.6–2.4% in the plan.

> **Docket Diary.** *Envision Healthcare, April 2022 to 3 November 2023; and 17 June 2025.* **April 2022:** a drop-down. A new AmSurg first-lien facility of about \$1.3bn sends about \$1.1bn of proceeds up to Envision, and AmSurg, the ambulatory-surgery business, is designated an unrestricted subsidiary, taking about 83% of the ambulatory-surgery EBITDA outside the old lenders' reach, by later accounts. **June–August 2022:** an uptier. AmSurg raises about \$1.3bn of second-lien debt and lends it to Envision, which repurchases about \$1.9bn of its own debt at a discount, capturing roughly \$582mm. Non-participants, about 9% by Octus's count, go to the back, the "fourth-out." S&P's reported actions took the rating to selective default, then CCC. **November 2022:** by one widely circulated account citing market prices, AmSurg debt near 98 and Envision's first lien near 25, against 73 for that first lien in March. **15 May 2023:** Chapter 11 in the Southern District of Texas, Judge Lopez, two silos and no DIP. **31 July 2023:** dissenting lenders sue on unanimous-consent grounds (dismissed with prejudice on 9 November). **11 October 2023:** plan confirmed, effective 3 November: AmSurg's first lien paid in full and its second lien taking the equity (projected recovery 73%); Envision's second-out lenders taking its equity, the unsecured notes warrants. **17 June 2025:** Ascension agrees to buy AmSurg for a reported \$3.9bn, about \$250mm above the top of the plan valuation; Octus estimates more than 1.3 times money for the drop-down lenders. *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel, asks which silo it is in, and is told both.*
>
> *PRS flags:* `unsub_capacity_pct_ev`, firing the day of the designation in April 2022 and readable before it; non-pro-rata repurchase capacity (`buyback_exception`); the SD-then-CCC rating tell. *Excluded markout:* Envision first lien from 73 to about 25 in eight months on that account, while AmSurg debt sat near 98: a gap of about 73 points inside one corporate family, before anyone filed. *Pod pair:* long AmSurg debt, short Envision first lien, which removes the healthcare-services factor and the sponsor and isolates one question: which silo the surgical EBITDA lives in.

Ines held up the November 2022 numbers. "Ninety-eight and twenty-five. Same sponsor, same industry, same management team, one org chart. What's a spread model's beta between those two loans?"

"High," said Kaz, "until April 2022."

"And after?"

Kaz thought about it honestly. "After, the beta is a question about a box on an org chart. Nothing in the return series would have told me the box was going to move." They wrote *beta is a document variable* under Marcus's line from Chapter 13, *seniority is a field with a date on it*.

> **Docket Diary.** *Incora (Wesco Aircraft), 28 March 2022 to the Fifth Circuit.* The four steps of 28 March 2022 are told in §13.9, and the diary only dates what followed. **February 2022:** a minority group announces more than one-third of the 2026 secured notes, blocking the majority's planned two-thirds; the 2026 notes, by one secondary account, run from about 85 to above par as both sides chase blocking positions. **28 March 2022:** the one-day transaction closes. **1 June 2023:** Chapter 11 in the Southern District of Texas, with an adversary proceeding the same day; a 35-day trial follows in 2024. **10 July 2024:** oral ruling from Judge Isgur: no breach of the 2024 indenture, a breach of the 2026 indenture by a "domino agreement" that needed two-thirds, liens on all 2026 notes survive. Direction $+1$. **27 December 2024:** a consensual plan confirmed, appellate rights preserved. **17 January 2025:** written report and recommendation: the 2026 liens are restored, and the majority's \$250mm of new notes is only an unsecured claim. **31 January 2025:** emergence; secured noteholders take 98.4% of the equity and \$420mm of take-back notes. **8 December 2025:** Chief Judge Crane of the district court reverses: each step complied with its own words, a single-day closing "is not unusual," and the remedy would in any case be damages, since rescission would be "plainly inequitable." Direction $-1$. **About 6 January 2026:** the minority noteholders are reported to have noticed an appeal to the Fifth Circuit. No Fifth Circuit ruling had been reported by the date on the tab. *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel, declines to say which Ad Hoc Group it is.*
>
> *PRS flags:* a *dilutable threshold*, a new feature: majority consent can issue pari passu notes beside a two-thirds test for anything having "the effect of" releasing collateral. A group with 59% of \$900mm reaches two-thirds once new notes $x$ satisfy $(531 + x)/(900 + x) \ge 2/3$, so $x \approx \$207$mm, below the \$250mm raised; `integration_gap` $= 2/3 - 0.59 \approx 0.077$. And a *blocking-position run*: abnormal buying of a distressed secured tranche toward par. *Excluded markout:* secured on 10 July 2024 and on 17 January 2025, unsecured behind about \$1.7bn of new debt on 8 December 2025, and an appeal pending; no TRACE path was available to the folder. *Pod pair:* there was no clean one. The likely participant and the likely excluded held the same bond, distinguished only by who held it, and a pod without a seat could only be long or short the bond's average holder.

"That's the first folder where the pair doesn't exist," said Theo.

"It's the first folder where you can see why," said Ines. "The excluded leg and the included leg were the same CUSIP. The difference was the name on the register. You can't borrow a name."

Kaz was redoing the dilution on the edge of the folder. "A thin blocking third goes for much less. A 34% stake in the \$900mm class falls under a third after $900 \times (3 \times 0.34 - 1) = 18$ million of new paper."

"Which is why the 2026s ran to par," said Mags. "Everybody was buying the same vote."

> **View From Another Seat.** *The rescue financier.* In the Incora record the majority wore two hats: the district court described it as led by funds managed or advised by PIMCO and Silver Point, the largest holders of the 2024 and 2026 notes, and the same funds had proposed rescue financing in December 2021, when UK auditors wanted \$150–200mm injected (*Wesco Aircraft Holdings v. SSD Investments*, S.D. Tex., 8 December 2025). From that seat the deal is a loan nobody else would make, priced partly in priority, and the minority's rival offers (about \$147.5mm, then \$173mm, against \$250mm) were the board's auction. The desk reads it as a state variable: when the holder of the vote is also the likeliest lender of last resort, the next dollar is likelier to arrive non-pro-rata (§13.4).

> **Docket Diary.** *Robertshaw, 9 May 2023 to 16 August 2024.* **9 May 2023:** a super-priority credit agreement: \$95mm of first-out new money, old loans exchanged into second-out and third-out, signed with an ad hoc group (Bain Capital Credit, Canyon, Eaton Vance) and Invesco. "Required Lender" means more than 50% of first-out plus second-out loans, read from the agent's register. **July 2023:** Invesco quietly buys past 50% of first-out plus second-out, mostly through the first-out, and becomes Required Lender. **October–November 2023:** interest is missed; Amendments 1 to 4 extend the grace period, add bridge money and set a Chapter 11 deadline, and Invesco directs the agent not to post them; the ad hoc group learns of them around 15 November. **11 December 2023:** the ad hoc group and the sponsor, One Rock, lend \$228.3mm to RS Funding, a vehicle the parent votes and Robertshaw economically owns; the cash repays the \$30mm ABL and prepays \$117.6mm of first-out loans plus a \$30.7mm make-whole. Invesco falls below 50%, the ad hoc group becomes Required Lender and signs Amendment 5. **15 February 2024:** Chapter 11, Southern District of Texas, Judge Lopez. **20 June 2024:** the court holds the ad hoc group were the Required Lenders and Amendment 5 valid; Robertshaw itself breached by not sending all net proceeds to RS Funding; the remedy is a money claim. Direction $-1$. **16 August 2024:** a liquidating plan is confirmed over Invesco's objection, with an \$11mm unsecured pool and a \$10mm deficiency pool. *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel, confirms that it was the Required Lender on every day it said so.*
>
> *PRS flags:* *Required Lender fragility*, a new feature: the control majority rests on a tranche the borrower can prepay, so the vote can be bought back with someone else's money; amendments that do not appear on the agent's posting site (observable only by their absence, and only afterwards); `runway_months`, after a missed coupon in the autumn. *Excluded markout:* Invesco's claim of about \$40mm, against the debtors' estimate of about 3% recovery for that class; about nine months from the first deal to the petition. *Pod pair:* none tradable. The thing that changed hands was a vote, and the vote turned out to be prepayable.

"Control is a long position in the denominator," said Kaz, pleased with it.

"Control was a long position in a denominator the other side could shrink," said Theo. "Invesco bought the majority. The ad hoc group bought it back by paying off part of it." He wrote the new feature in the margin, `rl_prepayable_share`: the fraction of the Required Lender majority held in tranches the borrower can prepay. Then, smaller: *damages against a bankrupt borrower*, which is where a won argument ends up.

No suit by excluded holders had been reported. *(Cooperation agreements signed in this book so far: still 2. Lumen's was counted in Chapter 13 while it was still warm, and the morgue does not count a body twice.)*
>
> *PRS flags:* `coop_reported` from 31 October; a new *within-issuer wedge* feature, the TSA-minus-non-TSA price gap, at about ten points; transfer restrictions, which show up in the data as `days_since_last_print` lengthening on the restricted paper. *Excluded markout:* the non-TSA Level 3 2028s at 47 in November 2023, offered 86 in September 2024 and tendered at about 99.4 in May 2026. Exclusion here was a bet on an asset sale, and the sale arrived. *Pod pair:* PB-08, below.

> **The Pair.** *Pair Book entry PB-08, closed. Dov Adler's pod at Vantress Capital Management, a fictional composite, as written up by Marcus Oyelaran (fictional); positions, the marks labeled illustrative, and all P&L are invented; Lumen, Level 3, their notes and the dates are real.* **Thesis.** Dated, as Dov dates all of them, on Friday 10 November 2023 (Chapter 13, §13.5): *by March 2024 the deal will protect the creditors whose collateral sits at Level 3 and leave the parent's unsecured holders behind them.* **Legs.** Long \$15mm face Level 3 Financing 3.875% first-lien notes due 2029, bought at 73 and outside the agreement by mandate, because the pod does not join groups; short \$10mm face Lumen parent senior unsecured notes, sold at 28 and borrowed from an asset manager through the prime broker at 150 bp. **What the hedge removed.** Family spread beta, the telecom factor and most of the rates, by market-value DTS (the short's market value a quarter of the long's). **What idio remained.** Which entity the deal protected, plus the pod's own seat, sized and unhedged. Both legs sat outside the agreement, so the pair was the difference between two excluded markouts, an *entity* wedge; the members captured the *seat* wedge on top. **The close, Friday 22 March 2024.** The replay marks the long at 76 and the short at 24 *(illustrative; the folder holds no TRACE prints for that day, and Exercise 1 asks you to replace both)*: +\$0.45mm on the long, +\$0.40mm on the short, \$0.85mm of idio. Carry over the four and a half months cost about \$0.17mm, and half a point on each leg in and out another \$0.25mm, so the net was about \$0.43mm against a \$1.0mm target, a capture ratio near 0.5. **What leaked.** The wall-crossed restriction: TSA paper, which held the full seat wedge, existed only for signatories, and signing restricts a fund's whole book, so the pod owned the version a non-signer could buy. And the recall: the week the amended agreement went out, the lender recalled half the Lumen notes, and Wen's borrow sheet shows a re-borrow at 400 bp *(illustrative)*. **What it costs to carry and to short.** About \$1,200 a day before the recall, and a short whose coupon is a sixth of its price. **Coda.** Dov closed on the date in his sentence. Kept, the short would have met September's exchange offering \$700 of new 10% notes per \$1,000 of Lumen's 4.5% 2029s: from a 28 entry, about forty-two points on \$10mm, roughly \$4.2mm, as the company tidied up its orphans (§14.9 prices that gap). A dated thesis is a stop-loss on a story.

## 13B.4 The Litigation Map as a Markov Chain

By seven the stripe of sun had gone and Kaz had the whiteboard, with a box for each place a folder could be: *announced*, *challenged*, *motion to dismiss denied*, *trial*, *ruled* $+1$, *ruled* $-1$, *reversed*, *settled*, *Chapter 11* (or its French and English cousins), *emerged*, and one box drawn with a double border, *cooperative close*.

Kaz drew the arrows in the order of a lawsuit, with bankruptcy at the end. Mags took the marker and redrew them. "Bankruptcy isn't at the end. It's in the middle. Half these rulings were made by a bankruptcy judge."

So they counted. From *announced*, four folders went to *challenged* (Revlon, Boardriders, Incora, Robertshaw), five straight into a court process (Envision, Trinseo, Saks, Altice France, Thames Water), and three into the double-bordered box: Lumen and Zayo, where the lenders' agreement was the deal, and Pluralsight, where the lenders took the keys. Vista would not call that cooperative; the lenders' word went on the box.

> **Math, Gently.** *A Markov chain is folders moving between trays.* Each folder sits in a tray labeled with its state, and a *Markov* chain says the chance of its next move depends only on the tray it is in now, not on how it got there. The table of those chances, one row per tray, is the transition matrix: row $i$, column $j$ answers "of the folders in tray $i$, what share went to tray $j$ next?" Twelve folders give the *announced* row directly: $4/12 \approx 0.33$ to *challenged*, $5/12 \approx 0.42$ into a court process, $3/12 = 0.25$ to the cooperative close. Add a calendar and each month a folder either stays or moves. A tray nobody leaves is *absorbing*, with a 1 on the diagonal; the cooperative close is absorbing here, which is the content of Lumen and Zayo. *A tiny worked number:* a folder that leaves the Chapter 11 tray with probability 0.10 a month stays $1/0.10 = 10$ months on average, a geometric waiting time. The honest caveat is that dockets have memory (Incora's 2025 reversal mattered because of its 2024 ruling), and splitting *ruled* from *reversed* is how the chain remembers. The full version, a filter that infers an unseen state, is §14.7.

Mags's second point was that the arrows move. A ruling in one case changes the odds on the arrows of every case whose document carries the same words, so Kaz wrote the transitions with the jumps of §13B.1:

$$
\operatorname{logit} p_{ij}(t) = a_{ij} + \sum_{r} J_{r,ij}\,\mathbf{1}\{t \ge t_r\},
$$

where $p_{ij}(t)$ is the monthly chance of moving from state $i$ to state $j$ at time $t$, $a_{ij}$ is its baseline log-odds, and $J_{r,ij}$ is the jump ruling $r$ induces on that arrow from its date $t_r$ onward. For a challenged "open market purchase" uptier, the arrow from *trial* to *ruled* $+1$ has a jump on 31 December 2024 and nothing before it. (Each row still sums to one, so in practice the jumps enter a multinomial logit across the row.) With $Q$ the transitions among transient states and $R$ those into the absorbing ones, the expected months spent in each transient state and the probabilities of each ending are

$$
N = (I - Q)^{-1}, \qquad B = N R .
$$

The row sums of $N$ are expected months to absorption, and each row of $B$ splits a starting state's future among the endings: a cooperative close, a plan, a liquidation.

![Figure 13-B.2: The litigation map as a Markov chain. Edge widths are transition counts across the twelve folders; the double-bordered state is the cooperative close. The time axis beneath marks the ruling dates of §13B.5, where the transition probabilities jump.](assets/figures/ch13b_litigation_markov.png)

Then Theo put two clocks beside the chain. The company's: among the six US filers, first deal to petition took 25, 9–13, 14, 9, 33 (16 from the second step) and 13 months, a median of about 13.5. The court's: transaction to the ruling that set the regime took 13, 15, 16, 22, 26, 27 and 54 months across seven cases, a median of 22. The European and private folders add a pact clock, first signed agreement to close, with a median of about 4.5 months.

"Twenty-two is bigger than thirteen and a half," said Kaz.

"That's the whole lesson of the room," said Mags. "The ruling arrives after the company has already filed. Courts reallocate the estate. They almost never prevent the filing." In the twelve folders the only ruling before any petition was Boardriders' motion to dismiss, and Boardriders never filed; it was sold. Mitel, in Chapter 14 §14.8.2, won its appeal and filed ten weeks later.

It is the cooperative close's in-court twin, and its convertible arithmetic is Chapter 23's.

The doctrine sits on two sides of one question. The defendants' line, from *Sharon Steel* and *United Airlines* as their briefs use them, is that creditors coordinating to enforce debt they already hold are not competitors fixing a price; the competition that antitrust protects happened when the debt was issued. The plaintiffs' line runs through *Catalano* (1980), where wholesalers who agreed to stop extending short-term credit to retailers were treated as fixing a price: credit terms are a price, and a group that blocks a borrower's access to new credit is setting one. Cleary Gottlieb's published view is that co-ops "should generally withstand antitrust scrutiny"; the LSTA, SIFMA and MFA filed as amici on the defendants' side.

## 13B.7 When the Autopsies Mislead

By eight Theo had a spreadsheet of twelve rows and nineteen rulings and the feeling, familiar from PJT, that he had built something persuasive. Ines took it apart in four moves.

The first was the sample. Every ruling comes from a case litigated to a decision; Boardriders and TriMark settled at par and vanish from the tally, as do the deals nobody sued over. Nine rulings for excluded holders against six, with four mixed, says which cases reach a judge, not the chance of winning. The second was dependence. The rows share judges, law firms and tokens, and *Serta* and *Mitel* landed the same day pulling opposite ways; nineteen rows are fewer than nineteen observations. The third was the two clocks: by the time a ruling lands, the filing has usually done the price's work, and a model that waits for the ruling has waited past the trade. The fourth was the sibling effect. A ruling shocks every document with the same words, so it is measured on *other* issuers' inside-minus-outside spreads from the day before to five days after (§14.6.1), and even then the announcement can beat the opinion. Lions Gate's 2029 notes were at 77.0 on 17 April 2025, 72.8 on 22 April after the arrangement was announced, and bid at 78.05 on the day British Columbia approved it over the holders' objection. The court did not move the price. The April announcement had.

"So what is the table good for?" said Theo.

"Ranking documents and dating jumps," said Ines. "Not for telling me the odds to two decimals."

> **Trend Watch.** *The form moved, not the frequency (2024–2026).* After the Fifth Circuit's *Serta* opinion, uptier components still appeared in 12 of 17 LMTs in the first quarter of 2025, and CreditSights found 43.4% of loans (44% of private-equity-backed loans) still carried the majority-subordination "Serta loophole" through Q1 2025 (courts dossier §2 and §11). By 2026 the modal deal had moved to amend-extend-exchange and co-op-negotiated extensions (Chapter 13's Trend Watch). A token map catches the words; substitutes need new tokens.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** Three, all from this room. *The feed without a label:* ROBO's 4,100 entries, faithfully parsed, contain no direction, and a model trained on entry counts learns that cases with more lawyers are worse, which is true and useless. *Settled as won:* a coder who labels Boardriders $+1$ because the excluded lenders were paid par has turned a sale into a ruling, and the jump fitted to it will fire the next time a judge denies a motion to dismiss. *Fitting jumps on nineteen rows:* a regression of price reactions on nineteen dependent ruling dates has more parameters than independent events, and its confident output is §13.8's separation problem wearing a robe.

Used in / builds on: §14.6 and §14.7 (the jumps and the filter these rows feed), Chapter 16 (excluded claims and releases after *Purdue*), Chapter 24 and Appendix D (the Altice auction and its deliverables), Chapter 25 §25.6–25.7 (dockets and Rule 2019 statements as data), Chapter 27 §27.3.3 (the Serta master-table rows), Chapter 28 (excluded-claim carry sizing), Appendix C (the dated rows), Appendix E (the court is a random variable with timestamps).

## 13B.8 Desk Debrief

- **The door beats the lien.** The same first lien was worth 74, 25 or 95 depending on which side of the deal it sat. Coalition status and a judge explain more of the excluded holder's P&L than seniority, leverage or rating.
- **A ruling is a dated jump.** Label it by direction for the excluded holder, keep all four timestamps, and add an elicited jump to the log-odds of every document carrying the same words.
- **Two clocks.** Deal to petition, about 13.5 months; transaction to the ruling that set the regime, 22. Courts reallocate estates; they rarely prevent filings.
- **The pair is the markout.** Long the likely participant, short the likely excluded: the hedge removes default and sector, and what remains is the seat and the judge. Both PB-06 and PB-08 closed on the excluded leg's markout, and both leaked time.
- **Exclusion is a bet on a sale or a ruling.** Boardriders and Lumen's orphans were paid near par after a sale; Serta's non-participants waited six years for a court.
- **Zayo is the false positive with a date on it.** A score that fires on every wall and pact will fire there, too.
- **Nineteen rulings are a diary, not a dataset.**

### Specification for AI Implementation: `docket_diary.py`

**The Spec You Hand the Robot.** What ROBO built from the vague version, "flag every ruling," was a list of 1,312 orders, of which 1,290 were scheduling orders, because a scheduling order is, literally, a ruling about the schedule. The precise version is smaller: keep the entries that decide something for excluded holders, each with a direction and up to four dates; map each case onto the states of §13B.4; count one-step transitions; and give, for any token and date, the excluded side's log-odds as a baseline plus the jumps of rulings on that token public by then.

It solves Theo's Monday problem: a feed of four thousand rows with no answer in any of them. It does not read dockets (Chapter 25 §25.6 builds the scraper); it starts after a human has labeled the rulings and turns those labels into the chapter's two objects, a transition-count table for the chain and a dated log-odds series per token. It is plain dataclasses and pure functions because the courts table changes by hand, a row at a time, and each number has to trace to one entry. Its inputs are `Entry` records (case, forum, kind, four optional dates, direction, tokens, the state the entry moves the case into, and an elicited jump); its outputs are a dictionary of case paths, a `Counter` of transitions, empirical row probabilities, and a log-odds for a token as of a date.

It earns its keep keeping the diary honest and feeding priors to the Hamilton filter of §14.7. It is the wrong tool for estimating jumps: nineteen rows hold nothing to estimate them from, and the function that sums jumps will happily sum numbers someone made up last week. The edge case that bites is the date. Oral, written, entry and first-public dates can differ by months (Incora) or land on a Saturday (*STG*), so the code takes the first public date and falls back in a fixed order, and one ruling is counted once. A settlement carries direction `None`, not a mixed ruling's `0`, and the filter keeps them apart.

### Exercises

1. **Replace the illustrative marks.** Pull TRACE prints for Level 3's 3.875% first-lien 2029s and one Lumen parent unsecured note for the five days around 10 November 2023 and 22 March 2024, and recompute PB-08's idio, net and capture ratio. *Solution sketch:* idio $= 15 \times \Delta P_{L3}/100 - 10 \times \Delta P_{\text{Lumen}}/100$ in \$mm; carry and spreads stay at about \$0.42mm. If the parent notes rose, the entity thesis lost although the seat wedge was real, and a capture ratio on negative idio is undefined.
2. **Fill a data gap.** For Envision (April and August 2022, 15 May 2023) and Incora (28 March 2022, 10 July 2024, 8 December 2025), build the excluded tranche's markout at $t-1$, $t+5$ and $t+20$ trading days. *Solution sketch:* Incora's notes are on TRACE; Envision's first lien is a loan and is not, so the markout needs a loan-mark vendor, and the two series are not comparable without saying how each was built. Noticing that is the exercise.
3. **Manufacture a majority.** A group holds 55% of a \$600mm class; majority consent can add pari passu notes, and releasing collateral needs two-thirds. How many new notes does the group need, and how few would erase a 34% blocking stake? *Solution:* $(330 + x)/(600 + x) = 2/3$ gives $x = \$210$mm; $600 \times (3 \times 0.34 - 1) = \$12$mm erases the block.
4. **Absorb a folder.** Monthly, an announced deal stays announced with probability 0.90, enters court with 0.06 and closes cooperatively with 0.04; a case in court stays with 0.92 and emerges with 0.08. From *announced*, how many months to absorption, and how often does it end cooperatively? *Solution:* $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0.90 & 0.06 \\ 0 & 0.92 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $N = (I-Q)^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 7.5 \\ 0 & 12.5 \end{pmatrix}$, so 17.5 months; $B = NR$ gives 0.40 cooperative and 0.60 through court.
5. **Kaz's Saturday.** Kaz proposes dropping weekend-dated rulings "as data errors." What does that do to the log-odds for *STG*-style wording on 12 January 2026? *Solution sketch:* it deletes a $+1$ jump public nine days before the petition, so the model enters the bankruptcy with stale odds; the fix is a `first_public_at` column, not a filter.
