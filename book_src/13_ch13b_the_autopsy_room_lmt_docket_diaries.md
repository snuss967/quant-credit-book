## Cold Open: Twelve Folders and a Blind That Does Not Close

*Friday, 16 October 2026, 5:35 p.m. The desk, its cast and ROBO are fictional. Every company in the folders is real, and every date on a tab comes from a court record, a filing or a named report.*

The small room with the blind that does not close is where the desk takes things apart on Fridays. The blind has been stuck at forty degrees since before Theo arrived, so the low October sun lays a stripe across the table, and tonight the stripe falls across twelve manila folders.

Mags Dunleavy squared the stack with both hands. Each tab carried a company name and, under it in her handwriting, a date: Revlon, Boardriders, Envision, Incora, Robertshaw, Lumen, Trinseo, Saks, Altice France, Thames Water, Zayo, Pluralsight. "House rule," she said. "Nobody reads past the date on the tab. Each folder stops where the market stopped knowing."

Theo had been given one job that week, and it had gone wrong in a way he was still deciding how to describe. On Monday he had asked ROBO to "build a docket feed for the LMT cases." ROBO had built one. For Incora's adversary proceeding alone it returned 4,100 entries: every motion to appear pro hac vice, every certificate of service, every notice of appearance and every amended exhibit list, each parsed into case number, entry number, date and a searchable description. Theo searched it. He could find the day each lawyer in Houston had joined the case. He could not find out who had won.

"It did exactly what I asked," he said. "There's no field for which way it went."

"There never is," said Mags. "A docket is the court's diary. It doesn't know it's your P&L."

Ines Okafor set her coffee down like a chip. "What's the pot, and who at this table read the clause?" She tapped the stack. "Twelve hands, and we know how every one of them ended. That's the trouble with autopsies. Everyone is brilliant about the dead. So we play them blind, up to the tab, and at each entry we write two things: what the score would have said that morning, and what it cost the holder who was left on the wrong side of the door."

Kaz Lind pulled the Incora folder toward them, opened it to the first page, and found a list of law firms. "Is it always this many lawyers?"

"In the good ones," said Mags.

Theo wrote the question at the top of his pad, because Ines would ask it again on Monday: *can a ruling be a feature, and can an exclusion be priced on the day the deal closes?*

By the end of this chapter you will be able to read a docket as a time series rather than a story; to label a ruling by its direction for the excluded holder and give it a log-odds jump you can defend; to say, for each of twelve real liability-management cases from 2020 to 2026, which Priming Risk Score features would have fired and when, and what the excluded holder's markout was; to recognize the pod pair hiding inside every one of them (long the likely participant, short the likely excluded) and explain why that pair's P&L is, in the end, the excluded leg's markout; and to write the litigation map down as a Markov chain whose transition matrix jumps on ruling dates. You will also know why most of this is less certain than it looks, which is the part the twelve folders teach best.

## 13B.1 How to Read a Docket

Theo pulled ROBO's 4,100 rows up on the wall screen and scrolled, slowly, so that the size of the thing could do the explaining. Kaz watched the descriptions go past.

"'Motion for admission pro hac vice,'" Kaz read. "'For this vice.' Which vice? Leverage?"

"'For this occasion,'" said Theo. "A lawyer from out of state, admitted for one case. Incora has dozens of them. Each one files a motion, gets an order, and somebody files a certificate saying everyone was served with both." He stopped scrolling. "Most of a docket is the court proving to itself that it told everyone."

He walked Kaz through where dockets live, because the twelve folders came from five different places. Federal cases, including every US bankruptcy here, sit on PACER, which charges by the page and has opinions about automated readers. New York's commercial cases, where Boardriders fought over "open market purchase," sit on the state's own electronic filing system. English judgments, Thames Water's among them, are published by the National Archives as Find Case Law. And Altice France's most important "rulings" were not made by a court at all. They were made by the EMEA Credit Derivatives Determinations Committee, which publishes its decisions as PDFs on its own website.

Then he pulled up one entry, the Incora bankruptcy judge's oral ruling of 10 July 2024, and showed Kaz that an entry has four clocks on it. It was *filed* when someone delivered paper to the court. It was *entered* when the clerk put it on the docket. It was *argued* on the hearing day. It was *decided* when the judge said or signed the decision, and for Incora those were two different days: an oral ruling from the bench on 10 July 2024 and a written report and recommendation on 17 January 2025. Serta had the same split a year earlier: a bench ruling for the borrower on 28 March 2023, and a written opinion after trial that June.

"Which one is the event?" said Kaz.

"The first moment the market could know," said Theo. "Sometimes that's a reporter in the courtroom. Sometimes it's a law firm's client alert the next morning. Almost never is it the entry date."

Then he added the column ROBO had not built: *direction*. The desk borrows its convention from the courts dossier behind this chapter. A ruling scores $+1$ if it favours the excluded or non-consenting holders, $-1$ if it favours the majority, the debtor or the people being released, and $0$ if it is mixed. Judge Isgur's 10 July 2024 ruling for the Incora minority is $+1$. The district court's reversal of 8 December 2025 is $-1$. The English Court of Appeal's Thames Water judgment, which upheld the plan but refused any "hard-edged rule" that out-of-the-money creditors could simply be ignored, is $0$.

"So the label is one number per ruling," said Kaz. "Then what's the feature?"

"The feature is what the ruling does to the odds," said Ines, from the door.

> **Math, Gently.** *Odds, log-odds, and a ruling as a jump.* A probability of 0.5 is odds of 1 to 1; 0.8 is odds of 4 to 1. The log-odds, or *logit*, is the natural log of the odds: $\operatorname{logit}(p) = \ln\big(p/(1-p)\big)$. At 0.5 it is 0; at 0.8 it is about 1.39; it runs from minus infinity to plus infinity while $p$ stays between 0 and 1. That is why the desk adds a ruling's effect on this scale and not on probability itself: you can add any number to a log-odds and convert back, $p = 1/(1+e^{-\text{logit}})$, and you will always get a legal probability. Add 0.3 to a probability of 0.9 and you get nonsense. Picture a dial with no end stops, wired to a needle that can only move between 0 and 1; turning the dial a click moves the needle a lot near the middle and barely at all near the ends. *A tiny worked number.* Suppose that, before the Fifth Circuit's *Serta* opinion of 31 December 2024, a reader gives the excluded lenders an even chance in an "open market purchase" dispute: $p = 0.5$, log-odds 0. The reader then reads the opinion and assigns it a jump of $+1.0$ for the excluded side *(illustrative)*. The new log-odds is 1.0 and the new probability is $1/(1+e^{-1}) \approx 0.73$. The same click from a starting point of 0.9 lands at about 0.96. Written for the desk's variable $\theta_w$, the chance that a priming deal under wording $w$ *survives* challenge, the same ruling is a jump of $-1.0$, from 0.5 to 0.27. The full version, with one jump per ruling and per wording class, is §14.6.1.

The jump is a judgment, and the folder records whose judgment it is and on what day. Nineteen rulings are not enough to fit anything, which is the subject of §13B.7. They are enough to keep a disciplined diary.

That left the two lines Ines wanted at the bottom of every folder. Theo wrote them on the whiteboard as headings, and they close each diary below.

The first is *PRS flags*: which features of Chapter 13's Priming Risk Score would have fired, and on what date. Some are already in the §13.2.1 vector: `loan_px`, `runway_months`, `holder_hhi`, `coop_reported`, `unsub_capacity_pct_ev`, `buyback_exception`, `sponsor_repeat_lme`, `integration_gap`. Some are new, and the folders will propose them as they go.

The second is *Excluded markout*: what happened to the price of the tranche left outside the door, set against the tranche that went through it.

> **Math, Gently.** *A markout is an average of what happened next, given who you were.* Ines calls a markout "the hand history." For a trade, it is the price some time after the trade minus the price at the trade, averaged over many trades: the question it answers is whether the other side knew something. Here the "trade" is a liability-management deal, and the thing that sorts the hands is participation status. Write $P_{t_0}$ for the excluded tranche's price on the last day before the deal became knowable and $P_{t_0+h}$ for its price $h$ later. The excluded markout is the conditional expectation $M^{\text{excl}}(h) = \mathbb{E}\big[P_{t_0+h} - P_{t_0} \mid \text{excluded}\big]$, and the participant's is the same with the condition flipped. *A tiny worked number.* Two holders own the same first-lien loan at 70 *(illustrative)*. One is invited, swaps into super-priority paper and marks 90 a month later; the other is not, and marks 40. Same loan, same lien, same company: markouts of $+20$ and $-30$, a coalition gap of 50 points. Twelve folders do not give an expectation. They give a dozen dated points, from different seats, measured over different horizons, mostly from vendor quotes rather than prints. The full version, for dealer trades, is Chapter 2-B and Chapter 18.

"There's one more reason to care," said Ines. "Dov's pod across town runs these as pairs." Every folder that follows names the pair a pod would have put on: long the paper likely to be inside the deal and short the paper likely to be left out, or long the operating company and short the holding company, hedged so that default and the sector cancel. Ines had read enough of the Vantress Pair Book to see the pattern before the folders proved it. Once the hedge has removed default and sector, what is left is participation status plus a judge. The pair's P&L is the excluded leg's markout, with a sign on it.

Used in / builds on: §12B.4 (the menu of out-of-court moves, here as observed transitions), §13.2.1 and §13.8 (the PRS vector these flags come from), §14.6.1 (the log-odds jump, in full), Chapter 25 §25.6 (the docket as an event stream, where the feed ROBO built gets rebuilt properly), Appendix E (the court is a random variable with timestamps).

## 13B.2 The US Diaries, in Deal Order

Mags dealt the US folders in the order the deals were done, not the order they died, because the order they were done is the order the market learned. Each one gets the same treatment: a diary of dated entries, about a page long, then three lines in Theo's hand. Ines read the entries aloud and stopped at each date to ask what the room would have done with only that much paper.

> **Docket Diary.** *Revlon BrandCo, 7 May 2020 to 3 April 2023.* **7 May 2020:** Revlon signs a BrandCo credit agreement with Jefferies as agent: \$815mm of new-money term loans and a \$950mm roll-up commitment that lets lenders in the 2016 term loan, a \$1.8bn facility with Citibank as agent, swap at par into new 2025 paper. Elizabeth Arden and other brand intellectual property move to BrandCo subsidiaries that do not guarantee the 2016 loan; Revlon delivers a solvency certificate. By the end of May, \$846mm of 2016 loans have rolled up. **August 2020:** the 2016 loan trades at "20 to 30" cents, in the Second Circuit's later words. **11 August 2020:** Citibank, as agent, wires about \$894mm of the 2016 lenders' principal by mistake; that story is Chapter 27's (§27.4, the Long Scene "The default field"), and it gets one sentence here. **12 August 2020:** excluded 2016 lenders authorize UMB Bank to sue, alleging insolvency "by as much as \$1.71 billion"; the complaint is never served and is dismissed without prejudice on 6 November. **15 June 2022:** Chapter 11 in the Southern District of New York. **21 February 2023:** an amended restructuring support agreement adds 2016 lenders holding over 94% of that loan. **3 April 2023:** plan confirmed. As summarized by debtor's counsel and press reports, BrandCo B-2 lenders take 82% of the equity and of a \$670mm rights offering, 2016 and B-3 lenders 18%, and non-electing 2016 and B-3 lenders share a \$56mm cash pool; the 2016 litigation is resolved "conclusively and consensually." *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel: "We note for the record that we were not wired anything. We note it with regret."*
>
> *PRS flags:* capacity to move intellectual property outside the guarantor group (`unsub_capacity_pct_ev`, the basket triangle of Chapter 25 §25.3) and room for a par roll-up offered to a subset, both readable in the 2016 agreement before May 2020; `loan_px` only afterwards. *Excluded markout:* the 2016 loan at 20–30 within three months of the deal, behind roll-up paper exchanged at par; in the plan, a minority share of equity and rights alongside B-3 lenders, or a slice of a \$56mm pool. *Pod pair:* long BrandCo paper, short the 2016 loan. Nobody lends a term loan, so the short is a forward sale re-lit every settlement cycle, and what it isolates is one question: where did the brands go?

Kaz read the flags line twice. "The capacity was in the document for four years. Why did the score wait?"

"It didn't have to," said Theo. "`unsub_capacity_pct_ev` was there the day the agreement was signed. What changed in 2020 was `runway_months`. A basket is an option the sponsor holds. The runway is what tells you it's about to be exercised."

Mags grunted, which in her register is agreement. "Back in my day we found out about the basket when the lawyer for the other side read it to us at the closing. Then we argued about it for three years, and the company paid both of us." She tapped the folder. "This is better. Now the closing reads it to you in May, and you argue with a spreadsheet."

> **View From Another Seat.** *The dealer as agent.* In the Revlon folder a dealer appears twice, and neither time as a trader. Jefferies is the agent on the new BrandCo facility; Citibank is the agent on the 2016 loan whose collateral walked. An agent bank earns a fee for administering a loan and owes its lenders the mechanics: notices, payments, the register of who holds what. A dealer desk at the same firm sees the LMT as a financing event: new paper to place, an existing loan whose holders are about to want bids. The same pattern recurs in the Saks folder below, where Jefferies was initial purchaser on the 2024 notes the 2025 exchange later subordinated (§13B.2, entry 8). For the systematic desk the agent's seat is a source of timestamps. The register, the amendment postings and the payment notices are the agent's work product, and Robertshaw below shows what happens when an amendment is never posted. Chapter 27 §27.4 tells what happened when Revlon's agent pressed the wrong field.

> **Docket Diary.** *Boardriders, August 2020 to September 2023.* **April 2018:** credit agreement; about \$450mm of first-lien debt, held by Oaktree (also the sponsor), by lenders who will later participate, and by lenders who will not. **August 2020:** \$110mm of new super-priority money; \$321mm of participants' loans rolled up at par into equal super-priority debt, \$431mm in all. Majority amendments strip the debt covenant and the requirement that any intercreditor agreement be junior or pari passu, and the roll-up is papered as "open market purchase" agreements. Non-consenting lenders, about \$85mm, go to the back. Their loans trade at a 40–50% discount. **Late September 2020:** S&P raises the rating from SD to CCC and says the deal was "tantamount to default." **October 2020:** ICG, York and others sue in New York Supreme Court. **17 October 2022:** Justice Masley denies the motion to dismiss the breach and implied-covenant claims: the "open market" exception is ambiguous and the no-action clause does not bar suit. The tortious-interference claim against Oaktree is dismissed. **November 2022:** Boardriders appeals. **August 2023:** the parties announce a settlement tied to Authentic Brands' purchase of Boardriders; non-participants are repaid in full. **September 2023:** dismissal with prejudice. Authentic closed around 1 September at an undisclosed price (Moody's cited \$1.25bn, Bloomberg about \$1.3bn). *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel: "We were paid par. We would like it known that we always believed in the brand."*
>
> *PRS flags:* `buyback_exception` (an undefined "open market purchase" in the pro rata sharing carve-out), a sponsor that is also a lender (`holder_hhi`, with the sponsor's affiliate inside the majority), and the rating tell, SD then CCC, which Chapter 13's vector does not yet carry. *Excluded markout:* about 50–60 at the deal; par on the sale, three years later. *Pod pair:* long the super-priority paper, short the excluded loan, which is what everybody wanted on the day of the deal and which lost, in the end, roughly the excluded leg's 40-to-50-point recovery.

"'Open market purchase,'" said Mags. "The most expensive three words in finance. Here they were the cheapest. A judge said she couldn't tell what they meant, and not knowing was worth par."

Theo was stuck on the pair. "So the trade everybody wanted on the day of the deal was a short of something that went to par."

"The trade everybody wanted on the day of the deal was a short of a lawsuit and a sale process," said Ines. "Boardriders' excluded lenders didn't win a ruling. They won the right to keep arguing, and a buyer arrived who preferred paying them to finding out." She wrote it on the whiteboard where everyone could see it: *exclusion is a bet on a sale or a ruling, not a stable recovery*. TriMark, a month later in 2020 with \$120mm of new money and \$307.5mm rolled up, settled in January 2022 by moving the excluded lenders into the super-priority tranche at par. Serta's non-participants, who had neither a buyer nor a settlement, received 0.6–2.4% in the plan.

> **Docket Diary.** *Envision Healthcare, April 2022 to 3 November 2023; and 17 June 2025.* **April 2022:** a drop-down. A new AmSurg first-lien facility of about \$1.3bn sends about \$1.1bn of proceeds up to Envision, and AmSurg, the ambulatory-surgery business, is designated an unrestricted subsidiary, taking about 83% of the ambulatory-surgery EBITDA outside the old lenders' reach, by later accounts. **June–August 2022:** an uptier. AmSurg raises about \$1.3bn of second-lien debt and lends it to Envision, which repurchases about \$1.9bn of its own debt at a discount, capturing roughly \$582mm. Participants are prioritized; the rest go to the back, to what lenders came to call the "fourth-out." Octus puts participation near 91%. S&P's reported actions took the rating to selective default and then back to CCC. **November 2022:** by one widely circulated account citing market prices, AmSurg debt near 98 and Envision's first lien near 25, against 73 for that first lien in March. **15 May 2023:** Chapter 11 in the Southern District of Texas, Judge Lopez, two silos and no DIP. **31 July 2023:** a dissenting lender group sues on unanimous-consent grounds; dismissed with prejudice on 9 November. **15 September 2023:** an independent director reports about \$1.7bn of quantified benefit from the transactions and no fraud. **11 October 2023:** plan confirmed; effective 3 November. At AmSurg the revolver and first lien are paid in full and the second-lien holders take the equity, with a projected recovery of 73%. At Envision the second-out lenders take the equity; the unsecured notes get warrants. **17 June 2025:** Ascension agrees to buy AmSurg for a reported \$3.9bn, about \$250mm above the top of the plan valuation; Octus estimates more than 1.3 times money for the drop-down lenders. *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel, asks which silo it is in, and is told both.*
>
> *PRS flags:* `unsub_capacity_pct_ev`, firing the day of the designation in April 2022 and readable before it; non-pro-rata repurchase capacity (`buyback_exception`); the SD-then-CCC rating tell. *Excluded markout:* Envision first lien from 73 to about 25 in eight months on that account, while AmSurg debt sat near 98: a gap of about 73 points inside one corporate family, before anyone filed. *Pod pair:* long AmSurg debt, short Envision first lien, which removes the healthcare-services factor and the sponsor and isolates one question: which silo the surgical EBITDA lives in.

Ines held up the November 2022 numbers. "Ninety-eight and twenty-five. Same sponsor, same industry, same management team, one org chart. What's a spread model's beta between those two loans?"

"High," said Kaz, "until April 2022."

"And after?"

Kaz thought about it honestly. "After, the beta is a question about a box on an org chart. Nothing in the return series would have told me the box was going to move." They wrote *beta is a document variable* under Marcus's line from Chapter 13, *seniority is a field with a date on it*, and looked pleased with the pair.

> **Docket Diary.** *Incora (Wesco Aircraft), 28 March 2022 to the Fifth Circuit.* The four steps of 28 March 2022 are told in §13.9, and the diary only dates what followed. **February 2022:** a minority group announces more than one-third of the 2026 secured notes, blocking the majority's planned two-thirds; the 2026 notes, by one secondary account, run from about 85 to above par as both sides chase blocking positions. **28 March 2022:** the one-day transaction closes. **October 2022:** state-court suit. **1 June 2023:** Chapter 11 in the Southern District of Texas, with an adversary proceeding filed the same day. **January–July 2024:** a 35-day trial. **10 July 2024:** oral ruling from Judge Isgur: no breach of the 2024 indenture, a breach of the 2026 indenture by a "domino agreement" that needed two-thirds, liens on all 2026 notes survive. Direction $+1$. **27 December 2024:** a consensual plan confirmed on the strength of the rulings, appellate rights preserved. **17 January 2025:** written report and recommendation: the 2026 liens are restored, and the majority's \$250mm of new notes is only an unsecured claim. **31 January 2025:** emergence; secured noteholders take 98.4% of the equity and \$420mm of take-back notes. **8 December 2025:** Chief Judge Crane of the district court reverses: each step complied with its own words, a single-day closing "is not unusual," and the remedy would in any case be damages, since rescission would be "plainly inequitable." Direction $-1$. **About 6 January 2026:** the minority noteholders are reported to have noticed an appeal to the Fifth Circuit. No Fifth Circuit ruling had been reported by the date on the tab. *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel, declines to say which Ad Hoc Group it is.*
>
> *PRS flags:* a *dilutable threshold*, a new feature: majority consent can issue pari passu notes beside a two-thirds test for anything having "the effect of" releasing collateral. A group with 59% of \$900mm reaches two-thirds once new notes $x$ satisfy $(531 + x)/(900 + x) \ge 2/3$, so $x \approx \$207$mm, below the \$250mm raised; `integration_gap` $= 2/3 - 0.59 \approx 0.077$. And a *blocking-position run*: abnormal buying of a distressed secured tranche toward par. *Excluded markout:* secured on 10 July 2024 and on 17 January 2025, unsecured behind about \$1.7bn of new debt on 8 December 2025, and an appeal pending; no TRACE path was available to the folder. *Pod pair:* there was no clean one. The likely participant and the likely excluded held the same bond, distinguished only by who held it, and a pod without a seat could only be long or short the bond's average holder.

"That's the first folder where the pair doesn't exist," said Theo.

"It's the first folder where you can see why," said Ines. "The excluded leg and the included leg were the same CUSIP. The difference was the name on the register. You can't borrow a name."

Kaz was doing the dilution arithmetic again, on the edge of the folder. "Two hundred and seven million dollars of new notes turns 59% into two-thirds. A blocking third is erased by much less if it's thin. A stake of 34% of the \$900mm class needs only $900 \times (3 \times 0.34 - 1) = 18$ million of new paper to fall under one-third."

"Which is why the 2026s ran to par," said Mags. "Everybody was buying the same vote."

> **View From Another Seat.** *The rescue financier.* In the Incora record the majority wore two hats. The district court described the majority group as led by funds managed or advised by PIMCO and Silver Point, the two largest holders of the 2024 and 2026 notes, and the same funds had proposed the rescue financing the company needed in December 2021, when its UK auditors wanted \$150–200mm injected (*Wesco Aircraft Holdings v. SSD Investments*, S.D. Tex., 8 December 2025). Seen from that seat, the transaction is a loan to a company nobody else would lend to, priced partly in priority. The minority's competing offers, about \$147.5mm and then \$173mm, against the majority's \$250mm, are the auction the board ran. The systematic desk reads this as a state variable: when the holder with the vote is also the likeliest lender of last resort, the probability that the next dollar of money arrives non-pro-rata rises, and the outsider's recovery variance with it (Chapter 13 §13.4, the sponsor's knapsack).

> **Docket Diary.** *Robertshaw, 9 May 2023 to 16 August 2024.* **9 May 2023:** a New York-law super-priority credit agreement: \$95mm of first-out new money, existing first- and second-lien loans exchanged into second-out and third-out, signed with an ad hoc group (Bain Capital Credit, Canyon, Eaton Vance) and Invesco. "Required Lender" means more than 50% of first-out plus second-out loans, read from the agent's register. **July 2023:** Invesco quietly buys past 50% of first-out plus second-out, mostly through the first-out, and becomes Required Lender. **October–November 2023:** interest is missed; Amendments 1 to 4 extend the grace period, add \$17mm of bridge money, set a Chapter 11 deadline and install an independent director chosen by Invesco. Invesco directs the agent not to post them, and the ad hoc group learns of them around 15 November. **11 December 2023:** the ad hoc group and the sponsor, One Rock, lend \$228.3mm to RS Funding, a vehicle the parent votes and Robertshaw economically owns; the cash repays the \$30mm ABL and prepays \$117.6mm of first-out loans plus a \$30.7mm make-whole. Invesco falls below 50%, the ad hoc group becomes Required Lender and signs Amendment 5. **Late December 2023:** Invesco sues in New York. **15 February 2024:** Chapter 11, Southern District of Texas, Judge Lopez. **20 June 2024:** the court holds the ad hoc group were the Required Lenders and Amendment 5 valid; Robertshaw itself breached by not sending all net proceeds to RS Funding; the remedy is a money claim. Direction $-1$. **16 August 2024:** a liquidating plan is confirmed over Invesco's objection, with an \$11mm unsecured pool and a \$10mm deficiency pool. *The Ad Hoc Group, through counsel, confirms that it was the Required Lender on every day it said so.*
>
> *PRS flags:* *Required Lender fragility*, a new feature: the control majority rests on a tranche the borrower can prepay, so the vote can be bought back with someone else's money; amendments that do not appear on the agent's posting site (observable only by their absence, and only afterwards); `runway_months`, after a missed coupon in the autumn. *Excluded markout:* Invesco's claim of about \$40mm, against the debtors' estimate of about 3% recovery for that class; about nine months from the first deal to the petition. *Pod pair:* none tradable. The thing that changed hands was a vote, and the vote turned out to be prepayable.

"Control is a long position in the denominator," said Kaz, pleased with it.

"Control was a long position in a denominator the other side could shrink," said Theo. "Invesco bought the majority. The ad hoc group bought it back by paying off part of it." He wrote the new feature in the margin, `rl_prepayable_share`: the fraction of the Required Lender majority held in tranches the borrower can prepay. Then he wrote, smaller, *damages against a bankrupt borrower*, because that was where a won argument had ended up: an unsecured claim, at about three cents.

The sixth folder was thicker than the others and had a second document clipped to the front: two pages from Marcus Oyelaran at Vantress, the platform's own write-up of a pair Dov's pod had run against it. Ines had asked for it on the condition that she could grade it.

> **Docket Diary.** *Lumen Technologies and Level 3, 31 October 2023 to 22 March 2024, with an aftermath to 20 May 2026.* **Before:** about \$10bn of Lumen and Level 3 debt falls due in 2027. **31 October 2023:** a transaction support agreement with holders of "over \$7 billion," including \$1.2bn of new Level 3 first-lien money. **Week of 9 November 2023:** CreditSights quotes non-TSA paper: Level 3's 3.875% first-lien 2029s at 73, its unsecured 2028s at 47, Lumen's own unsecured notes in the high 20s, and a gap of about ten points between the same paper inside and outside the agreement; about 49% of the Level 3 unsecured class is to be primed by the other half. **22 January 2024:** the amended agreement covers "over \$12.5 billion." Term-loan pieces are open to all; note pieces are privately negotiated and "not offered to all holders." Transfers to non-parties are void. **22 March 2024:** closing.
