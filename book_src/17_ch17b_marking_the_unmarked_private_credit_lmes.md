## 17B.0 Cold Open: The Hedge That Wasn't Short Software

*(From the pod's history: Wednesday 4 February 2026. The pod, its explain, its sizes and every P&L figure are fictional and illustrative; OBDC, HYG, the BIS figures and the dated events are real.)*

At 7:10 on Wednesday 4 February 2026 the morning explain for Dov Adler's pod arrives the way it always does, as a PDF nobody requested and everybody reads: fourteen pairs, a page each, every page split into the four line groups of Chapter 8-C, the market line, the leak, the friction and the idio. Dov reads it standing, on the theory that sitting down implies the news is good enough to take seriously.

PB-16 is page eleven. It is three months old and, on paper, the quietest pair in the book: long \$20mm of Blue Owl Capital Corporation's shares, ticker OBDC, bought about 14% below the net asset value the manager reports; short \$26mm of HYG, the high-yield bond ETF, borrowed through the prime broker. The thesis carries a date, as all of Dov's do: *OBDC's discount says the marks will fall further than the public data says they will; the overshoot narrows by August 2026* (§17.3, the PB-16 box).

Tuesday's page reads like a typo. OBDC down 4.8%, HYG down 0.15%. The long lost \$960,000 and the short made \$39,000. The market line is close to zero, so the hedge did its job: 1.3 times a 0.15% fall in HYG is about 0.2% on the long, and the short paid for exactly that. The other \$920,000 the explain has dropped into the line labeled *idio*.

"That isn't idio," Dov says, to nobody in particular, which in the pod means Wen.

Wen Zhao already has the borrow sheet open, because when a short misbehaves the first suspect is a lender who wants the bonds back. Nothing has moved: HYG is general collateral, no recall, the fee unchanged. "The weather's fine. Your short is exactly where you put it."

The trade press has the rest. New AI tools launched days earlier automated the kind of legal, sales and data work that software companies sell; software equities had been falling since, and the lenders to software companies went with them. Chapter 17 has the trade-press percentages (§17.3, Beat 3), and the BIS's later count was software equities down almost 30% from October to February against about 10% for BDC shares. OBDC is one of the large lenders to software. HYG holds the bonds of companies that mostly make, mine, drill, lease or broadcast things.

"So the long is a software lender," Dov says, "and the short is a basket of companies that sell almost anything else."

"The ledger said so on the first day." Wen reads the third item in PB-16's leak column aloud, in the voice the pod reserves for reading people's earlier statements back to them: *the proxy shorts the wrong thing: HYG holds public high-yield bonds, a different mix from middle-market software loans, so a software shock can hit the long and miss the short.*

"Written down isn't hedged." Dov crosses out *idio* on the printout and writes *software* above it.

At 7:31 Marcus Oyelaran's overnight x-ray arrives (Chapter 8-B) with the question it always asks: *What is the book long that it did not mean to be?* There is no orange line on PB-16 yet. The risk model's software factor is built from public stocks and bonds, and OBDC's loading on it was fitted over 2025, when software lenders and software stocks moved together about as much as anything did, which was not much.

> **Math, Gently.** *A hedge that shorts the wrong thing.* A pair's return is the long's return minus the hedge ratio times the short's. Suppose the long responds to two things, high-yield spreads and software, and the short responds only to high-yield spreads. With loadings of 1.3 on high yield and 0.6 on software (both illustrative), and the short sized at 1.3, the pair's return is $(1.3-1.3)\times$ the high-yield move, plus $0.6\times$ the software move, plus the long's own noise. The hedge erases the first term. It cannot touch the second, because there is no software in the short. Over a calm year in which the software factor moved about 1% a month, the second term was worth about 0.6% a month, invisible beside the noise of a listed stock, so the risk model rated it small. Then the software factor falls 8% in a day, and the same term is $0.6\times(-8\%)=-4.8\%$ on the long and zero on the short. The covariance was there all along: small on paper because the window was quiet, large on the day because the day was not. The full version, with the hedge matrix, is §8.6; the comparison of rulers is §8D.3.

In the book's notation, with factor returns $F_{k,t}$, loadings $\beta_{i,k}$ and idiosyncratic returns $\epsilon_{i,t}$,

$$
r^{\text{pair}}_t = r^{L}_t - h\,r^{S}_t = \big(\beta_{L,\text{HY}}-h\,\beta_{S,\text{HY}}\big)F_{\text{HY},t} + \beta_{L,\text{SW}}\,F_{\text{SW},t} + \epsilon_{L,t}-h\,\epsilon_{S,t},
$$

and a hedge sized at $h=\beta_{L,\text{HY}}/\beta_{S,\text{HY}}$ zeroes the first bracket and leaves the software term whole. (Here $h$ is Chapter 8-D's hedge ratio, not §17.6's haircut.)

Dov does not unwind. A thesis about marks is not wrong because software had a bad Tuesday, and selling a discounted asset into a sector panic is the trade the thesis was written to take the other side of. Under the thesis on the whiteboard he writes the date and four words, *4 Feb 2026: the leak is the P&L*, then two questions. The first is this chapter's: *what can a desk that trades only public things know about a private mark before the mark moves?* The second is shorter and worse: *if the long is really long the mark's lag, what is short the mark?* Section 17B.5 answers it, and the answer is the pair's epitaph.

*Card. 7:40 a.m., Wednesday 4 February 2026. Write three lines before reading on: what PB-16 is long that it did not mean to be; whether you add a short, and in what; and what would have to be true by August for the thesis to pay. The cards are graded in §17B.7.*

Chapter 17 named the features a desk can compute on the schedules of investments, wrote down the queue's recursion and opened PB-16. This chapter fits the features, tests them on the borrowers whose stories are public enough to test on, estimates the queue from the funds' own filings, and follows the pair through the spring. By the end of it you will be able to read one private loan across every public vehicle that holds it, with the valuers clustered; put a default or non-accrual number on a stated basis; separate a redemption series into new belief and last quarter's line coming back, with the censoring in the likelihood; build and evaluate a multi-state hazard model from PIK amendments to the keys; and show, with arithmetic, why nothing a pod can trade is short a private mark.

> **Math, Gently.** *How many valuers is seven?* Seven marks of one loan look like seven opinions, and averaging seven independent opinions cuts the noise by about $\sqrt7\approx2.6$. Suppose instead that every pair of valuers shares an anchor, so their errors have a correlation $\rho$. Then seven marks are worth $n_{\text{eff}}=7/\big(1+6\rho\big)$ independent ones: at $\rho=0.5$, 1.75. Seven committees, fewer than two opinions. If two of the seven are the same committee, as the two Blackstone vehicles in the Medallia ledger are (§17.3.3), the count starts at six before the correlation applies. The arithmetic also runs backwards, which is the useful direction: when valuers anchor on each other, a disagreement that survives the anchoring is unlikely to be noise. A 7.8-point gap among herding valuers is worth more than the same gap among independent ones. The full version, with clustered standard errors and the design effect, is §7-B.1 and §7-B.6.

## 17B.2 PIK as a Hidden Default, Measured

#### Quant-Shop Life: The Reconciliation That Would Not Tie

*(A fictional composite of month-end on a systematic credit desk. The desk, its people, tickets and dialogue are invented; every BDC figure is from the company releases and filings cited, and the arithmetic on them is the desk's.)*

The last day of September 2026 is a Wednesday, so it is month-end, so by four o'clock every number on page nine of the risk committee's pack has to agree with its source. Page nine is new: the share of each large BDC's loans on non-accrual, at cost and at fair value, over four quarters. Theo volunteered to own it, on the theory that a man who has reconciled a claims register to a docket can reconcile anything. Kaz wrote the code, on the theory that anything Theo reconciles by hand will one day need a machine.

The desk's number comes from the `soi` table of §17.8, every facility in every schedule of investments with the schedule's non-accrual marker. At 10:15 it matches none of seven releases. The misses are small and boring, the worst kind, because small boring misses get waved through and become the number. One release divides by total investments including equity; the desk divides by debt. Another reports first-lien non-accruals in a separate table, and the parser read only one table. A third flags a loan in a footnote instead of beside its row. By 1:40 six of the seven tie within a tenth of a point. By 2:20 Theo has a list of loans held by more than one BDC and finds what he half expected: loans on non-accrual at one holder and accruing at another, in the same quarter.

Kaz looks at the list the way a model looks at data with opinions. "So non-accrual is a legal event? Like an event of default under the credit agreement?"

"It's an accounting decision," Theo says. "The lender stops booking interest it no longer expects to collect. The credit agreement can say the borrower is fine and the lender can still decide it doesn't believe the borrower."

"So the default is whenever the CFO says so?"

"Welcome to private credit," Mags says from two chairs down, without looking up.

Octus measured the same thing at scale: adjusting sector non-accruals for loans held by several BDCs raised them from 1.45% to 2.18% of cost (Octus, 16 April 2026; §17.3.3). A loan one committee has stopped believing in and another still accrues is §17B.1's dispersion in a second unit, a flag instead of a price, and it enters the detector of §17B.4 the same way, per valuer, clustered.

> **Math, Gently.** *A ratio of two rulers.* Every BDC reports its non-accrual loans twice: as a share of the portfolio at cost, and as a share at fair value. If the bad loans are 2.8% of cost but only 0.8% of fair value, the manager carries them at roughly the ratio of the two shares, $0.8/2.8\approx0.29$, times the portfolio's fair value over cost (about 0.98 here), so about 28 cents on the dollar. Two rulers, one bucket, and the ratio between the readings is a mark the release never prints. That is OBDC's June 2026 quarter, derived in full in §17.3.3. What matters for a series is the drift: a falling fair-value share beside a rising cost share means new bad loans arriving already marked down hard, so the headline improves while the hole deepens.

## 17B.3 The Queue, Estimated

*(Theo's desk, Thursday 15 October 2026. The desk, its dialogue, ROBO's output and the nowcasts are fictional and illustrative. Every request, fill, backlog and quoted phrase is from the funds' Schedule TO-I/A filings and letters, the Federal Reserve or the IMF, or from a tracker or press report where the text says so; "computed" marks the desk's arithmetic.)*

The week before, Kaz had asked ROBO to "forecast redemptions for the non-traded BDCs." ROBO returned every fund, every quarter through 2028, at 5.0%. It had fitted the shares-repurchased line of each fund's cash-flow statement and observed, correctly, that almost every recent number was the cap. It forecast the fills, was right about the fills, and learned nothing about how many people wanted to leave, because its series could not show a number above five.

Theo spends Thursday building the series that can. A non-traded BDC's tender is filed as a Schedule TO-I; the fund amends it as the offer runs, and the final amendment reports shares tendered, shares accepted and the proration factor. By eleven he has thirty final amendments open in thirty tabs.

"It's a docket," he says.

"It's a tender offer," says Kaz.

"Numbered entries, filed with a public authority, in order, each amending the last, and the final one says who got paid how much. And the 10-Q is the claims register: every holder of a loan files its claim each quarter in a schedule anyone can read." He reaches the sentence he has been circling since Danny's story. "The restructuring I used to run from a data room is all here, late and in pieces, filed by seven lenders who disagree with each other. And the disagreement is the signal."

That is the chapter's aha, and it arrives with a filing index. The private market has no tape, but every large private loan sits in several public vehicles and every semi-liquid vehicle publishes what its holders asked for. The trail is late, quarterly and written by people with reasons to write it a particular way, all of which is also true of a bankruptcy docket.

> **Math, Gently.** *A censored series.* Picture a bathroom scale that stops at five. Anyone lighter shows their weight; anyone heavier shows five. Average the readings and you badly underestimate how heavy people are, and if the last three days all read five, a model trained on them predicts five forever. That is ROBO's table. A fund's repurchases are the scale: below the cap they show demand exactly, above it they show the cap. Seeing past five takes a second instrument, and funds supply one when they report requests or a proration factor. OTIC, Blue Owl's software-focused fund, reported requests for 40.4% of its shares in the first-quarter cycle of 2026 and a fill of 14.4%, so it repurchased about 5.8% and left about 34.6% of its shares in line (computed). A forecast built on the 5.8% says next quarter looks like this one. A forecast built on the 34.6% says most of next quarter's requests are already known. Law 14 has the likelihood that uses both readings at once.

Theo's second table is the one his thirteen-week model has wanted since Chapter 1: opening balance, arrivals, amount served, closing balance. The model refuses to die again, this time as a queue. The arrival rate is new requests; the service rate is the cap, 5% of shares a quarter, and what service costs the fund in cash is the cap less new subscriptions, in the row that used to say *revolver draws*; the backlog is the line. Kaz recognizes §17.6.2's recursion and points out that it takes the arrivals as an input. Theo's job is to recover them.

In Chapter 17's notation, $a_t$ is the share of shares tendered in cycle $t$, $f_t$ the fill and $p_t=a_tf_t$ the share repurchased, which is $\min(a_t,c)$ when the cap $c$ is applied as written. With $\xi$ the share of last cycle's unfilled requests that come back and $\iota_t$ the shares issued between offers as a fraction of last cycle's shares, the returning line and the joiners are

$$
b_t=\xi\,\frac{a_{t-1}-p_{t-1}}{1-p_{t-1}+\iota_t},
\qquad
a^{\text{new}}_t=\frac{a_t-b_t}{1-b_t}.
$$

The second is §17.6.2's $a^{\text{new}}$, the fraction of holders not already in line who joined it, recovered from published numbers instead of assumed. With $\xi=1$ and $\iota_t=0$ every prorated holder resubmits and nobody subscribes, so $a^{\text{new}}_t$ is a lower bound on new demand. North Haven's fund lets the bound be checked: 11.6% requested and 43.0% filled in the second cycle, 11.4% requested in the third, so with $\xi=1$ resubmissions would be about 61% of the third cycle (computed). Its letter says nearly two thirds were. On the one fund that publishes both sides, the assumption is almost right.

> **Math, Gently.** *How long is the line, really?* A queue has three numbers: how fast people arrive, how fast they are served, and how many are waiting. If arrivals are slower than service the line drains; if faster, it grows until someone changes the rules. Little's law links the three on average: the typical wait is the length of the line divided by the rate at which it is served. OTIC after its first cycle had about 35% of its shares in line (34.6%, computed) and served 5% a quarter, so a holder at the back of a first-come-first-served line would wait about $35/5=7$ quarters. A tender line is not first come, first served. It is pro rata: everyone in line gets the same fraction each quarter, so at OTIC's 13.1% fill a holder who tenders everything every quarter still has $(1-0.131)^n$ of the shares after $n$ quarters. Half is out after 5 quarters, nine-tenths after 17, more than four years (computed, at a constant fill). Pro rata is fair to everyone in line and slow for all of them. The fill rises as the line drains, so the 17 is a ceiling, not a forecast; the recursion with a backlog is §17.6.2.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The sponsor.* From the chair of the firm that runs a semi-liquid fund, the queue is a problem with five known tools, and in 2026 sponsors used all five (§17.6, Long Scene 2). The sponsor's seat sees what an investor often does not: each tool moves value between leavers and stayers, in a direction that depends on whether the marks are right. *Upsizing the cap with house money* (BCRED, first quarter) buys leavers' shares at NAV; if the marks are high, the sponsor pays the leavers' premium instead of the stayers. *Return of capital in place of tenders* (OBDC II, February) pays everyone pro rata and ends the race to the door by removing the door. *Selling loans at par* (Blue Owl, 18 February) is fair to stayers only if the loans sold were average, and the buyer chose them. *Buying back the listed sibling below NAV* (OBDC, at 86% of book) moves value the other way, from sellers to holders who stay. *Cutting the distribution* (BCRED, 10.0% to 9.1%) keeps cash in the fund and taxes the holders who came for income. The IMF read the gate as "discretionary," noting managers who "either accommodated redemptions above the 5 percent limit or gated them despite apparent balance sheet capacity" (IMF *Global Financial Stability Report*, April 2026). None of the five is in the recursion, and all five change it.

## 17B.4 The PC-LME Detector

*(Theo's desk, the Friday model review of 16 October 2026, Ines presiding. The review, its dialogue, the fitted models and their outputs are fictional and illustrative; the marks, dates and terms of Pluralsight, Medallia and Zayo are from the filings and reports cited in §17B.1, §17B.2 and Chapter 13-B.)*

The Fed had put private credit into its Financial Accounts that day (§17.7), and nobody at the Friday review mentioned it, because the review was about something smaller and harder: whether a model built on four marks a year could tell anyone anything before the fifth.

Kaz presents. Five states, in the order an RSSG banker would recognize them on a schedule: *performing*; *PIK by amendment*; *non-accrual* at any valuer; *restructured*, the keys or an exchange; and *re-cut*, a second restructuring of the restructured loan. Each quarter a facility sits in the most advanced state any public beat supports, and it can skip a square, as Medallia went from a lapsed PIK straight to non-accrual.

> **Math, Gently.** *A board game with weighted dice.* Picture five squares in a row. Every quarter each loan rolls a die that decides whether it moves forward, and the die is weighted differently on each square, by what can be seen about the loan that quarter: how far apart its holders mark it, whether the sponsor has written off its equity, whether the coupon just changed. The chance of moving off a square in one quarter is that square's *hazard*. A hazard of 5% a quarter means that of a hundred loans on the square about five move on by next quarter, and over a year about $1-0.95^4\approx19\%$. The model learns the weights from loans that have already played and reads them for loans still on the board. The full version is §14.2 (competing exits), §7-B.4 (survival in the right order) and Chapter 12-B (the restructuring as a state machine).

With $s_{i,t}\in\{0,\dots,4\}$ the state of facility $i$ in quarter $t$ and $x_{i,t}$ its features, the model is a discrete-time hazard,

$$
h^{(k)}_{i,t}=\Pr\big(s_{i,t+1}>k\;\big|\;s_{i,t}=k,\;x_{i,t}\big)=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}\!\big(\alpha_k+\vartheta^{\top}x_{i,t}\big),
\qquad
h^{(k)}\approx1-e^{-\lambda^{(k)}\Delta t},
$$

with one intercept per square and one set of weights shared across squares, because two borrowers' worth of history cannot support five. The superscript keeps $h^{(k)}$ apart from §17.6's haircut and Chapter 8-D's hedge ratio; $\lambda^{(k)}$ is the book's default intensity split by stage.

The features are the chapter so far, written as columns: the clustered dispersion, taken as the largest range in the trailing four quarters; the lowest valuer's shortfall below par, Theo's "whoever looked hardest" from §17.4; a sponsor flag for an equity mark gone to zero or halved, dated when it became public; a drop-down flag parsed from 8-K and 10-Q footnotes; the two PIK columns of §17B.2; the pending markdown $\max(0,-\Pi)$ of §17.8.3; a software flag; and the joiners of the holder's own vehicle from §17B.3.

The out-of-sample test is Pluralsight. Fitted on everything else, the detector reads the December 2023 schedules, public by early March 2024, and puts Pluralsight in its top decile with the beat *dispersion*: a ten-point range on one coupon. The March schedules, public in May, move it to the top at 13.6. The keys change hands around 22 August, about five and a half months after the first flag and three and a half after the second. Kaz's first run had the sponsor flag firing on 28 May, the date of Vista's update, and Theo caught it. "That was an update to Vista's fund investors. We learned it when the press did, weeks later." The flag moves to its publication date, and the backtest loses nothing it was entitled to; §26.7 is a section about the backtest that knew the ending.

Medallia changed the model. Kaz's first fit used the *change* in dispersion, on the intuition that disagreement widens as trouble arrives, and found nothing. Medallia's range was 12.1 points at the end of 2024, then 5.3, 13.9, 6.2, 10.8 and 11.1 through June 2026 (BDC schedules). It swings without trending: disagreement arrives early and stays, at a level, while committees take turns being the outlier. The level fires in the December 2024 schedules, with the par-weighted mark still near 94 and more than a year before the forties; the PIK cliff fires in December 2025, the non-accrual flag in March 2026.

Zayo is the control, and it caught version 1, which had a feature for a signed cooperation pact borrowed from Chapter 13-B's process clock. Zayo's first-lien lenders reportedly signed one on 23 December 2024, four days after talks over a haircut failed, with the loan in the mid-nineties. Then Zayo amended and extended: a support agreement in August 2025, closing in late September at more than 99.8% participation, with 2027 secured debt pushed to March 2030 and a \$2.0 billion paydown, and no default, haircut or court (Zayo's release). A pact against a maturity wall is a negotiation, and negotiations end at par more often than a distress model likes. Version 2 keeps the pact date as a clock and takes it out of the score.

"Which default is it predicting?" Mags asks, as she asks of every model with the word in it.

"Advancing one square, with the square set by the most advanced valuer."

"So one committee's accounting decision moves the label for all seven."

"The second label uses the least advanced valuer. They differ on Medallia by a quarter and on Pluralsight not at all." Both are in the deck's appendix, where Mags always looks first. Law 13 is the reason: what you call an event decides what the model can learn.

"What's the null?" Ines asks.

That the marks are right and the listed prices overshoot. PIMCO argued it in 2026: BDC shares lagged badly while BDC bonds recovered, which reads as equity holders pricing a mark reset that bondholders did not expect, and "daily pricing is not price discovery." If the detector only re-describes the listed discount, it adds nothing to §17.8.3's mark gap $G$. So the test is narrow: does a facility's rank predict its next mark change after controlling for the pending markdown $\Pi$? On two borrowers with full paths, one control and a few hundred quiet facilities, the answer is a positive coefficient inside an interval that would embarrass anyone who quoted it as a probability.

Ines writes the verdict in §13.8's words for the Priming Risk Score: *a ranking, not a measurement.* The honest output is a dated list, each facility with the beat that put it there, refreshed the week the 10-Qs land.

Then she replays the hand with §17.4's three Pluralsight cards. Kaz's override was the average of the March marks, about 90, the one number that cannot rank a loan above its holders' consensus. Theo's was the lowest mark, 83.4, with a dispersion flag: the detector's first two columns, by hand. Mags's was 50, because the sponsor was handing over the keys. "Mags still wins," Ines says. "She read one folder. The list reads three thousand, and it would have handed her that folder in May without anyone opening a newspaper."

> **Ways This Blows Up.** (1) *Look-ahead by document date.* A sponsor's mark dated in May and reported in July enters the backtest in July; a footnote in a 10-Q filed in August is August's information. (2) *Valuers counted twice.* Unclustered, two vehicles with one adviser halve the measured dispersion when they agree and double the evidence when they move together. (3) *The schedule's survivors.* A loan sold, repaid or written off vanishes from the schedules, and a parser that drops it censors exactly the events the model exists to see; the parser has to keep the exit row. (4) *Two positive examples.* With two borrowers, anything they share looks predictive, including the industry. Both were software. (5) *The flag as a habit.* A BDC that moves loans to non-accrual early will dominate the first label; per-adviser intercepts or the second label are the check.

The listing makes the review run every quarter across three thousand facilities without anyone re-deciding which vehicles share a valuer or when a sponsor's mark became public. `cluster_marks` collapses the `soi` table to one par-weighted mark per facility, quarter and adviser, and needs two columns §17.8's schema lacked, `adviser_id` and `nonaccrual_flag`. `facility_features` builds the facility-quarter table: valuation processes, lowest and par-weighted marks, clustered dispersion and its trailing maximum, the PIK flags, software and, from an `events` table keyed to publication dates, the sponsor, drop-down and restructuring flags. `assign_state` puts each facility on its square, carries the state forward, marks a second reset as a re-cut and counts quarters since the last reset, which is §14.3's re-default clock. `fit_hazard` is a ridge logistic regression solved by Newton steps, because the panel is small and the ridge is how a prior gets in; `watchlist` scores one quarter and names each row's largest contribution as its beat. The checks rebuild Medallia's June 2026 row (six valuation processes, an 11.1-point range), walk a toy facility across all five squares and the clock, and confirm on a seeded panel that a Pluralsight-shaped row far outscores a Zayo-shaped one.

It belongs on loans held by several public vehicles. The edge that bites is the loan with one valuation process: its dispersion is blank, the design matrix fills blank with zero, and zero reads as "no disagreement." A single-adviser loan and a unanimous loan look identical to the score, so the list prints the number of valuation processes on every row, and a reader who skips that column trusts the quiet loans most exactly where the model sees least.

## 17B.6 When to Apply It, and When It Breaks

The detector and the queue model work in a specific place: loans held by several public vehicles, with schedules parsed and matched, sponsors whose marks surface somewhere public, and holders whose vehicles publish their tender results. That place is larger than it sounds. PIMCO's cross-section of loans held by at least two BDCs found 83% of them marked within two points of each other at the end of 2025 (§17.3.3), which makes the other 17% a short list, and the short list is where Medallia lived (§17.3.3) and where Pluralsight's 13.6-point March spread would have put it.

It breaks in three ways, each with a story. The first is the loan with one holder: a bilateral loan in a fund that files nothing has no cross-section to disagree with, and the empty dispersion column reads as calm. The second is collateral that was never there. First Brands filed for bankruptcy on 28 September 2025, and an indictment unsealed in the Southern District of New York on 29 January 2026 alleges invoices and inventory pledged to several lenders; the SEC charged former Tricolor executives on 18 August 2026 with what its release called "double pledging"; the UK lender Market Financial Solutions went into administration in February 2026 amid press-reported allegations of the same kind. All of these are allegations. No model of marks catches a double pledge, because every holder's mark is built on the same false collateral; a lien search does, which is why §17.8.2's sixth feature is a UCC lien-overlap check rather than a statistic. The third is the wrong public factor: the pending markdown $\Pi$ is beta times a loan index that holds little software, so for the software-heavy books where the question mattered most in 2026 it leaned toward zero (§17.8.3), and PB-16's cold open is the same failure seen from a hedge.

A seasoned practitioner opening a new name checks four things before believing any score: who else holds it, and how many valuation processes that makes; the date each fact became public, which is rarely the date printed on it; whether the sponsor's equity mark exists anywhere outside its limited partners; and, because the reader of this book came from a restructuring desk, what the credit agreement allowed the borrower to move. That last is Theo's standing question, *but what does the credit agreement say?*, and the column Danny's committee did not have.

> **Trend Watch.** *Marks becoming prices, and what to watch next (as of 18 October 2026).* **Pending tender results:** ADS and HLEND had not filed third-cycle results; the desk's illustrative nowcast in §17B.3 puts ADS at about 16.8% to 22.2% of shares requested and HLEND at about 13.3% to 17.2%. **Daily pricing:** Apollo extended daily pricing to its credit vehicles on 1 October 2026, with asset-level pricing to follow from 30 October, and described the number as "an estimated fair value" that "is not intended to constitute a definitive valuation or to serve as a basis for any investment decision," nor for subscriptions, redemptions or transactions (Apollo release). **Official statistics:** on 16 October the Federal Reserve brought private credit into the Financial Accounts, \$1.49 trillion of loans at the end of 2025 with holders broken out (FEDS Note; §17.7 and Appendix B have the series). **The public tail:** the ICE BofA CCC option-adjusted spread was 12.78% on 15 October, 308 basis points wider than in June, against 3.23% for the high-yield index (FRED). **Rates:** the Fed raised its target by 25 basis points on 17 September, a quarter point of cash interest on every floating-rate private loan; the November schedules will show whether it turned some PIK into non-accrual. **What it means for the desk:** the first private-credit numbers faster than quarterly are model outputs labeled as such by their publishers, and the request series remains the one belief variable no manager controls.

### Exercises

**1. Take the line out.** *[queue]* A fund drew requests for 12.0% of shares and filled 41.7% of them; next cycle it drew 14.0%. With no new subscriptions, how much of the second cycle is the returning line if every prorated holder resubmits, and if half do? What are the joiners in each case? *Check:* about 7.4% and 3.7% of shares; joiners about 7.2% and 10.7%. *Solution.* Repurchased $0.12\times0.417\approx5.0\%$; unfilled about 7.0%; on the new share count about 7.36%, or 3.68% at $\xi=0.5$. Joiners $(14.0-7.36)/92.64\approx7.2\%$ and $(14.0-3.68)/96.32\approx10.7\%$. The resubmission assumption moves the belief series by half its value, which is why the one letter that published the other side is worth more than its length.

**2. Pro rata is not a line.** *[queue]* At a constant fill of 13.1%, how many quarters does a holder who tenders everything every cycle need to get half the shares out, and nine-tenths? Compare with Little's law for a first-come-first-served line of 35% of shares served at 5% a quarter. *Check:* 5, 17 and 7 quarters. *Solution.* The smallest $n$ with $1-0.869^n\ge0.5$ is 5, and with $\ge0.9$ is 17; Little's average is $35/5=7$. Pro rata gives everyone a little sooner and everyone the whole later.

**3. How many valuers?** *[dispersion]* Seven BDCs mark one loan; two share an adviser, and the remaining valuation processes' errors have a pairwise correlation of 0.3. How many independent opinions is that? *Check:* 2.4. *Solution.* Six processes; $6/(1+5\times0.3)=2.4$. A 13.6-point range that survives that much herding is information.

**4. Two rulers.** *[non-accrual]* FS KKR's second-quarter 2026 non-accruals were 3.8% of fair value and 7.1% of cost. If its portfolio fair value over cost were 0.90 *(illustrative)*, at what fraction of cost is the bad bucket carried? *Check:* about 0.48. *Solution.* $(3.8/7.1)\times0.90\approx0.48$, a mark and not a recovery; set it beside Chapter 14's realized recoveries rather than the manager's commentary.

**5. The lottery ticket.** *[short leg]* A loan is 1.7% of a listed BDC's net assets, marked at 96.8. It is restructured and marked at 47.0, and nothing else in the fund moves. By how much does NAV fall, and if the BDC's daily share-price volatility is 1.5% *(illustrative)*, what fraction of a quarter's ordinary noise is that? *Check:* about 0.87% of NAV; about a fourteenth. *Solution.* $1.7\%\times49.8/96.8\approx0.87\%$. A quarter's noise is about $1.5\%\times\sqrt{63}\approx11.9\%$, and $0.87/11.9\approx0.07$. Wen's refusal, in one line.

**6. Spec it (build).** *[robot]* Write the precise spec for "tell me when a private loan is about to restructure" so that ROBO can return neither "every software loan" nor "Pluralsight." *Solution sketch.* Name the label (advance one square, most advanced valuer; a second label at the least advanced), the unit (facility-quarter), the publication date of every input, the clustering of valuers, a minimum of two valuation processes for a dispersion value, the exclusion of the evaluation borrowers from the fit, and the output as a ranked, dated list with its beat. Then add the test ROBO fails first: a single-adviser loan scored as calm.
