From somewhere offstage comes the sound of a Rule 2019 statement being drafted. The Ad Hoc Group, represented by counsel, speaks in unison: *"We neither confirm nor deny that we exist. We hold 51.3% of the first lien."*

Invited, wall-crossed, has seen the term sheet, and is about to swap this loan into super-priority paper that trades well above 78, with a fee for the signature. Seat one cannot sell to me at all, because seat one is restricted."

By the end of this chapter you will be able to: write every LMT (uptier, drop-down, double-dip, pari-plus, amend-extend-and-exchange) as the difference between two waterfalls on the same enterprise value; convert the five clause families that enable priming into state variables with units; price an excluded lender's position as a short digital put on $V$ struck where document capacity says; solve the $n$-lender threshold game and the sponsor's procurement auction, and see why a cooperation agreement turns both into bilateral bargaining; build a multi-obligor clearing engine that handles guarantees, intercompany loans, and circularity without creating or destroying a dollar; and specify the Priming Risk Score, a Bayesian hazard model that a coding agent can build from about a hundred events without overfitting itself into the sun.

## 13.1 The LMT Zoo, Seen From the Desk

A liability management transaction is an out-of-court change to the capital structure, executed under the existing documents, that re-allocates recovery among claims that were drafted to be equal. That last clause is the whole thing. A pro rata amend-and-extend that treats every lender identically is liability management in the polite sense. The trades that make careers and lawsuits are the ones in which some holders of the same tranche end up senior to other holders of that tranche, or in which collateral that was everybody's becomes somebody's.

Start with a surf company, because the cleanest way to see the species is to watch one animal move. In April 2018 Boardriders, the owner of Quiksilver and Billabong, signed a credit agreement with about \$450 million of first-lien debt. Its owner, Oaktree, was also one of its lenders. Then 2020 arrived for everyone who sold board shorts through physical stores. In August 2020 the company did a deal that looked from a distance like a rescue: \$110 million of new super-priority money came in, and \$321 million of existing loans were rolled up, at par, into the same new super-priority tranche, \$431 million in all. A majority of lenders voted to delete the covenant that limited debt and the requirement that any new intercreditor arrangement be junior or pari passu. The roll-up was papered as "open market purchase" agreements. Remember those three words. Over this chapter they will cost a great many people a great deal of money.

Now look at that deal from the seats of two holders of the same loan on the same Friday. Holder A was in the group. Its loan was trading at a 40 to 50 percent discount, and it was exchanged at par into paper at the top of the stack. Holder B, one of the non-consenting lenders who together held about \$85 million, woke up on Monday holding exactly the document it had held on Friday, except that \$431 million now stood in front of it. Holder B's loan did not change a word. The words around it did.

The excluded lenders, ICG and York among them, sued in New York in October 2020. On October 17, 2022, Justice Andrea Masley refused to dismiss their breach-of-contract and implied-covenant claims, finding the open-market exception ambiguous. Then comes the twist that makes Boardriders the best teaching case in the zoo. In August 2023 Authentic Brands agreed to buy the company, and the settlement tied to the sale repaid the non-participants in full. Par. The case was dismissed with prejudice in September 2023.

So one uptier produced an excluded lender who was primed in 2020, litigating in 2022 and paid par in 2023. Nothing in the document predicted the par. What predicted it was the value of the company on the day the story ended, a sale large enough to pay everyone. That is half the answer to Kaz's question from the cold open; the other half, who decides which room you are in, takes until §13.5. The rest of this section builds the machine that turns "it depends what the company is worth when it ends" into a number.

From the desk, every species in the zoo has the same skeleton. There is an enterprise value $V$ at some resolution date (a refinancing, a sale, a Chapter 11 plan). There is a document state $\mathcal{D}$, which encodes who has a claim against which entity, at what priority, secured by what. And there is a waterfall operator $\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}$ that maps $V$ into a vector of recoveries per dollar of face, one entry per claim:

$$
\mathbf{R} = \mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(V), \qquad R_j \in [0, 1].
$$

An LMT is a move $\mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{D}'$. The economic content of the move, for claim $j$, is the **transfer function**

$$
\Delta_j(V) = \big[\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}'}(V)\big]_j - \big[\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(V)\big]_j .
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *Waterfalls and transfer functions.* Picture one till and one cash drawer, with creditors standing in queues. The waterfall is the house rule for service: the senior queue is paid in full before anyone behind it gets a cent, and people standing in the same queue split whatever reaches them pro rata. An LMT does not put cash in the drawer. It lets some people change queues. Tiny number: the drawer holds $V = 100$ and one class of lenders holds $F = 200$ of face, so everyone gets 50 cents on the dollar. Now let half the class, 100 of face, step into a new queue at the front. The front half is paid 100 cents, the back half gets zero, and the drawer still pays out exactly 100. The transfer function $\Delta_j(V)$ is "what claim $j$ got after the queue-jump, minus what it got before," written down for every possible size of drawer rather than just 100. Try a drawer of 250: before the jump everyone is paid in full, after it everyone is still paid in full, and the transfer is zero. Queue order matters only when the drawer is short, which is why this is a credit problem and not an accounting one. The full version, with guarantees and intercompany claims, is the clearing engine of §13.7.

Two facts make this useful. First, $\Delta_j$ is a function of $V$, so it is a derivative on enterprise value and can be priced like one: integrate it against a distribution of $V$ and you have the expected transfer. Second, waterfalls conserve value. If the face amounts under the two document states are $\mathbf{F}$ and $\mathbf{F}'$, then

$$
\mathbf{F}'^{\top}\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}'}(V) = \min\!\big(V,\ \mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{F}'\big), \qquad \mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(V) = \min\!\big(V,\ \mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\big),
$$

so in any state where $V$ is below both totals, the dollar transfers sum to exactly zero. An LMT does not create recovery in the states that matter. It moves recovery. (It may change $V$ itself, by buying liquidity runway or by burning fees, and we will keep that effect separate and explicit; see §13.3.)

That is the sentence to say out loud on your first day: *an LMT is a zero-sum transfer among creditors in low states, wrapped in a liquidity story about high states.* The liquidity story is sometimes true. The transfer is always true.

> **Math, Gently.** *What a distribution is, and why $V$ is lognormal.* Nobody knows what Larkspur will be worth when its story ends. A distribution is the honest way to say so: a histogram of possible futures, each bar as tall as the weight you put on that outcome. Different quantities want different shapes. The **normal** is the symmetric bell; it is a good first guess for things built from many small, independent pushes added together, such as a day's return or a measurement error, and it cheerfully puts weight on negative numbers. Enterprise value cannot be negative, and bad years compound rather than subtract: a 30% fall followed by another 30% fall leaves 49 out of 100, not 40. Quantities that grow and shrink by percentages are better described by the **lognormal**, whose logarithm is normal. It lives on the positive numbers, has a long right tail (the occasional sale at a lovely price) and bunches most of its mass to the left of its own mean. Tiny number: a normal with mean 2,000 and standard deviation 800 puts about 0.6% of its mass below zero (that is $\Phi(-2.5)$), which is 0.6% of futures in which the foam company is worth less than nothing; a lognormal with the same mean cannot put anything there. Two more shapes arrive in §13.8: the **Poisson**, which counts events in a window, and the **Beta**, which is a histogram over a probability itself. "Integrate the transfer against a distribution of $V$" now has a picture: in each future, multiply the bar's height by what the queue-jump costs you there, then add up the bars. The full version is the lognormal partial expectation in §13.3.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.228:* "Exchange offers are “coercive” when they compel cooperation by exposing nonparticipants to the risk of being made worse off."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer's definition was written for the bond exchange offers of the 1990s and 2000s, and it travels unchanged into the LMT era with one twist that changes the game theory completely. In Moyer's world, coercion is *uniform*: every holder is offered the same exchange, and the threat to non-participants (stripped covenants, structural subordination) is the stick that solves the holdout problem. Everyone can take the carrot. In a non-pro-rata uptier, coercion is *selective*. The carrot is offered only to an invitation list, and the stick lands on everyone else, including holders who would have happily taken the carrot. That one change turns a coordination problem (will enough of us accept?) into a coalition-formation problem (who gets to be in the coalition that does the coercing?). Section 13.4 solves the second problem. The desk's practical departure from Moyer is this: in the 1990s you modeled your *participation decision*. In 2026 you model your *invitation probability*, because for most holders, most of the time, the decision is made for them.

> **Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality.** *Myth:* "LMEs avoid bankruptcy; that's the point." *Reality:* Roe and Rotaru hand-collected 89 coercive non-pro-rata LMEs and found that fewer than half avoided both bankruptcy and re-default within a year, and "within two years, only 22% avoided both"; among firms with at least two years of post-LME history, 56% had already filed, and those that filed spent two to three times longer in bankruptcy than non-LME prepacks (Harvard Law School Bankruptcy Roundtable, Feb 24, 2026; SSRN abstract 6103369). Moody's found about 46% of 2023 distressed exchanges had already re-defaulted, most in under a year (reported by Bloomberg Law, Apr 23, 2025). Proponents cite an S&P figure that 63% of LME users avoid Chapter 11 (quoted in Antill, Jiang and Wang, HLS Roundtable, June 30, 2026; secondhand). The two camps differ in their samples, horizons, and default definitions. Whichever number you believe, the LMT is not the terminal state of the credit. It is a jump to a new state with a new, usually higher, hazard. Model it that way (§13.8).

Mags puts it more simply when Theo reads the box aloud. "An LMT is a jump to a new state, and the new state has a clock on the wall. Of the eight big ones I'd make you learn, six filed anyway, and the typical gap between the deal and the petition was about a year." (Six of the eight cases in §13.9's scoreboard filed, at a median of roughly 13.5 months; Boardriders and Lumen ended without one.) That clock is the re-default hazard, and it will be a covariate before the chapter ends.

## 13.2 Legal Prose to State Space

You have spent years reading credit agreements as text. The model needs them as numbers. The translation has five clause families, and each one maps to a small set of features with units. The goal is not to replace a lawyer. It is to make the lawyer's reading *comparable across 1,500 borrowers*, so that the desk can rank them.

The way Theo does it for Larkspur, at 8:15 on the same Tuesday, is the way he did it at the old shop for a creditor committee: out loud, one clause at a time, with somebody else holding the marker. Today the somebody is Kaz, at the whiteboard, converting each clause into a variable with a type and a unit. Kaz is fast and precise and gets exactly one thing wrong per clause family, which turns out to be useful, because each wrong guess is the reading a model would make if nobody had told it otherwise.

"Section 2.18," Theo reads. "Pro rata sharing. Then 9.02(b), the amendment section, and the list of things that need 'each Lender directly and adversely affected thereby.'"

Kaz writes `affected_lender`. "Affected as in upset?"

"Affected as in paid less. Feelings are not a sacred right." Theo keeps going.

**Family 1: Pro rata sharing and sacred rights.** The pro rata sharing clause (Serta's was §2.18) says every lender in a tranche shares payments ratably. The amendment section (Serta's §9.02(b)) lists the "sacred rights" that need the consent of each affected lender rather than a majority. The question for an uptier is whether *subordination* of the loans, or of the liens, is on that list. If it is, a majority cannot prime the minority. That is the blocker. Features: `sacred_rights_scope` $\in$ {`payments_only`, `payments_plus_liens`, `payments_plus_subordination`}, and `has_serta_blocker` $\in \{0,1\}$, which is 1 when lien or payment subordination requires each affected lender's consent.

**Family 2: Voting thresholds.** The required-lender fraction $\theta$ is the share of the class that can amend everything not on the sacred list. In syndicated loans $\theta$ is usually a simple majority. Many indentures require two-thirds for lien releases (that was the threshold the Wesco participants needed). Sacred rights are $\theta = 1$ for the affected lender. Feature: `theta_required` $\in (0, 1]$, measured *for the specific action the deal requires*, not for the agreement in general. One agreement has several $\theta$'s, and the trapdoor is usually the lowest one that can be reached.

Kaz has written `required_lenders` on the board and underlined it. "So these are the lenders who are required to attend."

"These are the lenders whose votes count," says Theo, "and you have just found the fight, because the real question is the denominator. 'More than 50%' of *what*?" A senior partner at Theo's old shop used to make juniors answer three questions about this one definition before they were allowed to say the word "uptier." First: who is excluded from the denominator. Defaulting lenders, the ones who failed to fund, are usually carved out of the count, which shrinks the class a coalition has to beat. Loans held by the sponsor or its affiliates are, in many documents, capped at a stated fraction of the class, and their votes are excluded or deemed cast in proportion to everyone else's. A model that divides by total face when the document divides by "non-defaulting, non-affiliated" face will overstate the bar. Second: can the amendment section itself be amended by Required Lenders? If changing the voting provisions, the definition of Required Lenders or the list of sacred rights is itself a sacred right, the "amend the amendment section" route is closed; if it is not, a majority can lower the bar and then walk under it. Third: what does releasing all or substantially all of the collateral take? In most secured indentures it is 66⅔% of holders; in many syndicated credit agreements it is every lender, a sacred right. (Chapter 12-B, §12B.3, tabulates the typical thresholds.) Each answer becomes a feature with the same discipline as the rest: `rl_denominator_excludes_affiliates` and `rl_denominator_excludes_defaulting` in $\{0,1\}$, `amend_amendment_is_sacred` in $\{0,1\}$, and `lien_release_theta`, which reappears in Family 5. Read against a specific agreement, not a form, because the percentages are where drafters earn their fees.

"So the denominator is a political choice," says Kaz.

"The denominator is the whole fight. The numerator is just counting."

Theo turns the page to the assignment section, which is where Serta was won and lost.

**Family 3: Buyback exceptions to pro rata.** Most agreements let the borrower buy back its own loans without offering the same deal to everyone, through Dutch auctions open to all lenders or through open market purchases. This exception is how a non-pro-rata exchange slips past the pro rata clause. Feature: `buyback_exception` $\in$ {`none`, `dutch_only`, `open_market`, `purchase`}. On December 31, 2024, two appellate courts decided on the same day that the difference between the third and fourth values is worth the entire priming loss. The Fifth Circuit held in *Serta* that "an open market purchase is a purchase of corporate debt that occurs on the secondary market for syndicated loans," so a privately negotiated exchange did not qualify. New York's First Department held in *Mitel* that an undefined "purchases" exception, with no "open market" qualifier, was unambiguous and was satisfied by an exchange paid in new loans (as summarized by Paul, Weiss, Jan 10, 2025). Same kind of deal, same day, one word, opposite results. "Open market purchase" really is the most expensive three words in finance, and the word "open" accounts for most of the bill.

Kaz writes `buyback_exception` with its four values and pauses at the second. "And a Dutch auction is held where, Rotterdam?"

"Anywhere. It's a descending-price auction open to every lender in the class. The point is that it's *open to every lender*. That's what makes it pro rata in spirit. The other two values are where the spirit leaves the building."

**Family 4: Investments, restricted payments, and unrestricted subsidiaries.** This is drop-down territory. The question is how many dollars of value can legally leave the collateral group. The capacity is a sum of baskets:

$$
C^{\text{unsub}} = B^{\text{gen}} + B^{\text{builder}} + B^{\text{ratio}}\,\mathbf{1}[\text{ratio test passes}] + B^{\text{unsub}} + B^{\text{other}},
$$

where $B^{\text{gen}}$ is the general investment basket (often a "greater of" dollar amount and percentage of EBITDA or total assets), $B^{\text{builder}}$ is the builder basket (the "Available Amount" or "Cumulative Credit"), $B^{\text{ratio}}$ is the unlimited capacity that opens if a leverage test is met, $B^{\text{unsub}}$ is a dedicated basket for investments in unrestricted subsidiaries, and $B^{\text{other}}$ covers the capacity that hides in "Permitted Investments" clauses that can be stacked (the J.Crew trapdoor was precisely a stack: an investment in a non-guarantor restricted subsidiary, then onward from that subsidiary to an unrestricted one). Features: `unsub_capacity_pct_ev` $= C^{\text{unsub}}/\text{EV}$ and `rp_builder_pct_ev` $= B^{\text{builder}}/\text{EV}$, both dimensionless. Divide by enterprise value, not by face, because the question is what fraction of the *pie* can walk out the door.

"Cumulative Credit," Kaz reads off Theo's screen, and writes `rp_builder_pct_ev`. "Like a credit score that only goes up."

"Worse," says Theo. "It can go up while the company goes down." That is not a joke, and Moyer explains why.

The builder basket is a running account, and Moyer lays out the classic bond-indenture version line by line.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.173:* "50% of all net income earned 100% of proceeds from the sale of common or preferred stock 100% of the proceeds from the issuance of any junior debt securities Less: 100% of all net losses 100% of all amounts distributed as"
>
> **Annotation.** (The extraction preserves the page's line breaks as spaces; the original is a formatted list in which each item is a line of the builder computation.) This is the accounting identity behind $B^{\text{builder}}$: a starter amount, plus half of cumulative consolidated net income, plus equity proceeds and certain junior debt proceeds, less net losses and less every restricted payment and unrestricted-subsidiary investment already made. Code it as a state variable that evolves quarterly: $B^{\text{builder}}_{t+1} = B^{\text{builder}}_t + 0.5\,\text{CNI}_t^{+} - \text{CNI}_t^{-} + \text{EqProc}_t - \text{RP}_t - \text{Inv}^{\text{unsub}}_t$, floored at zero. Two modern departures matter. First, leveraged-loan agreements written after about 2015 often build from Consolidated EBITDA minus a multiple of fixed charges, or from a "greater of" starter that grows with EBITDA, so the builder can *grow while the company deteriorates* if EBITDA add-backs are generous (Mags is right to distrust anything called "adjusted"). Second, the builder is only one of several stackable baskets; the drop-down cases were won or lost on whether baskets could be used *sequentially through intermediate subsidiaries*. The feature you want is not "the builder" but the maximum dollar amount that can reach an unrestricted subsidiary by any legal path through the entity graph, which is a max-flow problem on the definitions (the parser in Chapter 25 builds that graph).

**Family 5: Lien and guarantee release, and the IP blockers.** Can a guarantor be released when it stops being wholly owned (the PetSmart/Chewy move)? Can material intellectual property be transferred out of the collateral group (the J.Crew move)? Can liens be released by a majority? Features: `jcrew_blocker`, `chewy_blocker`, `envision_blocker` (the last named for a 2022 drop-down whose details we treat as illustrative), each in $\{0, 1\}$, plus `lien_release_theta`. Also flag `non_pro_rata_extension_allowed`, which captures the amend-and-extend mechanics that the 2025 "amend-extend-and-exchange" structures exploited.

Larkspur's security agreement has a springing lien in it: a lien that comes into existence on certain foreign assets only if a leverage test is failed. Kaz writes `springing_lien` and, in the column for type, "seasonal?"

"It springs into being when a trigger is hit," says Theo. "It has nothing to do with spring."

"It would be a much better clause if it did." Kaz changes the type to binary with a trigger date, which is correct, and draws a small flower next to it, which is not.

Kaz, who has followed this so far, raises a hand. "I have a question about the blocker."

"Go ahead."

"Is it a beta blocker? Because the deal seems to raise everyone's heart rate."

> **View From Another Seat.** *The direct lender who wrote the document.* Read the same five families from the seat of a private-credit manager holding most of a unitranche or club loan, and the questions change shape. ### 13.2.1 The feature vector

Collect the document features $x^{\text{doc}}_i$ and add three groups the document cannot tell you. They differ mostly in how fast they move and how hard they are to get.

The **market state** $x^{\text{mkt}}_i(t)$ moves every day, and it is the easiest block to fill because somebody sells it to you. It holds the loan price `loan_px` (points), months to maturity, the liquidity cost score `lcs` (Ben Dor's measure, Chapter 8), and three-month equity momentum or, for sponsor-owned names with no public equity, the price momentum of the loan itself. On the Larkspur morning this block changed first: the mark went from 84 to a client asking about 78 before anyone had finished reading the headline. The danger of the market block is that it is so available. A model fed mostly prices learns to predict LMTs from the price fall that the rumor of an LMT causes, which is a very accurate way of being late.

The **liquidity state** $x^{\text{liq}}_i(t)$ moves monthly or quarterly and has to be built. Its main entry is `runway_months`, defined as unrestricted cash plus undrawn committed revolver, divided by forecast monthly cash burn. This is Theo's 13-week cash flow model, reborn for the third time in this book as a covariate. He builds the Larkspur version from the last 10-Q and a burn forecast that he trusts about as much as he trusts the sponsor, and it says eleven months. When `runway_months` falls below about nine and `loan_px` below about 80, the company is in the region where a sponsor *must* do something, and the only question is whether the something is pro rata. Larkspur is already through the price line at 78 and two months short of the runway line.

The **holder and sponsor state** $x^{\text{hold}}_i(t)$ is the one nobody can download. It contains `sponsor_repeat_lme` (has this sponsor done a non-pro-rata deal before?), `holder_hhi` (Herfindahl concentration of the class from CLO holdings data and Rule 2019 statements), `coop_reported` (a cooperation agreement has been reported), and `forum_ny_vs_tx` (which court is likely to hear the fight). Each entry is assembled by hand from documents that were not written for you: trustee reports, court filings, a news wire's paraphrase of "people familiar with the matter." It is also the block that answers the cold open's question about seats, which is why §13.6 spends a whole vignette on how miserable it is to build.

The full state is $x_i(t) = \big(x^{\text{doc}}_i, x^{\text{mkt}}_i(t), x^{\text{liq}}_i(t), x^{\text{hold}}_i(t)\big)$. The document block is nearly static (it changes when the agreement is amended, which is itself an event). The other blocks move daily. Section 13.8 turns $x_i(t)$ into a hazard. Sections 13.3 to 13.7 compute what the hazard is a hazard *of*.

## 13.3 The Uptier as a Recovery Transfer

Start with the cleanest species and price it exactly. One secured class, face $F$, everyone pari passu. Before the deal, each lender recovers

$$
R_0(V) = \frac{\min(V, F)}{F}.
$$

The uptier has three moving parts. New money $N$ comes in as a first-out tranche. A fraction $p$ of the existing face (the participants) is exchanged into a second-out tranche at exchange ratio $x$ (new face per dollar of old face; $x < 1$ means the participants take a haircut in face to jump the line). The remaining $(1-p)F$ (the excluded lenders) stays in the old tranche, now third in line. Write $K_1 = N + xpF$ for the total face senior to the excluded lenders and $K_2 = K_1 + (1-p)F$ for the total face of the new structure. Then

$$
R^{\text{FO}}(V) = \frac{\min(V, N)}{N}, \qquad
R^{\text{SO}}(V) = \frac{\min\!\big((V - N)^+,\ xpF\big)}{xpF}, \qquad
R^{\text{excl}}(V) = \frac{\min\!\big((V - K_1)^+,\ (1-p)F\big)}{(1-p)F}.
$$

Before the algebra, a picture, because the picture is the trade.

> **Math, Gently.** *Three payoff shapes: the slice, the call spread and the digital.* Draw the excluded lender's recovery on graph paper, enterprise value along the bottom and cents on the dollar up the side. Before the deal it is a ramp: a straight line from the origin climbing at slope $1/F$ and flattening at 100 cents once $V$ covers the whole class. That is a **linear slice** of the firm. After the deal the ramp starts later, at $K_1$, because everyone now in front must be paid first, and it climbs more steeply, because the excluded lenders are a smaller group sharing each extra dollar. A ramp that starts at one strike and flattens at another is what an options desk calls a **call spread**: long a call struck at $K_1$, short a call struck at $K_2$. Shrink the excluded group and the ramp steepens into a cliff, zero just below $K_1$ and paid in full just above it. A payoff that jumps from nothing to everything at a single strike is a **digital**, or binary, option. The *difference* between the old ramp and the new one is a tent, rising with the old slice up to $K_1$ and falling away after it; as the excluded minority shrinks, the tent's right side becomes vertical, and what the lender lost is shaped like a **digital put**: it pays off against the lender in exactly the futures where $V$ falls short of $K_1$. Tiny number at Serta's terms (§13.3.1): $K_1 = 1{,}075$, so at $V = 1{,}000$ the old slice paid $1{,}000/2{,}400 \approx 41.7$ cents and the new one pays zero. The payoff algebra in full is in Chapter 6-B (§6-B.1), and the version for this deal is in "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section.

Call the excluded lender's priming loss $\Delta R^{\text{excl}}(V) = R_0(V) - R^{\text{excl}}(V)$: the transfer function of §13.1 for the excluded claim, with the sign flipped so that a loss is a positive number.

Now look at the shape. The excluded lender used to own a linear slice of the firm, $\min(V,F)/F$. It now owns a call spread on $V$ between strikes $K_1$ and $K_2$. When the excluded minority is small, $(1-p)F$ is small, the spread is steep, and the call spread is approximately a *digital call* struck at $K_1$. So the priming loss is approximately

$$
\Delta R^{\text{excl}}(V) \approx R_0(V)\,\mathbf{1}[V < K_1].
$$

**The excluded lender is short a soft digital put on enterprise value, struck at the face that now sits ahead of it, with notional equal to its old recovery.** It wrote that put on the day the uptier closed. Nobody asked it to.

The participants, meanwhile, bought the same put. Per original dollar, a participant's position changed by $x R^{\text{SO}}(V) - R_0(V)$ (ignoring the new-money economics, which are usually priced generously). In low states this is large and positive. In high states it is $x - 1 < 0$: the participant paid for its put with $(1-x)$ points of face. A participant in an uptier is a lender who bought downside protection and paid the premium in principal. The excluded lender is the counterparty who wrote that protection for free.

How much is the put worth? Pricing it means asking how much weight the histogram of §13.1 puts on futures below $K_1$, and how much the excluded lender would have collected in each of them.

> **Math, Gently.** *Partial expectations, and the ½s² that keeps showing up.* A partial expectation is an average over only part of the histogram: add up $V$ times its weight, but only in the futures where $V$ is below some $K$. That is exactly what the excluded lender loses, because in those futures it would have collected $V/F$ and now collects nothing. For a lognormal there is a wrinkle that trips everyone once. If $\ln V$ is centred at $\mu$ with spread $s$, the mean of $V$ is not $e^{\mu}$ but $e^{\mu + s^2/2}$. Averaging multiplicative outcomes favours the big ones: from 2,000, a move up by a factor of $e^{0.4}$ lands near 2,984, a move down by the same factor lands near 1,341, and the two average about 2,162, not 2,000. The up move adds more than the down move takes away. This is the same $\tfrac12\sigma^2$ that Itô's lemma charges in continuous time, and you will meet it again in Chapter 6-B and in the credit-derivatives chapters of Part VI. In the partial expectation it also pushes the argument of $\Phi$ down by a further $s^2$, because each future is weighted by its own size and the small futures below $K$ count for less. Tiny number: with $\mu = \ln 2{,}000$, $s = 0.4$ and $K = 1{,}075$, the argument is $(\ln 1{,}075 - \ln 2{,}000 - 0.16)/0.4 \approx -1.95$, $\Phi(-1.95) \approx 0.026$, and the first-region loss is about $2{,}166 \times 0.026 / 2{,}400 \approx 2.3$ points. One more word lives next to this formula: **risk-neutral**. Pricing "under $\mathbb{Q}$" means using a histogram that has been tilted, with extra weight on the bad futures, until every asset's expected return equals the risk-free rate; the tilt is where the risk premium hides. A market price is a $\mathbb{Q}$-flavoured number, which is why the court's 25-cent closing-day price in §13.3.1 is not the same object as a desk's $\mathbb{P}$ forecast. The full version is "The Full Derivation" below, and Chapter 6-B (§6-B.6) for the two measures.

The derivation makes this exact, but the headline fits on a napkin. To first order, the excluded lender's expected loss is the partial expectation of $V$ below $K_1$, divided by the old face; for a lognormal, that is the put leg of Black–Scholes with the strike at $K_1$. The answer is a statement about where you think $V$ lives. Centre the lognormal at 2,000 and Serta's first region costs about 2.3 points, because a company expected to be worth 2,000 rarely visits 1,075. Centre it at 1,200, closer to a mattress company whose loans were trading "far below par" in the spring of 2020, and the same term is about 13.5 points (illustrative; our arithmetic). The middle region, where the excluded lenders are partly paid, adds a correction to either. The put is cheap or ruinous depending almost entirely on one input, and that input is the one nobody can observe.

**Where value enters.** New money is not free value. If the $N$ dollars buy runway that survives to resolution, the post-deal enterprise value is $V + \kappa N$, with $\kappa \in [0, 1]$ the fraction of new money still in the firm at resolution, and every formula above should be evaluated at $V + \kappa N$ in the post-deal world. For a company burning cash, $\kappa$ is close to zero by the time anyone files. Roe and Rotaru's evidence that leverage did not fall and ratings stayed flat after LMEs is evidence that, on average, $\kappa$ is small.

#### The Full Derivation: the excluded lender's transfer

**Conservation check.** Multiply each recovery by its face and add: $N R^{\text{FO}} + xpF\,R^{\text{SO}} + (1-p)F\,R^{\text{excl}} = \min(V, K_2)$. Total face fell from $F$ to $K_2 - N = F - (1-x)pF$, which is the "deleveraging" in the press release. It is real, and it is paid for entirely by the participants, in exactly those states where nobody needed it.

**The excluded lender's transfer.** The priming loss is $\Delta R^{\text{excl}}(V) = R_0(V) - R^{\text{excl}}(V)$. It is piecewise linear with three regions:

$$
\Delta R^{\text{excl}}(V) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{V}{F}, & V \le K_1, \\[2ex]
\dfrac{V}{F} - \dfrac{V - K_1}{(1-p)F}, & K_1 < V < K_2, \\[2ex]
R_0(V) - 1, & V \ge K_2 .
\end{cases}
$$

In the first region the excluded lender loses everything it would have recovered. In the middle region the loss shrinks linearly, at slope $\frac{1}{F} - \frac{1}{(1-p)F} < 0$. In the third region the loss is *non-positive*: if $K_2 \le V < F$, the excluded lender is paid in full while it would have recovered only $V/F$ before. It gains, because the participants gave up face. (The gain is small and lives in a narrow band of states; when $V \ge F$ both recoveries are 1 and the transfer is zero.)

**The expected transfer, in closed form.** Suppose $V$ is lognormal under $\mathbb{P}$ at the resolution horizon: $\ln V \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, s^2)$. The dominant term of the expected loss is the partial expectation below the strike, which has the same form as the put leg of Black–Scholes:

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\!\big[V\,\mathbf{1}[V < K]\big] = e^{\mu + s^2/2}\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln K - \mu - s^2}{s}\right).
$$

So for $K_1 \le F$, the first region contributes $\frac{1}{F}e^{\mu + s^2/2}\Phi\big((\ln K_1 - \mu - s^2)/s\big)$ to $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\Delta R^{\text{excl}}]$. The middle and upper regions add the correction terms. The code in §13.8 computes all three exactly by quadrature and checks the first against this closed form by Monte Carlo. This is the uptier's version of checking a simulated Merton spread against the formula: if your simulated transfer and your partial expectation disagree, your waterfall has a bug.

### 13.3.1 Case Study: Serta Simmons (2016–2026), in the Formula

The desk keeps a binder for Serta. It is the only binder on a floor that otherwise believes paper is a legacy system, and on the Wednesday evening after the Larkspur headline Theo takes it down, because Mags said "Serta" in the cold open the way other people say "the flood." Nobody in this scene was in the room in 2020. They are reading the record: the Fifth Circuit's opinion, the remand findings as the law firms summarized them, and a decade of Mags's memory, which she insists is also a primary source.

Kaz lays a sheet of paper over the opinion and slides it down a paragraph at a time. "Nobody reads below the sheet," Kaz says. "If we know the ending we will fit to it. That is look-ahead bias, and I am told it is frowned upon even in law."

**I. 2016: the document.** The opinion supplies the parameters. In 2016 Serta Simmons Bedding refinanced with "\$1.95 billion in first-lien syndicated loans and \$450 million in second-lien syndicated loans." Theo reads the three clauses that matter in the order a model would want them. Section 2.18 is pro rata sharing, with a remedy in §2.18(c): a lender that receives a disproportionate payment must "purchase (for Cash at face value) participations" so that the benefit is shared ratably. Section 9.02(b) makes the core payment terms sacred, needing each affected lender. Section 9.05(g) lets the borrower buy back loans without offering the same deal to everyone, through a Dutch auction open to all lenders, which the agreement defines to the comma, or through "open market purchases," which it does not define at all.

"In 2016 nobody read 9.05(g) for anything but buybacks," says Mags. "Buybacks were what you did when your loans were at 85 and you had cash in the drawer. It was a cleanup clause. It was the junk drawer of the agreement."

"And the junk drawer," says Kaz, "is where the knife was."

**II. Spring 2020: two groups and an auction.** Mattress showrooms closed along with the rest of physical retail, and Serta's loans "were trading far below par." Two organized groups of lenders formed and each took a proposal to the company. One group became, in the opinion's capital letters, the Prevailing Lenders. The other group did not lose because it was principled. The opinion records that the eventual Excluded Lenders "had made an alternative recapitalization proposal involving a similar kind of debt swap" themselves. Among those who would later fight the deal in court were the LCM lenders, whose names are CLO vehicles, and a Citadel fund that challenged the plan's indemnity.

Ines reads that paragraph twice. "So it was an auction, and the company chose a bidder. The losers weren't bystanders. They were the other bidder." She looks at Theo. "You ran processes like this. What did the company want?"

"Runway, a lower face amount, and enough votes," says Theo. "It needed a bare majority of the first lien. Whichever group could deliver that most cheaply won." He stops, because he has just described §13.4's knapsack without meaning to.

**III. June 22, 2020: the deal.** Kaz slides the sheet down. "The Prevailing Lenders provided SSB with \$200 million in new financing in exchange for \$200 million in first-out, super-priority debt. The Prevailing Lenders also traded in \$1.2 billion of their first-lien and second-lien loans for approximately \$875 million in second-out, super-priority debt." The Prevailing Lenders were Barings, Boston Management and Research, Credit Suisse Asset Management, Eaton Vance and Invesco Senior Secured Management. They "held a bare majority of the outstanding first-line debt" (sic; the opinion means first-lien), 52.6% of the class on the remand findings. First-lien loans went into the second-out at 74 cents of new face per dollar, second-lien loans at 39, discounts of 26% and 61%. The deal closed on Monday, June 22, 2020.

Map it, treating the two liens as one blended class for the moment (an approximation we flag; the 2L lenders were already junior, so the blended class *understates* the 1L excluded lenders' pre-deal recovery in low states, and with it their loss, while overstating the 2L's; we unblend below):

- $N = 200$, $F = 2{,}400$ (all figures \$mm), $pF = 1{,}200$, so $p = 0.5$.
- $x = 875/1{,}200 \approx 0.729$, a face haircut of about 27 points for the participants.
- $K_1 = 200 + 875 = 1{,}075$. $K_2 = 1{,}075 + 1{,}200 = 2{,}275$.

So after June 2020, the excluded lenders recovered nothing unless enterprise value exceeded about \$1.075bn. In a state where $V = \$1.075$bn, their pre-deal blended recovery would have been $1{,}075/2{,}400 \approx 44.8$ cents. The transfer in that state is the entire 44.8 points. **IV. January 2023 to November 2025: the courts.** Serta filed for Chapter 11 in the Southern District of Texas on January 23, 2023, about 31 months after the uptier: longer than the year-or-so clock Mags quoted in §13.1, much shorter than the life of a loan. In 2023 the bankruptcy court read "open market purchase" for the borrower and upheld the uptier, and the plan that followed gave the participants an indemnity against the excluded lenders' claims. The plan paid the excluded lenders very little, low single-digit cents by one law-firm count (Skadden). For two years the hold-and-litigate trade was marked as a loss with a court opinion attached.

On December 31, 2024, the Fifth Circuit reversed: "the 2020 Uptier was not a permissible open market purchase within the meaning of the 2016 Agreement." It read the "deafening silence" around an undefined term against the meticulous definition of a Dutch auction a few lines away. On the same day, New York's First Department upheld Mitel's uptier under a bare "purchases" exception (Paul, Weiss, Jan 10, 2025).

The other remedy matters too. Serta's plan gave the participants an indemnity for their litigation exposure. The Fifth Circuit held that this violated the equal-treatment rule of Bankruptcy Code §1123(a)(4) because "the unequal treatment in this case implicates both opportunity and result," and chose "to excise the offending indemnity." In a footnote it disposed of the argument that offering everyone the same *opportunity* is enough, with a hypothetical worth memorizing: a plan that gives every class member "a note promising payment of \$5 million in one year if the member is headquartered in Louisiana." Equal opportunity, in the sense that anyone may move to Louisiana. In the language of this section, the indemnity was the participants' hedge against the litigation leg of their put. The court cancelled the hedge and left them with the exposure.

The Fifth Circuit denied rehearing on February 18, 2025, and the Supreme Court denied certiorari on November 10, 2025, on a petition that attacked only the indemnity ruling.

**V. July 2026: the remand.** Six years later a court put a number on it. On remand, the bankruptcy court for the Southern District of Texas issued a memorandum opinion on July 7, 2026, finding certain participating lenders liable for breach of the pro rata sharing provision and awarding \$261.13mm plus prejudgment interest. As summarized by Mayer Brown (July 31, 2026): damages were measured as of the breach date, June 22, 2020; the benefit taken was the face value (\$734mm) of the first-lien second-out debt the participants received; the excluded lenders' ratable share was \$348mm (\$895mm / \$1.887bn × \$734mm); the excluded loans were valued at their 25-cent closing-date market price; the "but-for" world was worth \$484.88mm and the actual outcome \$223.75mm (that is, \$895mm × 0.25); and the difference, \$261.13mm, carries mandatory 9% New York prejudgment interest from June 22, 2020. At simple interest for about six years, that adds roughly 54%, or about \$142mm (our arithmetic, illustrative).

Notice which measure the court used. It did not compute $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\Delta R]$ over a distribution of enterprise values. It used a market price on the closing date, which is a $\mathbb{Q}$-flavored number with the risk premium baked in. For the desk this is useful twice over. It gives a hard calibration point for the expected damages term in the litigation outside option (§13.4), and it tells you that if you are long excluded paper with a live claim, the claim's value is anchored to *where the loan traded the day the deal closed*, not to where it ends up. A claim measured at the trough is a put that a court can exercise for you, years later, with 9% interest.

On June 22, 2020, the excluded lender wrote a digital put on enterprise value, and kept, as a separate asset, a second option struck at the courthouse, whose payoff was measured at the closing-day price and accrued 9% a year. > **Trend Watch.** *The strike moved, and so did the volume.* **Documents:** Buccola and Nini's study of 600+ leveraged loan agreements (2016–2022) found that "prior to 2020, only 40% of loans restricted uptiers, often inadvertently," while "by mid-2022, 85% of new loans explicitly blocked uptiers, with 70% requiring unanimous lender consent for subordination" (HLS Roundtable, Apr 8, 2025; *Journal of Legal Studies* 53:489, 2024). **Aggressiveness:** Octus counted 11 non-pro-rata uptiers from March 2022 to July 2024; Wesco, Mitel and Robertshaw moved 100% of value away from non-ad-hoc creditors, while the six most recent moved an average of 18% (Apex 38%, Valcour 12%, EyeCare 13%, GoTo 13%) (Octus, July 16, 2024). **Counts:** LMEs hit a high-water mark of 38 in 2024 and fell to 24 in 2025, but still accounted for about two-thirds of default activity by issuer count in 2025 and exceeded both payment defaults and bankruptcies on a rolling 12-month basis in every month of 2025 (Wachtell Lipton, *Liability Management 2026: For Better or Worse*, Feb 3, 2026). In H1 2026, 25 LMEs closed, against 24 in H1 2025, and "the coercive uptier structures that defined 2025 have all but vanished," replaced by equity handovers and consensual amend-and-extends (9fin, Aug 28, 2026). **What it means for the desk:** in the formula above, blockers drive the probability of a non-pro-rata deal down, and later deals have higher $K_1$ relative to $F$ (smaller transfers). A PRS model trained on 2020–2022 events will overstate both the probability and the severity of a 2026 uptier, unless vintage enters as a covariate. The transfer did not disappear. It migrated to structures (drop-downs, double-dips, amend-extend-and-exchange) whose clauses the 2020-vintage blockers do not cover.

**When to reach for the digital put, and when it breaks.** The short-digital-put picture is at its best in the deal it was drawn from: one lien, a small excluded minority, and new money that leaves the building before anyone files. Then the call spread really is a cliff, the strike is a number you can read off the term sheet, and the partial expectation is most of the answer. It degrades in four recognizable ways. When the excluded minority is large, $(1-p)F$ is a big share of the class, the ramp between $K_1$ and $K_2$ is gentle, and treating it as a cliff overstates the loss in the middle states; use the full three-region formula. When a court can restore liens, the put has a knock-out that a waterfall cannot see: Incora's excluded holders had their liens restored by an oral ruling in July 2024 and lost them again when the district court reversed on December 8, 2025 (§13.9). When the company is later sold for enough to pay everyone, the excluded lender's realized recovery jumps back to par, as at Boardriders in 2023 and, near enough, for Lumen's outside holders in 2024–2026 (§13.5); the put was real, but $V$ ended up far above $K_1$. And when the new money actually bought something, a sale process or a turnaround that worked, $\kappa$ is not zero, the post-deal $V$ is higher, and the put is cheaper than the static formula says.

Mags has her own first check, and it is not on the list above. "Who advised the losing group?" Losing bidders sue, and a losing bidder with serious counsel has usually built its record before the closing date. That one fact moves the second option in Serta, the one struck at the courthouse, more than any parameter in the waterfall.

## 13.4 Game Theory I: Threshold Games and the Sponsor's Auction

The waterfall tells you what the transfer is. The game tells you who ends up on which side of it.

**The threshold game.** A class has lenders $i = 1, \dots, n$ with holdings $w_i$, $\sum_i w_i = 1$. An amendment passes if a coalition $S$ with $\sum_{i \in S} w_i > \theta$ consents. Let $u^{\text{in}}$ be the per-dollar value of being inside a winning coalition, $u^0$ the value of the status quo, and $u^{\text{out}}$ the value of being primed, with $u^{\text{in}} > u^0 > u^{\text{out}}$. (From §13.3: $u^{\text{in}} - u^0 = \mathbb{E}[x R^{\text{SO}} - R_0]$ plus new-money economics, and $u^0 - u^{\text{out}} = \mathbb{E}[\Delta R^{\text{excl}}]$.)

Take an invited lender $i$ and collapse everyone else into "the rest of the class." There are three relevant situations: the rest can win without $i$, the rest can win only with $i$ (lender $i$ is *pivotal*), or no deal is possible. The payoff to $i$ is:

| | Rest wins without $i$ | $i$ is pivotal | No deal possible |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $i$ **Joins** | $u^{\text{in}}$ | $u^{\text{in}}$ | $u^0$ |
| $i$ **Holds out** | $u^{\text{out}}$ | $u^0$ | $u^0$ |

*Join* weakly dominates *Hold out* in every column. That is the prisoner's dilemma Moyer describes for exchange offers, with one sharpening: in a selective deal, the bottom-left cell is $u^{\text{out}}$, the primed outcome, which is strictly worse than the status quo. Holding out does not preserve your position. It hands your recovery to the people who did not hold out.

> **Math, Gently.** *A payoff matrix, and winning without a forecast.* A payoff matrix is a grid: rows are your choices, columns are the states of the world you cannot see, and each cell is what you get when that row meets that column. Usually choosing a row needs a forecast, a probability for each column. Occasionally one row is at least as good as the other in *every* column, and then you need no forecast at all; game theorists say that row **weakly dominates**. Join is such a row. The sting is in the bottom-left cell: holding out when the rest can win without you does not leave you where you started, it leaves you below it. Tiny number, in cents and illustrative: $u^{\text{in}} = 80$, $u^0 = 60$, $u^{\text{out}} = 25$. Join pays 80, 80, 60 across the three columns; holding out pays 25, 60, 60. Joining is never worse, and in the first column it is 55 points better. The full version, with who gets to join, is the knapsack below and the Shapley value of §13.5.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.85:* "those not participating in the exchange may be better off than those that do, but if too many hold out, all will be worse off. This is the general game theory problem known as the “prisoner’s dilemma.”"
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer's holdout problem runs the opposite way from the uptier's. In a uniform exchange offer, the *holdout* is the free rider: it keeps its old claim at full face while the participants deleverage the company and make the old claim safer. The game's danger is that everyone free-rides and the deal fails. The issuer's response, in Moyer's era, was to make holding out costly (covenant stripping, structural subordination), which is the coercive exchange of §13.1. The non-pro-rata uptier takes that logic to its limit: it makes holding out *maximally* costly and then removes the option to participate from most holders. The payoff matrix above is the result. The 2026 desk departs from Moyer's framing in what it measures. Moyer asks: what participation rate makes the deal work? The desk asks: given the holder register, what is the probability *this* holder is in the minimal winning coalition? That second question is a combinatorial optimization, and it is the sponsor's.

**The sponsor's problem is a knapsack.** The sponsor (acting through the borrower) does not want every lender. It wants the cheapest set of lenders that clears the threshold. Let $c_i$ be the per-dollar concession needed to bring lender $i$ in: the exchange ratio, the new-money economics, the fees. Then

$$
\min_{S \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}} \sum_{i \in S} c_i w_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{i \in S} w_i \ge \theta + \varepsilon .
$$

This is a 0–1 knapsack in its minimization form. It is NP-hard in general and trivial at the sizes that matter: a loan class with a few dozen meaningful holders, of which the top dozen hold most of it, can be brute-forced in milliseconds. The slack $\varepsilon$ is not decorative. It covers holdings that change hands between signing and closing, disputed votes, and the holders who simply do not respond.

> **Math, Gently.** *The knapsack.* Picture the sponsor packing a bag that must weigh *at least* $\theta$ of the class, where every lender packed has a price. The quick way is greedy: pack the cheapest per dollar first until the bag crosses the line. Greedy usually works and sometimes overpays, because the last lender added may be a large one when a small one would have done; Figure 13.2's coalition is topped up by the smallest CLO for exactly that reason. The exact answer checks every possible bag. With 12 holders there are $2^{12} = 4{,}096$ bags, and a laptop checks them all before the coffee cools. "NP-hard" sounds frightening, but it means the count doubles with every extra holder, not that the problem is hard today: at 40 holders it is about a trillion bags, and then you use dynamic programming over holdings rounded to a basis point, or you notice that the bottom 28 holders are dust. The full version is the `min_cost_coalition` listing behind Figure 13.2 (§13.8).

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.87:* "the “conscious” participation rate must make up for certain “innocent” holdouts."
>
> **Annotation.** In a public bond exchange the innocent holdouts are retail holders who never read the notice. In a loan uptier they are the lenders the sponsor never called. The sponsor's coalition must clear $\theta$ with a margin $\varepsilon$, and the knapsack solution is what determines who is "innocent." The modern twist is that innocence is now a choice made by the counterparty, not by the holder.

The cost vector $c_i$ is where your institutional knowledge goes, and it predicts who gets excluded. A distressed fund that can write a new-money ticket is cheap to include: it *brings* value to the coalition. A CLO is expensive, for reasons written into its own rulebook (the seat box below). A lender that has sued this sponsor before is expensive in a different way. The knapsack therefore excludes the fragmented, the constrained, and the litigious, which is roughly who the Serta and Mitel excluded lenders were: in *Serta* the appellants included CLO managers and Citadel; the Fifth Circuit noted that after the bankruptcy, the remainder of the super-priority debt was "probably held by entities like Citadel that did not participate." Figure 13.2 runs the knapsack on the nine-holder register of the §13.8 demo.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The CLO manager.* The cost vector above prices a CLO as an expensive coalition member, and the reasons are in the vehicle's own rulebook. Beyond the 7.5% CCC bucket and the lowest-market-value haircut that Theo remembered at 7:41, a loan bought at a discount stays at its purchase price until it trades above about 90 for more than 30 days, and "the median CLO operates within 4% of the most stringent covenant threshold" (Kundu, ESRB Working Paper 141, March 2023). The cushions are thin in practice: one CLO-equity investor reported market-wide CCC exposure averaging 4.3% and junior overcollateralization cushions of 3.9% (Thomas Majewski, Eagle Point Income Co. shareholder letter, Feb 18, 2026). A CLO often cannot hold equity from an exchange, and its documents may limit the new instruments it can take, so it cannot sign up for a deal that pays in odd paper. That makes the CLO look like the natural victim of an uptier, the lender too constrained to say yes. Serta is the correction: the Prevailing Lenders were large loan managers too, so loan managers sat on both sides of one deal (§13.3.1), and a CLO's seat was decided the way everyone else's was, by who called whom in May. What the CLO seat changes is the exit. A downgrade pushes the bucket over 7.5%, the excess is marked at the bucket's lowest price, and the manager sells, often its higher-priced CCC loans first, because that is what the haircut rule rewards (Kundu's "contractual arbitrage"). The forced seller often sells the wrong loan, and the desk that knows the test dates knows when the forced seller will call.

**The auction between groups.** The richer version has two organized groups, $A$ and $B$, each able to reach $\theta$ by recruiting unaffiliated holders. Each proposes a package to the sponsor. Serta's excluded lenders were exactly this, as Ines read it off the opinion in §13.3.1: the other bidder.

That fact changes the auction's economics. In an ordinary auction a losing bidder gets zero. Here a losing bidder gets primed. Let $T^{\text{win}}$ be the winning group's gain from the transfer and $T^{\text{lose}}$ the losing group's loss from being primed, and let $b$ be the concession the winner pays the sponsor (better terms for the company, more new money, a bigger haircut). Group $B$ is willing to outbid $A$ as long as winning at bid $b$ beats losing:

$$
T^{\text{win}} - b \;\ge\; -T^{\text{lose}} + \pi^{\text{lit}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
b \;\le\; T^{\text{win}} + T^{\text{lose}} - \pi^{\text{lit}} .
$$

With symmetric groups, Bertrand competition drives the winning bid to that bound. The sponsor extracts not only the winner's gain but also the loser's *fear*, less the loser's expected litigation recovery $\pi^{\text{lit}}$. This is the auction-with-externalities logic of Jehiel, Moldovanu and Stacchetti, *How (Not) to Sell Nuclear Weapons* (American Economic Review, 1996): when losing hurts, bidders pay to avoid it, and the seller can capture more than the item is worth to the winner. In LMT terms, equity captures the transfer by auctioning the right not to be its victim.

> **Math, Gently.** *Auctions where the loser gets hurt.* In an ordinary auction the loser goes home with nothing and loses nothing, so nobody bids more than the item is worth to them. Change one rule, so that the loser is primed, and each bidder will now pay up to its gain from winning *plus* its fear of losing. When two similar bidders keep undercutting each other's terms (economists call this Bertrand competition, after a price war between two identical shops), the winning bid is pushed all the way to that ceiling. Tiny number: the seat is worth $T^{\text{win}} = 20$ points to the winner, being primed costs $T^{\text{lose}} = 30$, and suing afterwards is worth $\pi^{\text{lit}} = 10$. The ceiling is $20 + 30 - 10 = 40$. A group will hand the sponsor up to 40 points of concessions for a seat worth 20 to it, because the alternative costs 30, less the 10 a court might give back. The only party at the table that profits from the fear is the equity. The full version is the inequality above and the Jehiel–Moldovanu–Stacchetti paper.

**The litigation outside option.** The term that disciplines the auction is

$$
\pi^{\text{lit}} = q \cdot \mathbb{E}[\text{damages} \mid \text{win}] - \text{cost},
$$

where $q$ is the probability a court finds the deal impermissible. $q$ depends on the clause and the forum, and since December 31, 2024, the dependence is sharp: high for an `open_market` exception litigated in the Fifth Circuit after *Serta*, low for a `purchase` exception in New York after *Mitel*. The damages term now has an anchor: \$261.13mm plus 9% interest on roughly \$895mm of excluded holdings in *Serta*. A higher $\pi^{\text{lit}}$ lowers the sponsor's take and, through the inequality, makes groups less willing to bid. That is the channel through which a court ruling moves the frequency of deals, not just their legality.

**Levers the sponsor pulls.** Buccola and Kahan (*Getting to Yes*, HLS Roundtable, Mar 25, 2025; SSRN abstract 5014946) catalog the sources of coercion in these deals. In the language of the matrix, every lever does one of three things. It lowers $u^{\text{out}}$ (how deeply non-participants are primed), it raises $u^{\text{in}} - u^0$ (how rich the participants' deal is), or it lowers the effective $\theta$. The third is the most aggressive. In Wesco/Incora (2022), per Mayer Brown's account of the record, the participating group held more than two-thirds of the 2024 notes but *not* of the 2026 notes before the transaction began; the steps amended the indentures and issued additional secured notes to clear the threshold, the participants put in \$250mm of new money for new secured notes, and the liens securing the old notes were then released. Issue yourself enough notes to become two-thirds, then vote as two-thirds. Section 13.9 follows what the courts made of that.

**When the knapsack is the wrong model.** The knapsack assumes the sponsor assembles the coalition and the lenders wait to be called. Robertshaw shows the coalition assembling itself, twice. A May 2023 super-priority deal had defined "Required Lender" as more than 50% of the first-out plus second-out loans, read from the agent's register. In July 2023 Invesco quietly bought enough of those loans to cross 50%, and became Required Lender without anyone calling it. An ad hoc group of the other lenders (Bain Capital Credit, Canyon and Eaton Vance) and the sponsor, One Rock, answered on December 11, 2023: they lent \$228.3mm to RS Funding, a vehicle the parent voted and Robertshaw economically owned, and the cash prepaid \$117.6mm of first-out loans. Invesco fell below 50%, and the other group became Required Lender in its place. Robertshaw filed for Chapter 11 on February 15, 2024, and on June 20, 2024, Judge Lopez held that the new group were the Required Lenders and that Invesco's remedy was a money claim against the company, not its control back. Required-Lender status turned out to be a position you can trade into and out of, at prices nobody publishes. For the model, that means `holder_hhi` and every coalition input must be re-read from the register as often as the register can be had, not lifted from the last 10-Q or the last Rule 2019 statement. When the register is the battlefield, the knapsack is a snapshot of a fight, and the sponsor is only one of the people packing bags.

**Why prices drift before announcements.** One more refinement explains a pattern every loan trader has seen. Lenders do not observe $V$ exactly. Each gets a noisy signal $s_i = V + \sigma \epsilon_i$. In the global-games framework of Morris and Shin (*Unique Equilibrium in a Model of Self-Fulfilling Currency Attacks*, American Economic Review, 1998), a coordination game with noisy private signals has a unique threshold equilibrium: each player joins the coalition if and only if its signal falls below a cutoff $s^*$, so the coalition forms if and only if $V$ is below some $V^*$. The cutoff is common knowledge in equilibrium, which means *the act of organizing is itself a signal that the organizers' estimates of $V$ are low*. Loan prices drift down as groups organize, before anything is announced. For the desk, an organizing signal (counsel hired, a Rule 2019 statement, a co-op reported) is a downward update on $V$ and an upward update on the PRS at the same time.

> **Math, Gently.** *Global games in one picture.* Every lender in the class carries a thermometer, and every thermometer is slightly off. Nobody knows the company's true temperature, $V$. But each lender knows that everyone else's thermometer reads close to its own: if mine says "cold," yours probably says "cold-ish," and you know that mine does, and I know that you know. That chain of "I know that you know" collapses to a single cutoff reading. Below it you join a group; above it you don't. Nobody has to agree with anybody, and the cutoff still emerges from the noise. Tiny number: if the cutoff reading is 1,500 and thermometers err by about 100, a lender reading 1,400 joins, and it expects most others to have read below 1,500 too, which is why a group can form in a week once the first call is made. The full version is Morris and Shin's paper; the desk-sized consequence is that organizing is itself a reading of the thermometer.

Prices usually drift down as groups organize. Not always, and the exception teaches the rule. When the threshold is a supermajority and a minority can block it, the tranche that carries the blocking vote becomes a seat, and seats can be bid up. Wesco Aircraft's 2026 secured notes needed two-thirds consent for anything with "the effect of" releasing substantially all collateral. In February 2022 a minority group announced it held more than a third of the 2026s, enough to block the majority's plan, and the two sides raced for the marginal bond; the 2026 notes reportedly ran from about 85 to above par in the scramble (a secondary account; we have not seen the prints). The price moved *up* because the seat, not the company, was being bought. A model that reads every organizing headline as bad news for the bond will get that tranche exactly backwards, and §13.9 tells the rest of that story.

> **Poker Table.** "The sponsor isn't a player," Ines says. "The sponsor is the dealer, and the dealer chooses who sees the flop. Every lender at the table thinks they're playing the other lenders. They're playing for a seat." She taps the screen. "So when that client offers the Larkspur loan at 78, I don't ask what the loan is worth. I ask which seat the seller has." Her hand history, replayed through markouts: suppose the desk had bought the \$15mm at 78, the uptier closed three weeks later, and the excluded tranche marked at 52 *(illustrative)*. The markout is $-26$ points, or $-\$3.9$mm, on one ticket. Across many such hands the average markout on "client sells after an organizing headline" is the market's estimate of $\Pr(\text{excluded}) \cdot \mathbb{E}[\Delta R^{\text{excl}}]$, which is exactly what §13.8 tries to forecast before the headline.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.86:* "as in poker, which is analogous because the “multiple-hand” format of the game is similar to the series of transactions in the distressed marketplace, to be effective one must have a credible “bluff.”"
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer's point is about repeated play: an investor who never holds out cannot threaten to, so investors sometimes act against their immediate interest to keep the threat credible for future deals. The LMT era made this literal. A small set of sponsors and a small set of distressed funds meet across many capital structures, and a sponsor's history of non-pro-rata deals is a public record of its type. That is why `sponsor_repeat_lme` is a feature and why it carries a positive sign. Lenders have repeated-game reputations too, and the cooperation agreement (§13.5) is the institution they built to make their threats credible collectively when no single lender's bluff is large enough to matter.

**Autumn 2023: the wall.** Lumen Technologies and its subsidiary Level 3 had about \$10bn of debt due in 2027 and a market that did not want to refinance any of it. On October 31, 2023, the company signed a transaction support agreement, the TSA, with a group of creditors holding "over \$7 billion" of its debt. The first version of the deal had pieces for nearly every layer: \$1.2bn of new Level 3 first-lien money, exchanges of Level 3 first-lien notes that needed a 66.7% consent threshold, up to \$2bn of Level 3 unsecured notes moved into a second lien, and Lumen term loans into super-priority paper. Kaz slides the sheet down to the week of November 9, 2023, where a research note has the prices for paper outside the TSA. "Here is why this is fine," says Kaz, who has read ahead in this section to the paragraph with the inequality, and is visibly pleased about it. "The group holds more than $1-\theta$ of every class it cares about. So no coalition can form without it. So the game is bargaining, not an auction. "The excluded are the people outside the agreement," says Mags. "Look at the Level 3 unsecured 2028s. Forty-seven. "It's a ten-point word," says Ines. She has the same research note open and is doing what she always does with a spread, which is turn it into a bet. If being left out costs you something like twenty-five points of recovery, ten points says the market thinks there's about a forty percent chance you stay left out. If it costs forty, the market says one in four." She writes $\Pr(\text{excluded}) \times \text{loss} \approx 10$ on the whiteboard, under Kaz's flower and Theo's Serta options, which is getting to be a crowded whiteboard. "Sign," says Mags, at once. "I've watched what happens to people who stay out."

"It depends what four months of not being able to sell is worth to you," says Ines. "For a fund with redemptions, a lot. For us, the question is whether ten points of seat beats four months of optionality. On a bond at 73 it usually does. But that's a price, not a principle."

Kaz, for once, says nothing. Kaz is waiting for the sheet.

**January to March 2024: the deal.** On January 22, 2024, the TSA was amended to cover "over \$12.5 billion" of debt, more than 70% of the Lumen and Level 3 debt due through 2027, with \$1.325bn of new money and a \$1bn revolver. The structure had a seam down the middle. The term-loan pieces were open to all lenders. The note pieces were privately negotiated and "not offered to all holders." The closing thresholds were written into the agreement: 66.7% for the Level 3 secured notes, 50.1% for the unsecured notes, 95% for Lumen's Term A and A-1 loans. Each subscriber's new money was capped at \$250mm, transfers to non-parties were void, and the outside date was February 29, 2024.

It closed on March 22, 2024, with participation of 94.4% in the Lumen Term A and A-1 loans, 98.5% in the Lumen Term B and 99.5% in the Level 3 Term B. More than \$15bn of debt and commitments were addressed. The 2027 maturities fell from about \$9.5bn to about \$800mm, and those due in 2025 and 2026 from about \$2.1bn to about \$600mm. (Cooperation agreements signed in this book so far: 3.)

**Mags was right about the mechanism.** The non-TSA noteholders were primed, exactly as she said. The 2028 unsecured holder at 47 woke up behind a second lien held by its own classmates. No suit by excluded holders is reported; one research shop called the indenture claims "difficult." On every measure the chapter has built so far, this is the Serta picture: a digital put written by people who were not asked.

Here is the idea that reconciles them, and it arrives formally a few paragraphs below. The co-op's bargain decides the *seats*; enterprise value decides what the seats are *worth*. In Nash-bargaining terms, the joint surplus $\Pi$ was large, because there was a fiber business someone would pay \$5.75bn for, and the company's outside option $d_{\text{sp}}$ was small, because nobody else was offering to refinance \$10bn of 2027 maturities. Large $\Pi$ and small $d_{\text{sp}}$ put the deal deep inside the deal region and hand the co-op most of the surplus. But a large $\Pi$ also means the company comes out the other side with enough value to clean up its leftovers, and the cheapest way to clean up an orphaned bond is to buy it. The excluded holder's recovery was the waterfall of §13.3 evaluated at a $V$ far above $K_1$, plus a company that wanted a simpler balance sheet. Kaz's inequality had nothing to do with it.

That is also the answer, in part, to Kaz's question from the cold open about why "excluded" means 25 in one case and 99 in another. At Serta the court valued the excluded loans at 25 on closing day, and the company filed thirty-one months later. At Lumen the company sold an asset for enough to pay everyone. Same seat; different $V$.

**What the document does to the game.** A cooperation agreement is a set of lenders $C$, with combined holdings $s_C = \sum_{i \in C} w_i$, who promise not to negotiate separately, require any buyer of their loans to sign on, and agree on a sunset date. The number that matters is $s_C$ against $1 - \theta$. If $s_C > 1 - \theta$, any coalition $S$ that avoids the co-op satisfies

$$
\sum_{i \in S} w_i \;\le\; 1 - s_C \;<\; \theta ,
$$

so it cannot pass the amendment. Every winning coalition must contain a co-op member, and the no-separate-deal covenant turns "contains a member" into "contains the co-op." The sponsor's knapsack from §13.4 has no feasible solution that leaves the group out. The $n$-lender game, with its invitation list and its prisoner's dilemma, collapses into bargaining between two players: the sponsor and $C$.

> **Poker Table.** "A co-op," says Ines, "is the players agreeing not to play heads-up against the house. One at a time, every one of them can be dealt out. Together they're a blocking stack, and the dealer has to deal with the stack." Her hand history: the Larkspur loan, bid 78 before the co-op headline, trades at 81 the morning after *(illustrative)*. The three points are the market repricing $\Pr(\text{excluded})$, not $V$.

> **Math, Gently.** *Nash bargaining.* Two people are splitting a pie, and each has a walk-away: what they get if the talks fail. Neither will accept less than its walk-away, so the only thing really being bargained over is the pie *left over* after both walk-aways are paid. Nash's answer is that each side gets its walk-away plus a share of the leftover, the share set by bargaining strength. Tiny number: the pie is $\Pi = 100$, the co-op's walk-away is $d_C = 20$, the sponsor's is $d_{\text{sp}} = 30$, and they are equally strong, $\eta = \tfrac12$. The leftover is $100 - 20 - 30 = 50$, so the co-op gets $20 + 25 = 45$. Now let the sponsor find a better walk-away, an outside lender who will do a deal at 40. The leftover shrinks to 40 and the co-op gets $20 + 20 = 40$. Ten dollars of sponsor walk-away cost the co-op five, which is $\eta$ per dollar. That is the whole of the comparative static below. The full version is "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section.

**Nash bargaining.** Let $\Pi$ be the joint surplus from a deal (avoided Chapter 11 costs, runway, the equity's option value from an extension), $d_C$ the co-op's disagreement payoff (its litigation value or its Chapter 11 recovery), and $d_{\text{sp}}$ the sponsor's (a third-party "deal away," or Chapter 11). With $\eta \in (0,1)$ the co-op's bargaining weight, the generalized Nash solution gives the co-op

$$
\Pi_C = d_C + \eta\,\big(\Pi - d_C - d_{\text{sp}}\big),
$$

with the symmetric split at $\eta = \tfrac12$. Two boundary conditions matter. A deal exists only if $\Pi \ge d_C + d_{\text{sp}}$; otherwise each side's outside option beats the deal, and that is when you see deals away and free-fall filings. Inside the deal region, the comparative static is linear and brutal:

$$
\frac{\partial \Pi_C}{\partial d_{\text{sp}}} = -\eta .
$$

Every dollar of credible outside financing the sponsor can line up costs the co-op $\eta$ dollars at the table. This is why deals away came back in 2025, often with six-month par calls (Wachtell, Feb 2026). The par call makes the threat cheap: if the co-op folds later, the sponsor refinances the outside money at par after six months, so the cost of *executing* the threat is roughly six months of excess spread plus fees, while $d_{\text{sp}}$ rises by the full value of the alternative. The co-op's counter is to be the cheapest financing in the room. Miller (SSRN abstract 5549919) finds that "by operating collectively, incumbent lenders outbid third-party financing offers."

**Carve-out premiums are Shapley values.** Inside the co-op, members bargain with each other, and small holders have more power than their size suggests. Treat the co-op's blocking power as a weighted majority game in which a subset $T \subseteq C$ blocks if $\sum_{i \in T} w_i > 1 - \theta$. The Shapley value of member $i$ is the probability, over random joining orders, that $i$ is the member who turns a non-blocking subset into a blocking one (the summation formula is in "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section).

> **Math, Gently.** *The Shapley value.* Line the co-op's members up at the door in a random order and let them walk in one at a time. At some moment the people in the room first hold enough to block; whoever walked in at that moment is the *pivot*. Do this for every possible order and count how often each member is the pivot. That fraction is its Shapley value: its share of the power, measured by how often the group's power depends on it. Four members can line up in $4! = 24$ orders, few enough to count on a napkin. In the example that follows, A is the pivot in 10 of them, B in 10, and C and D in 2 each, so the values are $\tfrac{10}{24}, \tfrac{10}{24}, \tfrac{2}{24}, \tfrac{2}{24}$. Size is not power; being indispensable is.

Take a lien release with $\theta = \tfrac23$, so blocking needs more than a third, and a co-op of four: A holds 25%, B 6%, C and D 3% each, 37% in all. The blocking subsets are $\{A,B,C\}$ and $\{A,B,D\}$ (34% each) and all four; $\{A,C,D\}$ reaches only 31%. A and B sit in every blocking subset, so both hold vetoes. Counting orderings gives $\phi = (\tfrac{5}{12}, \tfrac{5}{12}, \tfrac{1}{12}, \tfrac{1}{12})$. B holds a quarter of A's position and an equal share of the blocking power. When B asks for a carve-out premium (a larger new-money allocation, a bigger backstop fee), it is asking to be paid its Shapley value instead of its pro rata share, and the arithmetic is on its side. A steering committee's carve-out premium is a Shapley value with a law firm attached. The code in §13.8 checks these four numbers. Figure 13.3 plots both splits.

**Three flavours, one failure mode.** Co-ops come *open* (any holder of the class may join), *closed* (membership fixed at signing), or *tiered* (anyone may join; early signers get the premium). A closed co-op that clears $\theta$ on its own is no longer a defense against a non-pro-rata deal. It *is* one, waiting for a term sheet; Miller (2025) calls the pattern "inclusive, but unequal." This is why `coop_reported` carries the split sign in the §13.2.1 table: it lowers the probability of a deal that excludes the co-op and raises the probability of *some* LMT, because a co-op that can block can also deliver.

**The antitrust attack is an attack on the commitment device.** Wachtell reports that "antitrust challenges in connection with cooperation agreements (Optimum and Selecta) are in their early days." In the model, a successful challenge voids the no-separate-deal covenant, $s_C$ becomes a sum of individual holdings that can each be bid away, and the game reverts to the §13.4 auction. Issuers have found a contractual route to the same place: Seaton (SSRN abstract 5247506) reports that Warner Bros. Discovery's 2025 bonds pioneered an "anti-cartel" provision, and that issuers' equity falls on co-op news. The second fact is the Nash solution showing up in a stock price: a co-op raises $d_C$, and the sponsor's share falls by $1 - \eta$ per dollar of it.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The opportunistic credit fund.* To this seat a bond is an option on a legal process. #### The Full Derivation: bargaining weights and pivots

**The Nash solution.** The generalized Nash solution maximizes $(\Pi_C - d_C)^{\eta}(\Pi_{\text{sp}} - d_{\text{sp}})^{1-\eta}$ subject to $\Pi_C + \Pi_{\text{sp}} = \Pi$, where $\eta \in (0,1)$ is the co-op's bargaining weight. Take logs and differentiate in $\Pi_C$:

$$
\frac{\eta}{\Pi_C - d_C} = \frac{1-\eta}{\Pi - \Pi_C - d_{\text{sp}}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Pi_C = d_C + \eta\,\big(\Pi - d_C - d_{\text{sp}}\big).
$$

Differentiating the solution in $d_{\text{sp}}$ gives $\partial \Pi_C / \partial d_{\text{sp}} = -\eta$, and the solution is interior only when $\Pi \ge d_C + d_{\text{sp}}$.

**The Shapley summation.** For the blocking game on the co-op $C$, member $i$'s Shapley value is

$$
\phi_i = \sum_{T \subseteq C \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|T|!\,(|C| - |T| - 1)!}{|C|!}\,\big(v(T \cup \{i\}) - v(T)\big), \qquad v(T) = \mathbf{1}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{j \in T} w_j > 1 - \theta\Big].
$$

The weight $|T|!\,(|C|-|T|-1)!/|C|!$ is the fraction of joining orders in which exactly the members of $T$ arrive before $i$, and $v(T \cup \{i\}) - v(T)$ is 1 exactly when $i$ is the pivot of such an order. For the four-member example, the 12 orders whose first three members include both A and B have their pivot in third place (A or B four times each, C or D twice each), and the other 12 orders have their pivot in fourth place (A six times, B six times), which gives $(\tfrac{10}{24}, \tfrac{10}{24}, \tfrac{2}{24}, \tfrac{2}{24}) = (\tfrac{5}{12}, \tfrac{5}{12}, \tfrac{1}{12}, \tfrac{1}{12})$.

## 13.6 Drop-Downs: Collateral Leakage Arithmetic

An uptier reorders the claims and leaves the assets where they are. A drop-down leaves the claims where they are and moves the assets. Same transfer, different mechanism, and a different payoff shape.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.244:* "if the existing bank debt has a lien on substantially all the assets, there may be little or nothing to offer."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer is describing the constraint on a classic coercive exchange: the issuer wants to offer bondholders secured paper, but the banks already hold the collateral. The drop-down is the modern answer to his problem. If nothing is unencumbered, *manufacture* unencumbered collateral by moving assets out of the restricted group through the investment and unrestricted-subsidiary baskets, then grant liens on them. Moyer's next item suggests offering "a structurally senior security"; the drop-down does both at once, because debt issued by the unrestricted subsidiary is structurally senior to the parent's lenders on the assets that moved. Where the desk departs: Moyer treats the absence of free collateral as a constraint. The 2026 desk treats it as a quantity, $C^{\text{unsub}}$, that a parser can measure and a model can rank.

**The arithmetic.** Let the collateral group be worth $V$ before the deal. The borrower moves assets worth $T$ to an unrestricted subsidiary, with $T \le C^{\text{unsub}}$ as declared at the time of the investment. (Agreements often let the borrower value the investment itself, in good faith, which makes $C^{\text{unsub}}$ a cap on the *declared* value of $T$, not its economic value. Read the valuation clause before you trust the cap.) The subsidiary borrows $D_u$ against the assets, structurally senior on them, and a fraction $\phi$ of the proceeds comes back into the collateral group as cash or debt paydown. The restricted group keeps the subsidiary's equity, worth $(T - D_u)^+$ at resolution. The existing lenders now recover

$$
R^{\text{post}}(V) = \frac{\min\!\big(V - T + \phi D_u + (T - D_u)^+,\; F\big)}{F}.
$$

Sanity checks: $T = 0$ returns $R_0(V) = \min(V,F)/F$. With $\phi = 1$ and $D_u = T$ the lenders keep every dollar but swap an asset for cash, and cash is the first thing a distressed company spends (the $\kappa$ of §13.3 again). The J.Crew trapdoor is the opposite corner, $\phi \to 0$ and $D_u \to T$: the asset leaves, is levered to the hilt, and the proceeds pay a different class. Then

$$
\Delta R(V) = R_0(V) - R^{\text{post}}(V) = \frac{\min(V, F) - \min(V - T, F)}{F} =
\begin{cases}
T/F, & V \le F, \\[1ex]
(F + T - V)/F, & F < V < F + T, \\[1ex]
0, & V \ge F + T.
\end{cases}
$$

Compare the shape with §13.3. The uptier's excluded lender wrote a *digital put*: it loses everything below $K_1$ and little above. The drop-down's victim sold a *forward*: it loses $T/F$ in every state where it matters. And for any $\phi \ge 0$ the loss is capped by capacity, because $V - T + \phi D_u + (T - D_u)^+ \ge V - T$ implies

$$
\Delta R(V) \;\le\; \frac{T}{F} \;\le\; \frac{C^{\text{unsub}}}{F}.
$$

That bound is why `unsub_capacity_pct_ev` is a feature, and why the IP blockers exist: a blocker sets $T$ to zero for the assets it names. It also tells you how the market prices the two species. A digital put is a tail problem that the market prices slowly, as the probability of a low $V$ rises. A forward is a level problem that the market prices on the day the investment is announced. Figure 13.4 puts the two shapes on one axis.

**PetSmart and the guarantee lever.** BC Partners bought PetSmart in 2015 for about \$8.7bn and bought Chewy in May 2017 for \$3.35bn. In June 2018 PetSmart moved 20% of Chewy to its parent, Argos Holdings, and 16.5% to an unrestricted subsidiary, and sued the term loan agent, Citibank, that month *(approx.; Wikipedia, citing Reuters, the FT and the WSJ)*. The lever was the guarantor-release clause: the position was that a guarantor that stops being wholly owned is released. Moving 36.5% of the shares was argued to release all of the guarantee, so the effective $T$ was the guarantee's value, not the shares'. That is leverage on the transfer, and it is why the code should compute $T$ from the release clause, not from the share count. Chewy listed in June 2019 at \$22. The `chewy_blocker` (no release merely because a guarantor stops being wholly owned) exists because of those two sentences.

**When steps are judged together.** In *STG Logistics* (N.Y. Sup. Ct., Jan 3, 2026), the court viewed "amendments permitting a covenant strip, a drop-down exchange, and an intercompany loan – as a single integrated plan for purposes of analyzing 'sacred rights' (i.e., unanimous lender vote) claims," denied the motion to dismiss, and "shortly following the decision, the company filed for bankruptcy" (Wachtell, Feb 2026). A drop-down that needs only majority votes step by step can need unanimity as a whole. Section 13.9 makes that precise.

**When to reach for the capped forward, and when it breaks.** The forward picture, a flat loss of $T/F$ in every state that matters with capacity as the ceiling, is the right first model when two things are true: the valuation clause is honest, and the baskets are readable. Then $C^{\text{unsub}}$ is a number a parser can produce, $T$ is bounded by it, and the bound is the trade: a loan whose document lets 20% of enterprise value walk out the door is short a 20-point forward, and the market will price most of it on the day the investment is announced. It breaks in three ways, each from a case above. If the borrower values the investment itself, in good faith, the cap bounds the *declared* $T$ and not the economic one, and a parser that reads the cap as a fact will understate the loss. If a guarantee falls away when a subsidiary stops being wholly owned, the effective $T$ is the guarantee's value rather than the shares' (PetSmart), and the forward has leverage the basket arithmetic cannot see. And if a court reads several steps as one transaction, the whole move may need a vote it never got, as in *STG*, where the New York ruling came in early January 2026 and the company filed for Chapter 11 nine days later, on January 12, 2026. In that case the forward has a knock-out, and its value depends on a forum rather than a formula. A seasoned reader checks the valuation clause first, the guarantor-release clause second, and only then does the arithmetic.

## 13.7 Double-Dips and Pari-Plus: A Waterfall Engine That Conserves Value

"I'm told the new financing is a double-dip," says Kaz. "Is that a hygiene issue?"

"At a party," says Mags. "In a capital structure, it's a structure."

The desk's working definition: a guarantee and an intercompany loan walk into the same collateral pool and order twice. Pari-plus adds a foreign subsidiary that buys the first round.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.165:* "Grants of security interests can similarly be viewed as provisions to override the general rule that property in an estate is shared pro rata among creditors."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer frames liens (and, on the same page, guarantees and subordination) as overrides of the pari passu default. A double-dip is an override built from *claims* instead of liens: the new lender holds two claims of equal rank against the same obligor for one dollar of credit. Nothing about it is senior. It is counted twice in the pro rata denominator, and pro rata sharing does the rest. The desk's departure is computational. Moyer's overrides can be read off a priority chart. A double-dip can only be read off a *graph* of obligors, claims and equity ownership, which is why the engine below is a fixed-point solver rather than a lookup table.

**The closed form.** A special-purpose vehicle (SPV) borrows $F_2$ from new lenders and lends the cash to OpCo under an intercompany note that ranks pari passu with OpCo's existing first lien, $F_1$. OpCo also guarantees the SPV's debt, pari passu. In a pro rata distribution of OpCo's value $V$, three claims share: the first lien ($F_1$), the guarantee ($F_2$) and the note ($F_2$), at the rate $\rho = \min\big(1, V/(F_1 + 2F_2)\big)$. The new lenders collect $\rho F_2$ on the guarantee directly and, through the SPV, the $\rho F_2$ it collects on the note, capped at what they are owed. While $2\rho < 1$, that is, $V < (F_1 + 2F_2)/2$,

$$
R^{\text{DD}} = \frac{2V}{F_1 + 2F_2}, \qquad R^{\text{1L}} = \frac{V}{F_1 + 2F_2}.
$$

The double-dipper recovers exactly twice what the first lien recovers, from a claim of the same rank. With illustrative numbers $F_1 = 1{,}000$, $F_2 = 500$ (\$mm) and $V = 800$: without the dip both would get $800/1{,}500 = 53.3$ cents; with it, the first lien gets 40 and the new money 80. The \$133mm the first lien lost is exactly the \$133mm the new money gained, and on this desk that equality is the first thing anyone confirms, before the deal terms and long before the lawyers. Figure 13.5 draws the structure and the recoveries.

Above the kink the arithmetic turns circular, and for once Kaz is the most comfortable person at the whiteboard. Once $V$ passes $F_2 + F_1/2$, the new lenders' two claims would collect more than they are owed, so the SPV ends up holding cash it does not need, and that excess is SPV equity. If OpCo owns the SPV, the equity flows back up into OpCo. A bigger OpCo pays more on every pro rata claim, the note included, so the SPV's excess grows, so OpCo grows again. Theo draws an arrow from OpCo to the SPV, another arrow back, and stares at the loop.

"It's a dog chasing its tail," he says.

"It's a dog that runs half as far each lap," says Kaz, and starts a column of numbers. Take $V = 1{,}200$ with the same $F_1 = 1{,}000$ and $F_2 = 500$, so OpCo's three pro rata claims add up to 2,000. Guess that OpCo pays 50 cents. Then the SPV collects 250 on its note, owes its lenders the 250 the guarantee did not cover, and has nothing left over, so OpCo has just its own 1,200, which pays 60 cents, not 50. Guess 60: the SPV collects 300, needs 200, returns 100; OpCo has 1,300 and pays 65. Guess 65: the SPV returns 150, and OpCo pays 67.5. The column reads 50, 60, 65, 67.5, 68.75, and the distance to 70 is cut in half on every line: 20, 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25.

"Seventy," says Kaz. "At seventy the SPV collects 350, needs 150, hands back 200. OpCo has 1,400, and 1,400 over 2,000 is seventy. Nothing moves."

"Back in my day," says Mags, "the circularity was resolved by whoever hung up the phone last." She studies the column. "This is better. It stays in this room that I said so."

Seventy cents is the fixed point, and it has a clean meaning. The first lien collects $0.70 \times 1{,}000 = 700$; the new lenders collect 350 on the guarantee and 150 through the SPV, which is exactly the 500 they lent. In general, above the kink,

$$
\rho = \frac{V - F_2}{F_1},
$$

so the new lenders take $F_2$ and the first lien takes everything else, as conservation demands. The regimes meet at $\rho = \tfrac12$, so recoveries are continuous and monotone in $V$. Kaz's halving is not luck. Each lap moves the payment rate by $2F_2/(F_1 + 2F_2)$ times the previous lap's move, and with these numbers that ratio is exactly one half. The fixed-point map has slope $2F_2/(F_1 + 2F_2) < 1$, so simple iteration converges geometrically. (The algebra is in "The Full Derivation: the clearing map" at the end of this section.)

> **Math, Gently.** *Fixed points, and why iteration converges.* A fixed point of a rule is an input the rule hands back unchanged: feed in 70 cents, get 70 cents out. A surprising number of problems in this book have the shape "the answer depends on itself" (a recovery that depends on equity that depends on the recovery; later, a quote that depends on an inventory that depends on the quote), and the honest way to solve them is to guess, apply the rule, and feed the output back in. Whether that settles down depends on one number, the rule's slope: how far the output moves when the input moves by one. Think of a thermostat set to 70. A good one closes part of the gap each cycle and creeps up on the target. A twitchy one overshoots, but by less each time, and still settles. A broken one overshoots by more each time, and the room swings between sauna and meat locker. A slope between $-1$ and $1$ is the good or the twitchy thermostat; a slope bigger than 1 in size is the broken one. Here the slope is $2F_2/(F_1 + 2F_2)$, which is below 1 whenever there is any first lien at all, and with $F_1 = 1{,}000$ and $F_2 = 500$ it is exactly $0.5$, so every step halves the error. An error of a whole dollar per dollar of face falls below $10^{-10}$ in about 33 steps, because $2^{33}$ is about 8.6 billion. A slope of 0.99 would need about 2,300 steps for the same accuracy, which is why the engine of §13.8 allows 10,000 passes and raises an error, rather than quietly returning a number, when it runs out. The full version, for any number of entities, is the Eisenberg–Noe clearing map in "The Full Derivation" below.

Real structures have more entities than one OpCo and one SPV, and the general tool is the clearing framework of Eisenberg and Noe (*Systemic Risk in Financial Systems*, Management Science, 2001), extended to priority classes. It is Kaz's whiteboard column, done for every entity at once. Each debtor has a pot: its external value, plus whatever its own intercompany claims were paid, plus whatever is left over at the subsidiaries it owns. Each debtor pays its claims rank by rank, senior first, and pays every claim within a rank the same fraction of what is still owed on it. The pots depend on the payments and the payments depend on the pots, so the engine guesses, pays, recomputes the pots, and pays again, until no payment moves by more than $10^{-10}$. The invariant is conservation: payments to outside creditors plus residual outside equity equal total external value, because every intercompany payment is one entity's outflow and another's inflow. The precise map is in "The Full Derivation: the clearing map."

**Pari-plus.** Add a non-guarantor foreign subsidiary worth $V_f$ that guarantees the new money directly. Before the deal, $V_f$ reached the first lien only as equity flowing up to OpCo. After it, the new lenders take $\min(F_2, V_f)$ first and double-dip OpCo for the deficiency, so the first lien loses the foreign value and is diluted on the rest. With $V_f = 0$ the structure collapses to the double-dip, which the code asserts. Sabre's May 2023 financing is the template: a "\$665 million new senior secured credit facility" from "lenders led by affiliates of Centerbridge Partners, L.P." to Sabre Financial Borrower, LLC, "a newly created wholly-owned subsidiary of Sabre GLBL," "secured by an intercompany loan by the Borrower to Sabre GLBL" and by "direct guarantees from, and security interest in assets of, certain foreign subsidiaries of Sabre," with proceeds funding capped tenders for three series of notes (8-K Ex. 99.1, May 25, 2023).

#### The Full Derivation: the clearing map

**The double-dip fixed point.** Above the kink, let $A$ be OpCo's distributable value including the returned SPV equity $(2\rho - 1)F_2$, with $\rho = A/(F_1 + 2F_2)$. Then

$$
A = V + \Big(\frac{2A}{F_1 + 2F_2} - 1\Big)F_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\rho = \frac{V - F_2}{F_1}.
$$

Written as a map on the payment rate, one pass sends $\rho$ to $\big(V - F_2 + 2\rho F_2\big)/(F_1 + 2F_2)$. Its slope in $\rho$ is $2F_2/(F_1 + 2F_2) < 1$, so the map is a contraction on the regime $\rho \ge \tfrac12$ and the iteration converges geometrically to the unique fixed point there. Kaz's column is this map at $V = 1{,}200$: $\rho \mapsto 0.35 + 0.5\rho$.

**The general map.** For a debtor $d$ with external value $e_d$, define its distributable value as external value plus intercompany receipts plus the residual equity $E_c$ of the subsidiaries $c$ it owns. A claim $j$ of rank $r$ owed by $d$ is then paid

$$
A_d(p) = e_d + \sum_{\ell:\ \text{cred}(\ell) = d} p_\ell + \sum_{c:\ \text{parent}(c) = d} E_c(p),
\qquad
p_j = \bar p_j \cdot \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{\big(A_d - \sum_{\text{rank}(m) < r} p_m\big)^+}{\sum_{\text{rank}(m) = r} \bar p_m}\right),
$$

where the sums in the fraction run over $d$'s own claims and $\bar p_j$ is the amount still owed on claim $j$ (face, less anything already collected from a co-obligor on the same debt). Iterate $p \mapsto \Phi(p)$ until $\|p^{(k+1)} - p^{(k)}\|_\infty < 10^{-10}$. Every payment is non-decreasing in every other payment, so $\Phi$ is monotone: iterating up from $p = 0$ climbs to the least clearing vector, and iterating down from face descends to the greatest. When every loop of ownership and claims has some outside value behind it, as in the structures of this chapter, the two meet; when they do not, the first item of "Ways This Blows Up" in §13.7.1 applies.

### 13.7.1 Case Study: Trinseo (2023–2026), the Double-Dip Meets Chapter 11

Theo meets Trinseo the way restructuring bankers meet most companies, through a disclosure statement. This one is dated Monday, May 25, 2026, which was Memorial Day, and it reaches the desk on Tuesday as an exhibit to an 8-K, filed the same day as the petitions. He reads it with the pleasure of a man back in his native language. Then he reads the history, because the history is where the claims came from.

On September 8, 2023, Trinseo announced "a senior secured term loan facility of \$1,077 million" borrowed by two SPVs, Trinseo Luxco Finance SPV S.à r.l. and Trinseo NA Finance SPV LLC. Of that, \$948mm was on-lent to OpCo as "2023 Term Loans" under its 2017 credit agreement. The SPV loans paid Term SOFR + 8.50% and matured May 3, 2028; the intercompany loans paid Adjusted Term SOFR + 9.66% and matured May 3, 2030. Proceeds repaid the 2018 term loans and redeemed \$385mm of 5.375% notes due 2025 (8-K, Sept 2023). Note the design: the intercompany loan pays more and runs longer than the external loan it funds, so the claim that shares pro rata with OpCo's lenders grows faster than the debt behind it.

What the 8-K did not describe, the excluded lenders later did, and their account is an allegation rather than a finding. By their telling, \$125mm of the SPV money went to OpCo as equity, which manufactured enough basket room to move a 50% interest in the Americas Styrenics joint venture (AmSty, about \$100mm of EBITDA) into an unrestricted subsidiary, and that subsidiary guaranteed the new loan. No existing lender was asked to consent, because no clause required it. If the allegation holds, the double-dip of §13.7 had a pari-plus leg from its first day: value the new lenders could reach and the OpCo lenders could not.

Then came a second chapter, the one the stylized engine knows nothing about. On January 17, 2025, about \$447mm of Trinseo's unsecured notes became second-lien notes, the intercompany loan grew by about \$494.5mm to roughly \$1.5bn, a \$300mm super-priority revolver replaced the old one, and a \$115mm Tranche C joined the Super HoldCo term loans. The SPV's intercompany term loans counted as votes toward the majority that approved the changes (by Debtwire's account of the excluded lenders' complaint). Theo stops on that sentence and reads it twice. "The SPV lends to OpCo, it belongs to OpCo's group, and it votes on OpCo's amendments." It is the denominator question of §13.2 in its purest form: whose loans count toward "Required Lenders," and whether a document written to cap affiliated votes saw this kind of affiliate coming.

Trinseo filed for Chapter 11 on May 26, 2026 (Bankr. S.D. Tex., lead case No. 26-90545, Judge Christopher Lopez). Its disclosure statement (dated May 25, 2026, filed as 8-K Ex. 99.2) is the first full-cycle test of the closed form, and the closed form turns out to be conservative. Funded debt was about \$2.9bn. Estimated recoveries: revolver (\$348mm) 99–100%; Super HoldCo first-lien term loans (\$1,266mm, including a \$115mm Tranche C from January 2025) **60–78%**; OpCo 2028 term loans (\$716mm) **2–9%**; 7.625% notes due 2029 (\$390mm) 0%. The OpCo intercompany term loans, \$1,508mm, were pledged to the Super HoldCo lenders. The restructuring support agreement of May 13, 2026 had 100% of the revolver, about 99.9% of the Super HoldCo first lien and about 86% of the OpCo term loans; 2025 adjusted EBITDA was \$162.5mm; the plan contemplates an \$850mm exit term loan and a \$270mm rights offering for 47.73% of the new equity.

The stylized engine says the double-dipper recovers twice what the first lien does. Trinseo's estimated multiple is roughly 7 to 39 (60/9 to 78/2). Theo's first instinct is that the disclosure statement is wrong. His second, better instinct is that his model is too small. He goes to the whiteboard and tries the closed form straight: $F_1 = 716$ for the OpCo 2028 term loans, $F_2 = 1{,}266$ for the Super HoldCo loans, a guarantee and a note of equal size, all pari passu. Whatever $V$ he writes in, the Super HoldCo lenders recover exactly twice what the OpCo lenders recover, so if Super HoldCo gets 60 to 78 cents, OpCo would get 30 to 39. The disclosure statement says 2 to 9. He is off by a factor of somewhere between three and twenty, depending on which ends of the ranges he pairs.

Mags, passing with a coffee, looks at the board for about four seconds. "Where's the revolver?"

Theo adds \$348mm across the top, paid in full, and every recovery below it falls. Kaz, who has drifted over, points out that the ratio has not moved. Pro rata sharing is indifferent to the size of the pot, and a multiple of two is still a multiple of two.

"What's the note?" says Mags.

"Nine forty-eight. On-lent in 2023."

"Read the disclosure statement again."

The OpCo intercompany term loans, the claim the Super HoldCo lenders hold by pledge, are \$1,508mm. Later tranches, accrued interest and the make-wholes live in the gap over the original \$948mm; about \$494.5mm of it arrived with the January 2025 transaction, by the excluded lenders' account, and the rest we leave undecomposed. Theo rewrites the pari pool at OpCo as 716 of term loans, 1,266 of guarantee and 1,508 of note, 3,490 in all. Now the Super HoldCo lenders collect $(1{,}266 + 1{,}508)/1{,}266 \approx 2.19$ times the OpCo rate from OpCo. Better. Still nowhere near seven.

"And who guarantees Super HoldCo," says Mags, "that doesn't guarantee you?"

That is the pari-plus leg, the value upstairs. Theo puts it in as a single number he cannot verify, an illustrative \$735mm that only the Super HoldCo lenders can reach, and leaves OpCo \$175mm after the revolver *(illustrative)*. The OpCo pool now pays $175/3{,}490 \approx 5.0$ cents. The Super HoldCo lenders collect $2{,}774 \times 0.050 \approx 139$ from OpCo and 735 from upstairs, about 874 on 1,266 of face, or 69 cents. Both numbers sit inside the disclosure statement's ranges, and the multiple is about 14. Finding the whole region of values that does this is Exercise 4; one point is enough to show what the ranges are made of. Ines adds up the board: \$348mm plus \$175mm plus \$735mm, about \$1.26bn. "Under eight times the \$162.5mm of 2025 EBITDA, for a chemicals company coming out with an \$850mm exit loan. Not a crazy place to stand."

What Theo takes from the whiteboard is that the multiple was never a stable statistic. The engine's job is to say where the excess comes from, and here it comes from three places: a revolver senior to everything; an intercompany claim of \$1,508mm against the \$948mm originally on-lent; and collateral at Super HoldCo that OpCo's lenders do not share. Once all three are in, the OpCo recovery is a small number left over after large ones have been subtracted, and the ratio of 69 to 5 is mostly a statement about how small 5 is. Cut OpCo's residual from 175 to 70 and OpCo falls from 5 cents to 2, while Super HoldCo slips only from 69 to 62; the multiple jumps from 14 to 31. A pair trade sized on "the multiple" would be sized on the noisiest number in the room.

The make-wholes are where the fight went, and the fight is about the size of the 1,508. An "OpCo Investigation" flagged the intercompany make-whole claims, and the proposed settlement took the form of "a gift through a carve-out of the Collateral securing the Allowed OpCo Intercompany Term Loan Claims."

"A gift," says Kaz. "From the senior lenders. Is there a card?"

"In bankruptcy," says Mags, "a gift is value a senior class hands to a junior class so the plan gets confirmed without the junior class fighting it. The card is the disclosure statement."

Some of the junior class fought anyway. CastleKnight, holding OpCo 2028 loans and 2029 notes, objects, and with Elevation CLO 2013-1 brought an adversary proceeding (No. 26-03208) seeking declaratory judgment on lien validity, priority and subordination. The excluded lenders' complaint attacks both the 2023 and the 2025 steps, asks the court to recharacterize the \$1.5bn intercompany loan as something other than debt, and challenges about \$56mm of consideration offered only to signers of the restructuring support agreement, under section 1123(a)(4), the equal-treatment rule that *ConvergeOne* (§13.9) put back in the news. CastleKnight had also bid to provide an alternative debtor-in-possession loan; the court approved a \$427.5mm DIP on June 17, 2026. On June 23, 2026 the plaintiffs opposed judgment-on-the-pleadings motions by the debtors and the "Super HoldCo Lender Defendants."

Ines prices the lawsuit on the same whiteboard, with the same illustrative stack, one count at a time. If the court strikes the gap and allows the note only at \$948mm, the pool shrinks to 2,930 and OpCo gets about 6.0 cents. If it recharacterizes the whole intercompany loan as equity, the pool is 1,982 and OpCo gets about 8.8. Only if the guarantee falls too, and the 2023 steps unwind, does OpCo stand alone in its pool, at about 24 cents, with more to come if the value upstairs comes back down. "So the recharacterization count is worth about four points," says Ines, "and the count that unwinds the deal is worth twenty. They have different odds, so they get different prices."

How a systematic desk flags it: the September 2023 8-K is machine-readable evidence of the three ingredients (a new SPV obligor, an intercompany loan pari with the existing first lien, an upstream guarantee). The parser emits `double_dip_present = 1`, the clearing engine reprices every OpCo-level claim with the new denominator, and the trade is the pair: long the dip, short (or underweight) the claims it dilutes. The pair is a bet on the recovery multiple, which the engine computes across $V$. Exercise 4 asks you to rebuild the stack and find the $V$ that reproduces the disclosure statement's ranges.

**Decision point.** It is Wednesday, June 24, 2026, the morning after the opposition brief. You have no verified secondary price for either instrument, only the plan's estimates: OpCo 2028 term loans at 2 to 9 cents, Super HoldCo first lien at 60 to 78. Buying the OpCo loans is buying the lawsuit: a few cents of plan recovery plus a claim on the unwind, worth roughly $q$ times the twenty-odd points the whiteboard found, less the legal bill and the chance that the plan is confirmed over the objection. Buying Super HoldCo is the same lawsuit with the opposite sign, plus the plan. Mags would buy the litigation, on her old principle that losing bidders sue, and that an excluded lender willing to fund the estate believes its own complaint. Ines would rather own the house, or the pair, because she cannot see the judge's cards and the Super HoldCo lenders hold most of the others. Which would you do, at what size, and what entry on the docket would change your mind? If you want to grade yourself against the record, it kept moving: on July 30, 2026 Judge Lopez denied judgment on the pleadings on Counts I to III; the confirmation hearing closed on August 19 with the matter under advisement and the judge asking who would fund an alternative; and we had found no ruling by mid-October 2026.

**When the clearing engine is the right tool, and when it is not.** The engine earns its keep the moment a structure has two or more obligors and some creditor can assert against more than one of them: a guarantee plus an intercompany note, as at Trinseo; a foreign-subsidiary guarantee on top of that, as at Sabre; an unrestricted subsidiary that lends back into the group that owns it. In those structures a priority chart misleads by omission, because it draws each claim once, and the arithmetic that matters is one dollar of credit being counted twice. The engine's best feature is that it fails loudly. Either the payments to outsiders plus the outside equity add up to the external value, or something is wrong, and on the morning a disclosure statement drops, that check is worth more than any recovery the engine prints. A single-obligor company with a clean lien stack gains nothing from it; a priority chart and a pencil give the same answer, and the extra decimal places are decoration.

What the engine cannot do is choose its own inputs. Theo's whiteboard reproduced the disclosure statement only once the intercompany claim was set at \$1,508mm, and that number is the subject of a lawsuit. Handed 1,508, the illustrative stack prints 5 cents for the OpCo lenders; handed 948, 6; handed nothing for the note, about 9; handed nothing for the note or the guarantee, 24. Each is a faithful computation in a different legal world. The engine settles what each answer does to a recovery, in milliseconds. It does not settle which answer the court will give, and a desk that lets one run of the engine stand in for that judgment is pricing a lawsuit at a single point. The seasoned habit is to run the engine across the plausible allowed claims, weight each by what the docket says about its odds, and treat the spread as the risk. The list below is where the inputs go wrong.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** *Five ways a clearing engine lies.* (1) **Circular ownership with no outside value.** If entities own each other in a loop that has no external value, Eisenberg–Noe can have many clearing vectors: iterating down from face finds the greatest, up from zero the least. Run both. If they differ, the answer is a legal question, not a numerical one. (2) **Deficiency conventions.** The engine assumes a creditor may assert full face against each obligor until paid in full, collecting no more than face in total. Some documents and jurisdictions net co-obligor recoveries first, which shrinks the dip. Make it a parameter, never a constant. (3) **Substantive consolidation.** Moyer observes that "a substantive consolidation makes the two claims pari passu" (Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.305). Consolidate OpCo and the SPV and the guarantee and the note become one claim; the dip disappears. (4) **The challenge list.** Moyer's checklist of attacks (p.351; list items joined) reads like an inventory of the engine's hidden parameters: "Invalidity of guarantees · Substantive consolidation · Critical creditor/vendor status · Interpretation of contractual subordination language · Violations of covenants · Transfers that might be deemed fraudulent conveyances." Each one deletes or resizes an edge in the claim graph. (5) **Claims at face plus premium.** Trinseo shows that the allowed amount of an intercompany claim is itself a litigated number. Price the dip over a range of allowed claims, not at one.

## 13.8 The Priming Risk Score

"You've told me how much I lose if I'm dealt out," says Ines. "Now tell me how often the dealer deals."

Theo's first answer is a banker's answer: it depends on the company, the sponsor, the advisors, and whether anyone has called sponsor counsel yet. Ines wants a trader's answer, a number per name per year that can be compared across the book on a Monday morning without anyone reading a credit agreement. Kaz has an answer that sounds like neither.

"Every loan has a clock on it," Kaz says. "It clicks when somebody does an LMT. We can't see the clock. We can see what the loan looked like each time it clicked."

Theo pulls up what there is to see. Roe and Rotaru hand-collected 89 coercive LMEs. Priya's list from §13.6 has about 1,500 loans in the desk's universe. The documents that produced most of the 89 were written before anyone had heard of a Serta blocker, and the documents the desk is pricing were written after.

"Eighty-nine," says Mags. "In my day we'd have called that a war story, not a dataset."

She is not wrong, and the section is built around her objection. It needs three ideas, each of them small: a hazard, to say exactly what "how often" means; a logistic link, to turn a row of features into a probability; and a prior, to stop 89 examples from saying something absurd.

**From hazard to a fixed-horizon score.** The template is the proportional-hazards model Bohn and Stein use for default (Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.233), applied to the LMT event:

$$
\lambda^{\text{LMT}}_i(t) = \lambda_0(t)\,e^{\beta^{\top} x_i(t)}, \qquad
\text{PRS}_i(t, h) = 1 - \exp\!\Big(-\int_t^{t+h} \lambda^{\text{LMT}}_i(u)\,du\Big) \in [0,1].
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *Hazard rates and the Poisson clock.* A hazard rate is the speed of a clock that clicks at random. Picture a Geiger counter on the desk, pointed at one loan, where each click is an LMT. Its hazard is the number of clicks per year you would expect if the counter held its speed. Clicks in a window follow the **Poisson** distribution: if you expect $H$ clicks over the year, the chance of exactly $k$ is $e^{-H}H^k/k!$, and in particular the chance of no click at all is $e^{-H}$. That is the survival probability, and one minus it is the PRS, the chance of at least one click. For a counter at steady speed $\lambda^{\text{LMT}}$, the wait until the first click is **exponential**, with mean $1/\lambda^{\text{LMT}}$, and it is memoryless, which is a polite way of saying that a loan nobody has primed for three years is not "due." Tiny number: a one-year PRS of 10% means $e^{-H} = 0.90$, so $H = -\ln 0.9 \approx 0.105$, a counter expected to click 0.105 times a year; held at that speed, the average wait would be about 9.5 years. The proportional-hazards model says the counter's speed is a baseline $\lambda_0(t)$ times $e^{\beta^{\top}x}$, so each feature turns a dial, and a coefficient of 0.69 doubles the speed, since $e^{0.69} \approx 2$. When the speed changes over time, "expected clicks" becomes the integral of the hazard in the formula above, which is all that integral means. Mags's clock from §13.1 is the same kind of counter, attached to a company after its LMT and clicking much faster. The full version, with intensities that are themselves random, is in §6.6 and §7-B.4.

($\lambda^{\text{LMT}}$ carries a superscript because $\lambda_t$ is reserved for default.) With roughly a hundred observed LMTs (Roe and Rotaru hand-collected 89 coercive ones), a time-varying baseline is a luxury. Fit at a fixed horizon $h = 1$ year with a logistic link on standardized features $z_i$: $\text{PRS}_i = \sigma(\beta_0 + \beta^{\top} z_i)$, $\sigma(u) = 1/(1+e^{-u})$. For small probabilities $1 - \exp(-e^{u}) \approx e^{u} \approx \sigma(u)$, so the logistic and the Cox model agree in the region where the desk lives.

> **Math, Gently.** *The logistic link.* A regression produces a score that can be any number, $-3$ or $+7$; a probability has to live between 0 and 1. The logistic function $\sigma(u) = 1/(1+e^{-u})$ is the squasher. Large negative scores come out near 0, large positive ones near 1, and a score of 0 comes out as exactly one half. Run it backwards and you get the log-odds, $\ln\big(p/(1-p)\big)$, so a logistic regression is a straight line drawn in log-odds. Tiny number: a score of $u = -2.2$ gives $\sigma = 1/(1 + e^{2.2}) \approx 1/10.0 = 10.0\%$. Feed the same score to the Poisson clock as a speed, $e^{-2.2} \approx 0.111$ expected clicks, and it says $1 - e^{-0.111} \approx 10.5\%$. At $u = -3$ the two say 4.7% and 4.9%. At $u = 0$ they say 50% and 63%, thirteen points apart, but a loan with a coin-flip chance of being primed this year has bigger problems than the choice of link. Below about 10%, where nearly every name on the desk sits, the logistic and the clock tell the same story. The full version, with calibration, is in §7-B.3.

**Priors, because 89 is not a sample size.** Mags's objection has a formal answer, and it is to say in advance what the desk believed before the data arrived. The desk puts $\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(\beta^{\text{prior}}, \tau^2 I)$ on the slopes and a Beta$(a,b)$ prior on the base rate $\pi_0 = \sigma(\beta_0)$, the base-rate framing of Bohn–Stein p.447. The maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate is the coefficient vector that best balances the logistic likelihood against both priors, and two consequences carry all the intuition. The intercept's first-order condition says that the mean fitted PRS equals the observed event rate plus a correction of order $\max(a,b)/n$, so the base-rate prior nudges the level and does not overrule it. And each fitted slope is, to a good approximation, a weighted average of the prior's slope and the maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE), with the weight set by how much information the data carry relative to $1/\tau^2$. The boundary conditions are the sanity checks: as $\tau \to \infty$ the prior vanishes and the fit returns the MLE; as $\tau \to 0$ the data vanish and the fit returns the prior. The code tests both limits. The objective, the first-order condition and the shrinkage matrix are written out in "The Full Derivation: the MAP estimate and its two limits" at the end of this section.

> **Math, Gently.** *The Beta prior, and shrinkage as a dial.* A **Beta** distribution is a histogram over a probability itself. Instead of saying "the base rate of LMTs is 10%," it says how sure you are of that. Beta$(a, b)$ behaves as if you had already seen $a$ events and $b$ non-events before the real data arrived, and its mean is $a/(a+b)$. Tiny number: $a = 2$, $b = 18$ says "10%, about as sure as twenty observations would make you." Now add real data, say 90 events in 1,000 borrower-years *(illustrative)*. The updated estimate is $(2 + 90)/(20 + 1{,}000) \approx 9.02\%$, against 9.0% from the data alone: twenty imaginary observations barely move a thousand real ones, which is the "correction of order $\max(a,b)/n$." Take instead 30 borrower-years with 9 events. The data say 30%; the prior pulls the estimate to $(2+9)/(20+30) = 22\%$. When data are thin, the prior pulls hard. That pull is shrinkage, and it is a dial, not a fog. The Gaussian prior on each slope works the same way, with $\tau$ as the knob: a large $\tau$ says "I barely believe my prior" and returns the data's answer, a small $\tau$ says "I barely believe my data" and returns the prior's, and every fitted slope lives somewhere in between. The listing's default, Beta$(2, 38)$, is a 5% base rate held with the confidence of forty observations. Beta distributions return as models of recovery in §5.5; the full version of this one is in "The Full Derivation" below.

>
> **Annotation.** Bohn and Stein pose this for portfolios with few or no defaults. LMTs are the same problem one level up: the event is rare, its definition changed in 2020, and the population that generated the history (pre-blocker documents) is not the population you are pricing. The Beta prior turns "we have seen few events like this" into an honest upper bound rather than a zero. Where the desk departs from them: the prior must be *vintage-aware*. A base rate estimated on 2020–2022 documents overstates the risk of a 2026 agreement with a Serta blocker (Trend Watch, §13.3), so the prior's mean should come from events in documents of the same vintage, and its width should admit that there are few of them.

**What the desk prices.** The score is one factor of the expected priming loss, in fraction of par:

$$
\text{EPL}_i = \underbrace{\Pr(\text{LMT})}_{\text{PRS}_i} \cdot \Pr(\text{non-pro-rata} \mid \text{LMT}) \cdot \Pr(\text{excluded} \mid \text{NPR}) \cdot \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[\Delta R^{\text{excl}}\big],
$$

and the quote is approximately $P \approx P_{\text{no-LMT}} - 100\cdot\text{EPL}_i - \text{risk premium}$, in points. The last factor comes from §13.3 (or §13.6 for a drop-down), the third from the knapsack of §13.4, the second from the co-op state of §13.5. **Measure discipline:** PRS is a $\mathbb{P}$-forecast of an event. The price impact is $\mathbb{Q}$-flavoured, because the marginal holder is averse to exactly this jump, and the *Serta* damages award shows a court measuring it with a market price. Calibrating PRS to prices would bake the market's fear into the forecast and then subtract it a second time in the quote, so the desk keeps the two apart: it regresses price residuals on EPL and calls the slope the premium.

**When to trust the PRS, and when not to.** On the Monday after the model goes live, Ines prints the top forty names by score and reads down the list the way she reads a hand history, looking for the one that does not belong. A packaging company the desk has never traded has jumped from 0.12 to 0.19 overnight *(illustrative)*. She asks why. Kaz pulls the attribution, and almost all of the move is `loan_px`, which fell three points on Friday afternoon. Nothing in the document changed. Nobody new signed a co-op. The runway did not shorten. "So the price fell," says Ines, "and the score told us the price fell. That's a mirror, not a forecast." It is her first check on any move, and the reason is circular in the plainest way. The score exists to explain the price, so a score that moves only because the price moved has added nothing, and an EPL built on it would take the same three points off the quote twice. A score that moves because the parser found a new incremental-debt basket, or because a new Rule 2019 statement landed, is telling the desk something the price may not know yet.

That check opens onto the larger judgment about what the PRS is for. It is good at ranking. Within one vintage of documents and one forum, a name scored 0.19 is more likely to see an LMT next year than a name scored 0.05, and the ordering survives most of the ways the model can be misspecified. It is much worse at levels, and three things break the level. The first is vintage. Buccola and Nini found that before 2020 only 40% of loans restricted uptiers, and that by mid-2022, 85% of new loans explicitly blocked them (Trend Watch, §13.3). A base rate fitted on the earlier documents describes a population that no longer exists, which is why the prior's mean comes from events in documents of the same vintage. The second is forum. Courts in Texas and New York read buyback exceptions for the borrower, against it, and for it again within about two years (§13.9), and a score with a static forum coefficient averages across a sign flip. The third is the holder block, which the score sees only dimly. `holder_hhi` and `coop_reported` arrive late and dirty (Priya's Tuesday in §13.6), and Robertshaw showed that a Required-Lender position can be bought quietly in a single month (§13.4). A desk that uses the PRS to decide which forty documents to read first is using it well. A desk that multiplies a PRS of 0.19 by three other factors and quotes the product to the second decimal has mistaken a ranking for a measurement, and the EPL inherits the false precision.

> **Ways This Blows Up.** *Separation.* On the training data, `loan_px < 80` and `runway_months < 9` together nearly separate events from non-events. An unpenalized logistic responds by sending those coefficients toward infinity and reporting a PRS of 1.000 for every distressed name, which is confident and useless. The Gaussian prior keeps $\hat\beta$ finite. The prior's most important covariate is Theo's 13-week model, reborn as `runway_months`: the spreadsheet he carries for emotional support is now the only thing standing between the desk and an infinite coefficient.

#### The Full Derivation: the MAP estimate and its two limits

**The objective.** In $\beta_0$-space, including the Jacobian, the Beta$(a,b)$ prior on $\pi_0 = \sigma(\beta_0)$ has log density $a \ln \sigma(\beta_0) + b \ln(1 - \sigma(\beta_0))$. With the Gaussian prior on the slopes, the MAP estimate is

$$
\hat\beta = \arg\max_{\beta}\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[y_i \eta_i - \ln(1 + e^{\eta_i})\big] - \frac{\lVert \beta_{1:k} - \beta^{\text{prior}} \rVert^2}{2\tau^2} + a \ln \sigma(\beta_0) + b \ln\!\big(1 - \sigma(\beta_0)\big), \qquad \eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_{1:k}^{\top} z_i .
$$

**The intercept.** The first-order condition in $\beta_0$ says exactly what the base-rate prior does: $\sum_i (y_i - \hat p_i) + a(1 - \sigma(\hat\beta_0)) - b\,\sigma(\hat\beta_0) = 0$, so the mean fitted PRS equals the observed event rate plus a correction of order $\max(a,b)/n$.

**The slopes.** A quadratic expansion of the log-likelihood around the MLE (information matrix $H$) gives the shrinkage form

$$
\hat\beta_{1:k} \approx \Lambda\,\beta^{\text{prior}} + (I - \Lambda)\,\hat\beta^{\text{MLE}}, \qquad \Lambda = (H + \tau^{-2} I)^{-1}\tau^{-2},
$$

with boundary conditions $\tau \to \infty \Rightarrow \Lambda \to 0$ (the MLE) and $\tau \to 0 \Rightarrow \Lambda \to I$ (the prior). ($\Lambda$ here is the shrinkage matrix of this subsection only; the order-arrival intensity $\Lambda(\delta)$ of Part V is a different object.) The code tests both limits.

### Specification for AI Implementation

*What ROBO built from the vague version.* Theo typed: "Flag every borrower that could be primed." ROBO returned the entire index, correctly: any agreement can be amended with 100% consent. The precise version names $\theta$ for the specific action, the `buyback_exception` enum, and a horizon. ROBO returned 11 names. One was the desk's own warehouse line.

**Schemas.** `entities`: `id` (str), `value` (float, \$mm, $\ge 0$), `parent` (entity id or `None`). `claims`: `id`, `creditor` (`"ext"` or entity id), `debtor` (entity id), `face` (\$mm, $>0$), `rank` (int, 1 = most senior), `reduce_by` (list of claim ids on the same debt owed by co-obligors). `features`: one row per borrower-year, columns as in the §13.2.1 table; `buyback_exception` one-hot; `y` = 1 if an LMT closed within 12 months. **Edge cases:** no claims at an entity (all value is residual); $N = 0$ or $p = 1$ in the uptier (guard the divisions); co-op share above $1 - \theta$ (return `None`, never an empty coalition); burn $\le 0$ in the runway (cap at 120 months); quasi-separation (prior required, never `tau=inf` in production). **Stability:** overflow-safe logistic via `tanh`; `logaddexp` in the likelihood; Newton steps with step-halving; clearing iteration capped at 10,000 with a convergence error, not a silent return.

The closed form in §13.7 is a pleasure to derive and a trap to deploy. It knows about one SPV, one guarantee and one first lien that holds still. Trinseo had a revolver ahead of everything, Super HoldCo lenders with a pledge over a \$1,508mm intercompany claim, OpCo lenders sitting on 2 to 9 cents, and a lawsuit about whether the make-wholes belong in the denominator at all. Nobody solves that by hand on the morning a disclosure statement drops, and Theo's 13-week model, which has never met a subsidiary that owns a lender, will not help. What a desk wants is one function that takes whatever the parser pulled out of the documents (who owes whom, how much, at what rank, who owns which entity) and returns recoveries that add up to the value in the structure, every time. "Adds up" is the part ROBO can be tested on, so it is the part the listing is built around.

`waterfall_clearing` takes two inputs. `ents` maps each entity to its external `value` (\$mm) and its `parent`, with `None` marking the top of the tree. `claims` lists the `creditor` (`"ext"` or another entity), `debtor`, `face`, `rank` (1 is most senior) and an optional `reduce_by` list of claim ids, meaning that whatever a co-obligor has already paid on the same debt comes off what this claim may still assert. It returns payments by claim id and the equity left at the top.

Each pass builds what every entity has to distribute: its own value, plus what its intercompany claims were paid last pass, plus what its subsidiaries had left over last pass. Then, for each debtor, it walks the ranks from senior to junior, pays every claim in a rank the same fraction of what is still owed (face less the `reduce_by` payments, floored at zero), and carries the remainder down. What survives every rank is the entity's residual, which flows to its parent next pass. The loop stops when nothing moves by more than 1e-10, and raises `RuntimeError` after 10,000 passes rather than return a number that merely looks settled. Iteration is needed because the structure is circular: the SPV's payment depends on OpCo's pot, which depends on what the SPV hands back.

`dd_recoveries` builds the stack of §13.7 (OpCo, the SPV, an optional foreign subsidiary) and returns first-lien and new-money recoveries per dollar of face. Every call asserts conservation of value and that no claim is paid above face. The checks below it reproduce the closed forms: $2V/(F_1 + 2F_2)$ for the double-dipper and half that for the first lien below the kink; 0.70 and 1.00 at \$1,200mm; monotone recoveries in $V$; and pari-plus collapsing to the plain dip when the foreign subsidiary is worth nothing.

Use it when claims cross entities and you need recoveries by claim with a conservation check attached. Do not use it to decide whether a guarantee survives challenge or whether the court consolidates the entities: it will faithfully compute the wrong answer from a wrong claim list. The edge case that bites is circularity. The loop starts from zero payments and only walks up, so it finds only the clearing vector reachable from below. For loops with no outside value, the chapter advises iterating from face as well and comparing. This listing does not, so write that twin before trusting a circular structure.

`uptier(V, F, N, x, p)` takes an array of enterprise values and the deal terms (face $F$, new money $N$, exchange ratio $x$, participation $p$) and returns four arrays of per-dollar recoveries: the old pari passu class, first-out, second-out and excluded. `priming_loss` is the old recovery minus the excluded one. It is deliberately not floored at zero, because between $K_2$ and $F$ the excluded lender gains from the participants' face haircut. Calibrated to Serta ($F = 2{,}400$, $N = 200$, $p = 0.5$, $x = 875/1{,}200$, so $K_1 = 1{,}075$ and $K_2 = 2{,}275$), the checks are: face times recovery sums to $\min(V, K_2)$; the loss equals the old recovery wherever $V \le K_1$ and vanishes above $F + N$; and a degenerate deal ($x = 1$, $N = 0$, $p = 1$) transfers nothing.

The expected transfer comes from an illustrative lognormal for $V$ (median \$1,500mm, volatility 0.45). Quadrature with breakpoints at $K_1$, $K_2$ and $F$ gives about 0.22, or 22 cents per dollar of par. A 400,000-draw Monte Carlo must land within four standard errors of it, and the closed-form partial expectation of §13.3 must match its own Monte Carlo.

`min_cost_coalition(w, c, theta, eps, coop)` solves the sponsor's knapsack. It removes co-op members from the candidates, returns `None` if the rest cannot reach $\theta + \varepsilon$ (a veto, not an empty coalition), and otherwise picks the subset that clears the bar at the lowest $\sum c_i w_i$. Up to twelve candidates it enumerates every subset (at most 4,095) and is exact. Beyond that it takes the cheapest $c_i$ first, ignoring holding size, which is a heuristic only. In the demo, funds are cheap, CLOs dear and the litigator dearest: at $\theta = 0.5$ the chosen coalition (five holders, 54% of the class) leaves the litigator out, and a 36% co-op against $\theta = 2/3$ returns `None`. `shapley(w, quota)` enumerates every ordering and credits whoever tips the running total past the quota, reproducing the (5/12, 5/12, 1/12, 1/12) of §13.5. Because it visits $n!$ orderings, it suits a steering committee of eight, not a class of eighty.

The edge case that bites is $p = 1$. Nobody is excluded, so `uptier` returns `nan` for the excluded recovery by design, and `priming_loss`, and any average taken over it, comes back `nan` with no exception. Guard it.

A logistic regression with a dozen features and 89 positives will fit anything you hand it and, when the distressed names separate cleanly from the rest, report a PRS of 1.000 with a straight face. The desk's answer is not to give up but to say, in code, what it believed before the data arrived: a prior on each slope from the published studies, and a Beta prior on the base rate so the intercept cannot wander to wherever the sample happened to put it. Then it proves the shrinkage is a dial rather than a fog by turning the dial to each end. `fit_map(Z, y, prior, tau, a, b)` returns the intercept and slopes on standardized features. It maximizes the posterior of §13.8: the logistic likelihood, a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\text{prior}, \tau^2)$ on each slope, and a Beta$(a, b)$ on $\sigma(\beta_0)$, with defaults $a = 2$, $b = 38$, a 5% base rate worth forty pseudo-observations. Newton steps use an analytic gradient and Hessian, and each step is halved until it stops raising the negative log posterior. Setting `tau=np.inf` with `a=b=0` gives the plain MLE, which is how the listing proves both limits of the shrinkage formula: a huge `tau` reproduces the MLE, a tiny one returns the prior. The other checks: the intercept first-order condition holds to 1e-6; the mean fitted PRS stays within 1.5 points of the observed rate; a 200-resample bootstrap band covers the estimate; a blocker lowers the score and unsub capacity raises it; and on a fresh holdout the fit beats the constant base rate on Brier score.

## 13.9 Autopsies: The Court Is a Random Variable

The $q$ in the litigation option of §13.4 has been the most volatile parameter on the desk since 2023. Two features carry it: the clause and the doctrine.

Mags tells the Incora story the way a chess columnist annotates a famous game: every move legal, and the position lost long before anyone resigned. Theo has the district court's opinion open. Kaz has the whiteboard.

The board, as of early 2022. Wesco Aircraft, owned by Platinum Equity, had raised over \$2bn in 2020 to finance Platinum's acquisition of Wesco and its combination with Pattonair: \$650mm of 8.5% secured notes due 2024, \$900mm of 9% secured notes due 2026, and \$525mm of 13.125% unsecured notes due 2027. Two clauses in the secured indentures matter. A majority could consent to issuing more notes, pari passu. Releasing substantially all the collateral, or anything with "the effect of" doing so, needed two-thirds. By late 2021 the company needed money badly enough that its UK auditors wanted \$150–200mm injected or the UK entity faced being struck off. In December 2021 PIMCO and Silver Point, the two largest holders of the 2024 and 2026 notes, proposed a rescue. In February 2022 a minority group, which the district court later identified as led by BlackRock and JPMorgan, announced that it held more than a third of the 2026 notes. That was a blocking position against any two-thirds vote, and it set off the race for the marginal bond described in §13.4, in which the 2026s reportedly ran from about 85 to above par. The minority offered about \$147.5mm of financing, then \$173mm. The board chose the majority's \$250mm.

"So the minority has a third," says Theo. "The majority can't release the liens."

"Can't release them *today*," says Mags. "Watch."

Then came March 28, 2022, four steps on one day.

*Step one.* Holders supporting the deal owned more than two-thirds of the 2024 notes, more than 59% of the 2026 notes and more than half of the 2027s. A majority is enough to add pari passu notes, so they signed the Third Supplemental Indentures, which permitted \$250mm of additional 2026 notes. Nobody's lien moved. Kaz writes $v_1 = 0.59 \ge \theta_1 = 0.5$ and a check mark.

*Step two.* The \$250mm was wired, and the new 2026 notes were issued to the majority group. Kaz does the arithmetic out loud. The class used to be \$900mm, of which the group held at least 59%, about \$531mm. Now it is \$1,150mm, of which the group holds about \$781mm, or 67.9%: two-thirds, with a point to spare. The minority's \$300mm-plus, a third of the old class, is about 26% of the new one. Its block has evaporated without a single minority bond changing hands.

"The least they could have walked in with," says Kaz, "is about 57.4% of the old class. They had 59. The whole lawsuit is in that point and a half."

*Step three.* With two-thirds in hand, the group signed the Fourth Supplemental Indentures, which released the liens securing the 2024 and 2026 notes.

Kaz, reading over Theo's shoulder: "The fourth supplemental indenture. Is that the one with the new teeth?"

"It's the one that pulled them," says Mags.

*Step four.* The participants exchanged their 2024 and 2026 notes into new super-senior first-lien notes (about \$1.27bn, by a secondary account), and participating 2027 holders exchanged into 1.25-lien notes. The non-participants woke up holding unsecured paper behind more than \$1.7bn of new debt (also a secondary figure). Milbank had given an opinion that the steps complied with the indentures.

Kaz steps back from the board. Every step has a check mark: 0.59 against a half for the first, a wire for the second, 0.679 against two-thirds for the third, a voluntary exchange for the fourth. "Each move is legal."

"Each move," says Mags. "Now look at the position before the first move and ask whether they could have done the whole thing in one go."

They could not. Measured on the morning of March 28, the group held about 59% of the 2026s against a two-thirds bar, and that gap is the whole of the litigation. The excluded holders sued in state court in October 2022. Wesco filed for Chapter 11 on June 1, 2023, with an adversary proceeding the same day, and a 35-day trial ran from January to July 2024. At closings in June 2024, Judge Isgur said he believed "with a high degree of certainty" that the \$250mm was unauthorized, two weeks before he ruled. The plan on file before the ruling had given the participants 96.5% of the equity; at emergence on January 31, 2025, the secured noteholders took 98.4% plus \$420mm of take-back notes. Then the district court took the steps one at a time, as Kaz had, and found each one compliant.

"So who was right?" says Theo. "The bankruptcy judge or the district judge?"

"For the desk that's the wrong question," says Ines. "The right one is what the clauses made the odds. The two-thirds bar was in the document. The pari passu basket was in the document. Anyone who read both in 2021 could see that a 57.4% holder could manufacture a two-thirds holder in a day, and that some court would one day have to choose between reading steps and reading plans. That choice is $q$. Both groups were pricing it in February, when they paid up toward par for the marginal 2026 bond."

**Steps versus plans.** Write an LMT as a composition of document moves $\mathcal{D}_0 \to \mathcal{D}_1 \to \cdots \to \mathcal{D}_m$, where step $k$ needs holders with share $v_k(\mathcal{D}_{k-1})$ of the class to clear $\theta_k$. A step-by-step court asks whether $v_k(\mathcal{D}_{k-1}) \ge \theta_k$ for every $k$. An integrated court treats the composite as one move and measures support before anything happened, $v(\mathcal{D}_0) \ge \max_k \theta_k$, plus unanimity for any sacred right the composite touches. The gap $g = \max_k \theta_k - v(\mathcal{D}_0)$ is the feature `integration_gap`. When $g > 0$ the deal works only in a step-by-step forum, and $q$ becomes a bet on doctrine. Wesco/Incora is the textbook case: the group held more than two-thirds of the 2024 notes but not of the 2026 notes (§13.4; on the scene's numbers, $v(\mathcal{D}_0) \approx 0.59$ of the 2026s against $\theta = \tfrac23$, so $g \approx 0.08$), used a majority to issue itself enough secured notes to reach two-thirds, then used the two-thirds to release liens. Each step clears its own bar. The composite does not. *STG Logistics* (§13.6) is the same test applied to a drop-down.

On December 23, 2024 Saks bought Neiman Marcus at a \$2.7bn enterprise value, funded in part by \$2.2bn of 11% senior secured notes due 2029, with Jefferies as initial purchaser. By May 16, 2025 the notes were at 47.5, a holder group of about half the class was weighing new money that could leapfrog the rest, and a \$121mm coupon was due at the end of June. The exchange launched on July 21, 2025 and priced the coalition in plain sight. Per \$1,000 of old notes, holders who had pre-funded the new money received \$106 of new SPV notes and \$894 of second-out notes; holders who funded new money in the offer received \$550 of second-out and \$250 of third-out; holders who did neither received \$750 of third-out. About 98% tendered by August 8, 2025. Saks filed for Chapter 11 on January 13, 2026 with about \$3.4bn of funded debt, and the plan confirmed on June 5, 2026 cut that by roughly two-thirds to three-quarters (one account puts the remainder near \$1.2bn) and left junior creditors owed about \$1.5bn with a litigation trust of \$20mm as reported before confirmation. From new issue to petition took about thirteen months; from exchange to petition, five. The exchange did not end the story. It reordered the queue for the next chapter, which is what Mags meant in §13.1 by a state with a clock on the wall. The eight cases the desk keeps on its scoreboard put numbers on the clock.

**Forum is time-varying.** In 2023 a Texas bankruptcy court found *Serta*'s "open market purchase" "clear and unambiguous" in the borrower's favor. On Dec 31, 2024 the Fifth Circuit read the same words against the borrower, and New York's First Department upheld *Mitel*'s uptier under a bare "purchase" exception; Mitel nonetheless filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 on Mar 10, 2025, had its plan confirmed by Judge Lopez on Apr 17, 2025, and by June 20, 2025 had emerged owned by its former lenders with about \$1.15bn less debt (Paul, Weiss, *Mitel Emerges from Chapter 11 with Less Debt*, June 20, 2025; Davis Polk's account of the participating lenders' appellate win). A legal win and a credit win turned out to be nearly independent. A PRS fitted with a static forum coefficient averages a sign flip, so interact `forum_ny_vs_tx` with `buyback_exception` and a post-2024 dummy. In the bankruptcy court itself the *Serta* rule keeps spreading: in *ConvergeOne* (S.D. Tex. district court, 2025, on appeal), backstop fees on a \$245mm exit rights offering were "a form of recovery on participating creditors' pre-bankruptcy debt claims, and therefore had to be open to all creditors in the applicable class, absent robust market testing" (Wachtell). The next observations are already docketed: *Invesco v. Robertshaw* (5th Cir. No. 26-20422) and the bond-side exit-consent fight over Lions Gate/Starz's 2024 exchange of \$389,861,000 of 5.500% notes due 2029 (*CPPIB Credit Investments II v. Deutsche Bank Trust Co. Americas*, Sup. Ct. N.Y. Cnty., Mar 17, 2026). IDO or it didn't happen: LMT P&L is the one place where "idiosyncratic" was defined by two courts, in two states, on the same day.

## 13.10 Desk Debrief

*Tuesday, 4:50 p.m. The same Tuesday.*

At 7:51 that morning Ines made the call Theo could not yet make. "Tell them 75, for five." The client did not hit it. At 7:58 sales reported, in the voice sales reserves for a lost trade, that the client had done all fifteen million away at 78. Loans do not print on TRACE, so the desk learned the level the way it learns most things about loans: from a salesperson who heard it from a client who wanted them to know.

Theo spent the day doing what this chapter just did. At 4:50 he stands at Ines's desk with one sheet of paper, which is a regression from the legal pad, which was a regression from the napkin.

"Four factors," he says. "The PRS first. With this morning's headline coded as a group organizing, the model says 0.35 for an LMT inside a year. I checked what moved it, the way you do. The price falling from 84 to 78 accounts for about four hundredths of that. The headline and the document account for the rest, so it isn't a mirror."

"What's in the document?"

"An agreement signed in early 2022, before the blockers became the norm later that year. No Serta blocker. A J.Crew blocker, no Chewy blocker. And 9.05(g) says 'open market purchase,' not bare 'purchase.' After the Fifth Circuit, that wording makes a straight Serta-style exchange harder to defend, but it does nothing against an amend-extend-and-exchange. So the second factor, the chance the deal is non-pro-rata given that there is a deal, is a half. Before December 2024 I'd have said seven in ten."

"Third."

"The knapsack. The group says it holds 51.3%, and the action it needs is a bare majority, so the bag is already full. Anyone who arrives today with fifteen million is outside it by construction, and fifteen million is too small to be worth a seat even if the bag weren't full: a one-percent holder adds almost nothing toward $\theta$ and costs a chair at the table. The only way in is a sponsor who opens the deal to everyone to keep the litigators quiet, which happens more often since *Serta* than it used to. Pr(excluded) is 0.85."

"Fourth."

"The Serta-shaped put. Twenty-two cents of expected transfer, from the illustrative lognormal in the §13.8 listing, because I don't have Larkspur's terms yet. It's the factor I trust least." He writes the product, $0.35 \times 0.5 \times 0.85 \times 0.22 \approx 0.033$, about 3.3 points.

"That's for a random holder," says Ines. "Who sold at 7:41?"

"That's the part I couldn't do this morning." Theo turns the sheet over. "If the seller was in seat two, they knew they were out and the deal was close. Then the PRS is more like 0.8, the non-pro-rata chance 0.8, and they're excluded for certain: $0.8 \times 0.8 \times 1 \times 0.22$, about 14 points. If it was seat three, the CLO, it's the random-holder number, 3.3. Seat one can't sell. Given that somebody offered at 78 within a minute of the headline, I'd put four in ten on seat two. That's about 7.6 points of expected priming loss. Without any LMT risk, Larkspur would trade around 86 *(illustrative, from comparable names)*, and our residual regression says the market charges about a third on top of EPL for bearing the jump, call it two and a half points. Eighty-six, less seven and a half, less two and a half: 76. And five million, not fifteen. If they hit 76 in four seconds, I've learned which seat they had, and I paid for the lesson on a third of the size."

"In my day," says Mags, "we'd have bid seventy, hung up, and called it judgment."

"It was judgment," says Ines. "It just didn't have units."

Kaz leans over from the next desk. "And my question from this morning? Why 'excluded' means 25 in one case and 99 in another?"

"Because 'excluded' was never the variable," says Theo. "The variable is where $V$ lands against the strike when the story ends, and who gets to say the story has ended: a court, a sale, or the next filing. Serta ended in a courtroom with $V$ below $K_1$. Lumen ended with an asset sale big enough to put $V$ above everybody's strike, and so did Boardriders. Same word, different $V$, different referee."

Ines pulls up the hand history for a trade she did not do. Larkspur is quoted 74–76 at the close *(illustrative)*. The other dealer's 78 marks out at about −3, roughly \$450,000 on fifteen million. Her 75 for five, had it traded, would be flat. Theo's 76 for five would be down about \$50,000, and the desk would know something it does not know now. "The markout says the seller had a reason," she says. "Nobody followed with another offer at 78, and the bid leaked all day. Seat two, or a very nervous seat three. Write it down."

She does not say whether 76 was right, because the hand is not over. Three days later the lenders who had not been called would find each other and organize, the loan would trade at 81 (§13.5), and the same 78 would mark out at +3. Ines keeps markouts at the close, at a day and at a week for exactly this reason, and she keeps all three.

Theo writes the card that goes on his monitor.

- **An LMT is the difference of two waterfalls.** In low states it is zero-sum among creditors; the liquidity story lives in high states. Compute $\Delta_j(V)$ before you read the press release.
- **Know your payoff shape.** Excluded from an uptier: short a soft digital put struck at $K_1$. Victim of a drop-down: short a forward capped at $C^{\text{unsub}}/F$. Diluted by a double-dip: someone is counted twice in your denominator.
- **The sponsor solves a knapsack** and leaves out the constrained, the fragmented and the litigious. Model your invitation probability, not your participation decision.
- **A co-op above $1 - \theta$ turns an auction into a bargain.** Deal-away capacity costs the co-op $\eta$ per dollar; carve-out premiums are Shapley values. (Cooperation agreements signed in this book so far: 3. Antitrust complaints about them: 2, and counting.)
- **One word can be the whole loss.** The court is a random variable with a clause, a forum, a doctrine and a vintage.
- **PRS is $\mathbb{P}$; the price is $\mathbb{Q}$.** Never calibrate the score to prices. The prior keeps $\hat\beta$ finite, and Theo's runway is the covariate that separates.
- **The LMT is not the terminal state.** Only 22% of coercive-LME firms avoided both re-default and bankruptcy within two years. Price the next event.

### The Spec You Hand the Robot

Build `lmt_engine.py` (numpy/scipy; `rng = np.random.default_rng(13)`; runs top to bottom; values in \$mm, recoveries in fraction of par, runway in months, PRS at a 12-month horizon). (1) `waterfall_clearing(entities, claims)`: priority ranks, pro rata within rank, intercompany receipts and subsidiary equity routed to parents, `reduce_by` for co-obligor claims on one debt; stop at $\lVert \Delta p \rVert_\infty < 10^{-10}$, raise after 10,000 iterations; assert conservation to $10^{-8}$, no payment above face, monotonicity in $V$, the double-dip closed form, pari-plus collapse at $V_f = 0$. (2) `uptier`, `priming_loss`: guard $N = 0$ and $p = 1$; assert conservation, zero loss for $V \ge F + N$, full loss for $V \le K_1$; expected loss by quadrature checked against Monte Carlo and the lognormal partial expectation. (3) `min_cost_coalition`: brute force to 12 holders, greedy beyond, `None` when a co-op exceeds $1 - \theta$; `shapley` by enumeration. (4) `fit_map`: Newton with step-halving, Gaussian slope prior, Beta base-rate prior; assert the intercept first-order condition, both $\tau$ limits, the bootstrap band, signs, and a holdout Brier score below the base rate. (5) `expected_priming_loss` $=$ PRS $\times$ P(NPR $\mid$ LMT) $\times$ P(excluded $\mid$ NPR) $\times$ $\mathbb{E}[\Delta R]$, inputs in $[0,1]$. Never fit PRS to prices.
