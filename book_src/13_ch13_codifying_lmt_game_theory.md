# Chapter 13: Codifying Liability Management Transactions (LMTs)

## Cold Open: Three Seats at 78

*Tuesday, 7:41 a.m. Three days after the lender call. The desk smells like cold coffee and somebody else's leverage. (Larkspur Foam Holdings, its lenders, its sponsor and this desk are fictional; every clause in Larkspur's credit agreement is borrowed from a real one.)*

The headline lands at 7:40: **LARKSPUR FOAM LENDERS SAID TO TAP COUNSEL AS SPONSOR EXPLORES "LIABILITY MANAGEMENT."** At 7:41 a client asks for a bid on \$15mm of the Larkspur 2029 first-lien term loan. It was marked 84 last night. The client "would look at 78."

Theo Marsh already has the credit agreement open. Old habits. "Section 2.18 is pro rata sharing. Section 9.02(b) makes it a sacred right. But 9.05(g) carves out purchases by the borrower, and the question is whether it says *open market* purchase or just *purchase*—"

"I assumed a Serta blocker was a mattress protector," says Kaz Lind, reading over his shoulder.

"It is," says Mags Dunleavy, not looking up. "It protects the mattress company's lenders from the mattress company."

Kaz considers this. "So the lenders are the mattress."

"The excluded ones are what gets slept on."

Ines Okafor turns her chair a quarter turn, which is how she signals that a hand has started. "Theo. What's the pot, what are the odds, and who at this table knows more than you?"

"The pot is fifteen million face, call it a twelve-million-dollar check at 78. The odds depend on whether the sponsor can get a bare majority to sign up to a non-pro-rata deal. And the person who knows more than me is—" He stops. "Either this seller was invited into the group and is selling before they get wall-crossed, which is unlikely, or they *weren't* invited, and they know exactly what that means."

"Or," says Ines, "they're a CLO that just read the same headline you did and has a CCC bucket to manage. Three sellers. Three different hands. Same price."

From somewhere offstage comes the sound of a Rule 2019 statement being drafted. The Ad Hoc Group, represented by counsel, speaks in unison: *"We neither confirm nor deny that we exist. We hold 51.3% of the first lien."*

Ines does not acknowledge the chorus. She never does; it bills by the hour. "Say them out loud, Theo. Each seat, and the price at which that seat sells to you."

Theo pulls a legal pad across the desk, which on a systematic desk counts as a regression to an earlier form of life. "Seat one is the insider. Invited, wall-crossed, has seen the term sheet, and is about to swap this loan into super-priority paper that trades well above 78, with a fee for the signature. Seat one cannot sell to me at all, because seat one is restricted."

"Price?"

"No price. The seat is not for sale. If it shows up for sale anyway, somebody inside thinks the deal is bad even for the insiders, and I want to know why."

"Seat two."

"The excluded holder who knows. They rang the sponsor's banker and got a voicemail with very warm vowels. Or they heard the group is closed at 51.3% and nobody needs a fifty-second percent. They know that if this closes they sit behind new money and behind the exchanged paper. They would sell at 78. They would sell at 68. The only thing they care about is getting it done before every other seat-two holder calls me."

"Price?"

"Whatever I will pay. Which is the problem. If I buy from seat two, I am not buying a loan. I am buying their seat."

"Seat three."

Theo hesitates, then remembers a working paper he read on the train because the alternative was his phone. "The CLO. A CLO typically has a 7.5% limit on loans rated CCC or below, and the loans over the limit get marked at the lowest market value in the CCC bucket, not at their own price. If the agencies call this a distressed exchange, Larkspur goes to CCC or lower and lands in a bucket that might already be close to full. That manager sells because of a test, not because of a view. They might privately think the loan is worth 90."

"Price?"

"78 is their number because 78 is what they think clears before the month-end test date. Their price has nothing to do with Larkspur. It has to do with the arithmetic of their other two hundred loans."

Ines nods once. "One seat cannot sell, one will sell at anything, and one sells for reasons that are not about the credit. You cannot see which one is on the wire. Here is the part they never told you in restructuring: this loan is not a claim on a foam company. It is a claim on a seat. The seat gets decided by three people you have never met, reading one clause you have not finished reading."

Mags finally looks up. "Back in my day we did this with exit consents on bonds and called it an exchange offer. The innovation was doing it to the bank group. The bank group used to be the one holding the knife."

She says the next word the way you might name a horse that once threw you. "Serta. Mattresses, a sponsor, a 2016 first lien and second lien. Spring of 2020 the loans are trading far below par, two lender groups are pitching the company, and the company picks one. June 22, 2020, the deal closes. The winners put in two hundred million of new money and swap their old loans into new super-priority paper. The first-lien holders who got in swapped at 74 cents on the dollar into paper that now sat near the top of the stack. The ones who didn't get in? Years later a court went back and valued their loans on closing day. Twenty-five."

"Twenty-five," says Theo.

"Twenty-five. Same loan, same lien, same Monday. The only difference was which room you were in on Friday."

Theo does the arithmetic on a napkin, because the legal pad now feels like a commitment. If Larkspur is a Serta and he buys at 78 from seat two, the deal closes and the honest mark is somewhere near 25: a markout of about fifty points, more if he lies to himself about the bid side. He writes "−50" and circles it.

"Or it's a Lumen," says Ines. "Different animal. A telecom, autumn 2023, a cooperation agreement so large it became the deal. Holders outside the agreement got primed and some of their bonds traded in the forties. Then the company sold a business, and about two and a half years later it was tendering for paper that had been left outside at close to par." Theo writes "+20?" next to the −50 and does not circle it.

Kaz has been staring at the napkin. "I have a question that is probably stupid."

"Those are the expensive ones," says Mags.

"Serta's excluded lenders get 25. Lumen's excluded holders eventually get about 99. The word is the same. 'Excluded.' In my field, if one label produces 25 in one sample and 99 in another, the label is not the variable. Something else is. What is it?"

Nobody answers. Ines looks at Mags; Mags looks at the credit agreement; the Ad Hoc Group, represented by counsel, declines to comment. The sales chat blinks: *client needs a level in 10. 78? 75? pass?*

Theo lays the three options on the napkin. Bid 78 and he has paid the seller's price for a seat he cannot see. Bid 75 and the answer itself is information: if the seller hits 75 in four seconds, he has learned which seat they were in, and paid 75 for the lesson. Pass, and the desk keeps its capital and loses a client who will remember that the systematic desk went quiet on the one morning it was needed. Each choice has a story behind it. None of the stories has a number yet. Write your own card before reading on: what you bid, in what size, and why. The desk's answer, and the market's, arrive at 4:50.

Theo stares at three hundred pages of credit agreement and one rumor. **The question this chapter answers: how do you turn a document and a rumor into a number you can quote against?**

The number Theo carries to Ines's desk at 4:50 is a product of four factors: how often the dealer deals, whether the deal leaves anyone out, whether this holder is the one left out, and what being left out costs. The chapter builds them in reverse, from the waterfall to the game to the score.

## 13.1 The LMT Zoo, Seen From the Desk

A liability management transaction is an out-of-court change to the capital structure, executed under the existing documents, that re-allocates recovery among claims that were drafted to be equal. That last clause is the whole thing. A pro rata amend-and-extend that treats every lender identically is liability management in the polite sense. The trades that make careers and lawsuits are the ones in which some holders of the same tranche end up senior to other holders of that tranche, or in which collateral that was everybody's becomes somebody's.

Start with a surf company, because the cleanest way to see the species is to watch one animal move. In April 2018 Boardriders, the owner of Quiksilver and Billabong, signed a credit agreement with about \$450 million of first-lien debt. Its owner, Oaktree, was also one of its lenders. Then 2020 arrived for everyone who sold board shorts through physical stores. In August 2020 the company did a deal that looked from a distance like a rescue: \$110 million of new super-priority money came in, and \$321 million of existing loans were rolled up, at par, into the same new super-priority tranche, \$431 million in all. A majority of lenders voted to delete the covenant that limited debt and the requirement that any new intercreditor arrangement be junior or pari passu. The roll-up was papered as "open market purchase" agreements. Remember those three words. Over this chapter they will cost a great many people a great deal of money.

Now look at that deal from the seats of two holders of the same loan on the same Friday. Holder A was in the group. Its loan was trading at a 40 to 50 percent discount, and it was exchanged at par into paper at the top of the stack. Holder B, one of the non-consenting lenders who together held about \$85 million, woke up on Monday holding exactly the document it had held on Friday, except that \$431 million now stood in front of it. Holder B's loan did not change a word. The words around it did.

The excluded lenders, ICG and York among them, sued in New York in October 2020. On October 17, 2022, Justice Andrea Masley refused to dismiss their breach-of-contract and implied-covenant claims, finding the open-market exception ambiguous. Then comes the twist that makes Boardriders the best teaching case in the zoo. In August 2023 Authentic Brands agreed to buy the company, and the settlement tied to the sale repaid the non-participants in full. Par. The case was dismissed with prejudice in September 2023.

So one uptier produced an excluded lender who was primed in 2020, litigating in 2022 and paid par in 2023. Nothing in the document predicted the par. What predicted it was the value of the company on the day the story ended, a sale large enough to pay everyone. That is half the answer to Kaz's question from the cold open; the other half, who decides which room you are in, takes until §13.5. The rest of this section builds the machine that turns "it depends what the company is worth when it ends" into a number.

From the desk, every species in the zoo has the same skeleton. There is an enterprise value $V$ at some resolution date (a refinancing, a sale, a Chapter 11 plan). There is a document state $\mathcal{D}$, which encodes who has a claim against which entity, at what priority, secured by what. And there is a waterfall operator $\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}$ that maps $V$ into a vector of recoveries per dollar of face, one entry per claim:

$$
\mathbf{R} = \mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(V), \qquad R_j \in [0, 1].
$$

An LMT is a move $\mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{D}'$. The economic content of the move, for claim $j$, is the **transfer function**

$$
\Delta_j(V) = \big[\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}'}(V)\big]_j - \big[\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(V)\big]_j .
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *Waterfalls and transfer functions.* Picture one till and one cash drawer, with creditors standing in queues. The waterfall is the house rule for service: the senior queue is paid in full before anyone behind it gets a cent, and people standing in the same queue split whatever reaches them pro rata. An LMT does not put cash in the drawer. It lets some people change queues. Tiny number: the drawer holds $V = 100$ and one class of lenders holds $F = 200$ of face, so everyone gets 50 cents on the dollar. Now let half the class, 100 of face, step into a new queue at the front. The front half is paid 100 cents, the back half gets zero, and the drawer still pays out exactly 100. The transfer function $\Delta_j(V)$ is "what claim $j$ got after the queue-jump, minus what it got before," written down for every possible size of drawer rather than just 100. Try a drawer of 250: before the jump everyone is paid in full, after it everyone is still paid in full, and the transfer is zero. Queue order matters only when the drawer is short, which is why this is a credit problem and not an accounting one. The full version, with guarantees and intercompany claims, is the clearing engine of §13.7.

Because $\Delta_j$ is a function of $V$, it is a derivative on enterprise value and can be priced like one: integrate it against a distribution of $V$ and you have the expected transfer. And because waterfalls conserve value, the transfers net out. If the face amounts under the two document states are $\mathbf{F}$ and $\mathbf{F}'$, then

$$
\mathbf{F}'^{\top}\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}'}(V) = \min\!\big(V,\ \mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{F}'\big), \qquad \mathbf{F}^{\top}\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{D}}(V) = \min\!\big(V,\ \mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{F}\big),
$$

so in any state where $V$ is below both totals, the dollar transfers sum to exactly zero. An LMT does not create recovery in the states that matter. It moves recovery. (It may change $V$ itself, by buying liquidity runway or by burning fees, and we will keep that effect separate and explicit; see §13.3.)

That is the sentence to say out loud on your first day: *an LMT is a zero-sum transfer among creditors in low states, wrapped in a liquidity story about high states.* The liquidity story is sometimes true. The transfer is always true.

Nobody knows what Larkspur will be worth when its story ends, so "a distribution of $V$" means a histogram of possible futures. The shape this chapter uses is the lognormal, which lives on the positive numbers and lets bad years compound rather than subtract (Chapter 6, §6.1.1, draws it; Chapter 5 has the rest of the zoo). A normal with mean 2,000 and standard deviation 800 would put about 0.6% of its futures below zero, futures in which a foam company is worth less than nothing. Pricing the transfer then means, in each future, multiplying the bar's height by what the queue-jump costs there, and adding up the bars.

>
> **Annotation.** Moyer's definition was written for the bond exchange offers of the 1990s and 2000s, and it travels unchanged into the LMT era with one twist that changes the game theory completely. In Moyer's world, coercion is *uniform*: every holder is offered the same exchange, and the threat to non-participants (stripped covenants, structural subordination) is the stick that solves the holdout problem. Everyone can take the carrot. In a non-pro-rata uptier, coercion is *selective*. The carrot is offered only to an invitation list, and the stick lands on everyone else, including holders who would have happily taken the carrot. That one change turns a coordination problem (will enough of us accept?) into a coalition-formation problem (who gets to be in the coalition that does the coercing?). Section 13.4 solves the second problem. The desk's practical departure from Moyer is this: in the 1990s you modeled your *participation decision*. In 2026 you model your *invitation probability*, because for most holders, most of the time, the decision is made for them.

> **Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality.** *Myth:* "LMEs avoid bankruptcy; that's the point." *Reality:* Roe and Rotaru hand-collected 89 coercive non-pro-rata LMEs and found that fewer than half avoided both bankruptcy and re-default within a year, and "within two years, only 22% avoided both"; among firms with at least two years of post-LME history, 56% had already filed, and those that filed spent two to three times longer in bankruptcy than non-LME prepacks (Harvard Law School Bankruptcy Roundtable, Feb 24, 2026; SSRN abstract 6103369). Moody's found about 46% of 2023 distressed exchanges had already re-defaulted, most in under a year (reported by Bloomberg Law, Apr 23, 2025). Proponents cite an S&P figure, as quoted by Antill, Jiang and Wang (HLS Roundtable, June 30, 2026), that 63% of LME users avoid Chapter 11. The two camps differ in their samples, horizons, and default definitions. Whichever number you believe, the LMT is not the terminal state of the credit. It is a jump to a new state with a new, usually higher, hazard. Model it that way (§13.8).

Mags puts it more simply when Theo reads the box aloud. "An LMT is a jump to a new state, and the new state has a clock on the wall. Of the eight big ones I'd make you learn, six filed anyway, and the typical gap between the deal and the petition was about a year." (§13.9's scoreboard has the eight and the dates.) That clock is the re-default hazard, and it will be a covariate before the chapter ends.

## 13.2 Legal Prose to State Space

You have spent years reading credit agreements as text. The model needs them as numbers. The translation has five clause families, and each one maps to a small set of features with units. The goal is not to replace a lawyer. It is to make the lawyer's reading *comparable across 1,500 borrowers*, so that the desk can rank them.

The way Theo does it for Larkspur, at 8:15 on the same Tuesday, is the way he did it at the old shop for a creditor committee: out loud, one clause at a time, with somebody else holding the marker. Today the somebody is Kaz, at the whiteboard, converting each clause into a variable with a type and a unit. Kaz is fast and precise and gets exactly one thing wrong per clause family, which turns out to be useful, because each wrong guess is the reading a model would make if nobody had told it otherwise.

"Section 2.18," Theo reads. "Pro rata sharing. Then 9.02(b), the amendment section, and the list of things that need 'each Lender directly and adversely affected thereby.'"

Kaz writes `affected_lender`. "Affected as in upset?"

"Affected as in paid less. Feelings are not a sacred right." Theo keeps going.

**Family 1: Pro rata sharing and sacred rights.** The pro rata sharing clause (Serta's was §2.18) says every lender in a tranche shares payments ratably. The amendment section (Serta's §9.02(b)) lists the "sacred rights" that need the consent of each affected lender rather than a majority. The question for an uptier is whether *subordination* of the loans, or of the liens, is on that list. If it is, a majority cannot prime the minority. That is the blocker. Features: `sacred_rights_scope` $\in$ {`payments_only`, `payments_plus_liens`, `payments_plus_subordination`}, and `has_serta_blocker` $\in \{0,1\}$, which is 1 when lien or payment subordination requires each affected lender's consent.

**Family 2: Voting thresholds.** The required-lender fraction $\theta$ is the share of the class that can amend everything not on the sacred list. In syndicated loans $\theta$ is usually a simple majority. Many indentures require two-thirds for lien releases (that was the threshold the Wesco participants needed). Sacred rights are $\theta = 1$ for the affected lender. Feature: `theta_required` $\in (0, 1]$, measured *for the specific action the deal requires*, not for the agreement in general. One agreement has several $\theta$'s, and the trapdoor is usually the lowest one that can be reached.

Kaz has written `required_lenders` on the board and underlined it. "So these are the lenders who are required to attend."

"These are the lenders whose votes count," says Theo, "and you have just found the fight, because the real question is the denominator. 'More than 50%' of *what*?" A senior partner at his old shop would not let a junior say the word "uptier" until the junior could say who sits outside that denominator. Theo can, for Larkspur: lenders who failed to fund are carved out of the count, and the sponsor's affiliated lenders are capped and deemed to vote with everyone else, so the class a coalition has to beat is smaller than the face on the agent's screen. A model that divides by total face when the document divides by "non-defaulting, non-affiliated" face overstates the bar. The partner's two follow-up questions become features beside it in the §13.2.1 table: whether Required Lenders can amend the amendment section itself (if they can, a majority can lower the bar and then walk under it), and what releasing all the collateral takes (66⅔% in most secured indentures, every lender in many syndicated credit agreements; Chapter 12-B, §12B.3, tabulates the thresholds). The percentages are where drafters earn their fees, so they are read from the agreement, never from a form.

"So the denominator is a political choice," says Kaz.

"The denominator is the whole fight. The numerator is just counting."

Theo turns the page to the assignment section, which is where Serta was won and lost.

**Family 3: Buyback exceptions to pro rata.** Most agreements let the borrower buy back its own loans without offering the same deal to everyone, through Dutch auctions open to all lenders or through open market purchases. This exception is how a non-pro-rata exchange slips past the pro rata clause. Feature: `buyback_exception` $\in$ {`none`, `dutch_only`, `open_market`, `purchase`}. On December 31, 2024, two appellate courts decided on the same day that the difference between the third and fourth values is worth the entire priming loss. The Fifth Circuit held in *Serta* that "an open market purchase is a purchase of corporate debt that occurs on the secondary market for syndicated loans," so a privately negotiated exchange did not qualify. New York's First Department held in *Mitel* that an undefined "purchases" exception, with no "open market" qualifier, was unambiguous and was satisfied by an exchange paid in new loans (as summarized by Paul, Weiss, Jan 10, 2025). Same kind of deal, same day, one word, opposite results. "Open market purchase" really is the most expensive three words in finance, and the word "open" accounts for most of the bill.

Kaz writes `buyback_exception` with its four values and pauses at the second. "And a Dutch auction is held where, Rotterdam?"

"Anywhere. It's a descending-price auction open to every lender in the class. The point is that it's *open to every lender*. That's what makes it pro rata in spirit. The other two values are where the spirit leaves the building."

**Family 4: Investments, restricted payments, and unrestricted subsidiaries.** This is drop-down territory. The question is how many dollars of value can legally leave the collateral group. The capacity is a sum of baskets:

$$
C^{\text{unsub}} = B^{\text{gen}} + B^{\text{builder}} + B^{\text{ratio}}\,\mathbf{1}[\text{ratio test passes}] + B^{\text{unsub}} + B^{\text{other}},
$$

where $B^{\text{gen}}$ is the general investment basket (often a "greater of" dollar amount and percentage of EBITDA or total assets), $B^{\text{builder}}$ is the builder basket (the "Available Amount" or "Cumulative Credit"), $B^{\text{ratio}}$ is the unlimited capacity that opens if a leverage test is met, $B^{\text{unsub}}$ is a dedicated basket for investments in unrestricted subsidiaries, and $B^{\text{other}}$ covers the capacity that hides in "Permitted Investments" clauses that can be stacked (the J.Crew trapdoor was precisely a stack: an investment in a non-guarantor restricted subsidiary, then onward from that subsidiary to an unrestricted one). Features: `unsub_capacity_pct_ev` $= C^{\text{unsub}}/\text{EV}$ and `rp_builder_pct_ev` $= B^{\text{builder}}/\text{EV}$, both dimensionless. Divide by enterprise value, not by face, because the question is what fraction of the *pie* can walk out the door.

"Cumulative Credit," Kaz reads off Theo's screen, and writes `rp_builder_pct_ev`. "Like a credit score that only goes up."

"Worse," says Theo. "It can go up while the company goes down." That is not a joke, and Moyer explains why.

The builder basket is a running account, and Moyer lays out the classic bond-indenture version line by line.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.173:* "50% of all net income earned 100% of proceeds from the sale of common or preferred stock 100% of the proceeds from the issuance of any junior debt securities Less: 100% of all net losses 100% of all amounts distributed as"
>
> **Annotation.** (The extraction preserves the page's line breaks as spaces; the original is a formatted list in which each item is a line of the builder computation.) This is the accounting identity behind $B^{\text{builder}}$: a starter amount, plus half of cumulative consolidated net income, plus equity proceeds and certain junior debt proceeds, less net losses and less every restricted payment and unrestricted-subsidiary investment already made. Code it as a state variable that evolves quarterly: $B^{\text{builder}}_{t+1} = B^{\text{builder}}_t + 0.5\,\text{CNI}_t^{+} - \text{CNI}_t^{-} + \text{EqProc}_t - \text{RP}_t - \text{Inv}^{\text{unsub}}_t$, floored at zero. Modern documents depart from Moyer's list in two ways that matter. Leveraged-loan agreements written after about 2015 often build from Consolidated EBITDA minus a multiple of fixed charges, or from a "greater of" starter that grows with EBITDA, so the builder can *grow while the company deteriorates* if EBITDA add-backs are generous (Mags is right to distrust anything called "adjusted"). And the builder is only one of several stackable baskets; the drop-down cases were won or lost on whether baskets could be used *sequentially through intermediate subsidiaries*. The feature you want is not "the builder" but the maximum dollar amount that can reach an unrestricted subsidiary by any legal path through the entity graph, which is a max-flow problem on the definitions (the parser in Chapter 25 builds that graph).

**Family 5: Lien and guarantee release, and the IP blockers.** Can a guarantor be released when it stops being wholly owned (the PetSmart/Chewy move)? Can material intellectual property be transferred out of the collateral group (the J.Crew move)? Can liens be released by a majority? Features: `jcrew_blocker`, `chewy_blocker`, `envision_blocker` (the last named for Envision's 2022 drop-down, on the §13.9 scoreboard), each in $\{0, 1\}$, plus `lien_release_theta`. Also flag `non_pro_rata_extension_allowed`, which captures the amend-and-extend mechanics that the 2025 "amend-extend-and-exchange" structures exploited.

Larkspur's security agreement has a springing lien in it: a lien that comes into existence on certain foreign assets only if a leverage test is failed. Kaz writes `springing_lien`, types it binary with a trigger date, and says, before Theo can open his mouth, "Not seasonal. I was told." Then Kaz draws a small flower next to it anyway.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The direct lender who wrote the document.* Read the same five families from the seat of a private-credit manager holding most of a unitranche or club loan, and the questions change shape. Family 1 and Family 2 matter less, because the lender group is three or four funds that hold the votes between them; nobody can be primed by a rival lender they cannot outvote. The danger is Families 4 and 5, value leaving the collateral group with the lender's consent never asked. Pluralsight is the private-side J.Crew. Vista Equity Partners took the company private in April 2021 with, among other debt, a \$1.175bn first-lien recurring-revenue term loan, sized on revenue rather than EBITDA and paying SOFR + 800 by 2023. In late May and early summer 2024 the company moved its intellectual property into a new restricted subsidiary, with about \$50mm from Vista funding an interest payment; lenders reportedly disputed the valuation of what moved. Around August 22, 2024, a lender group led by Blue Owl and Ares took ownership, converting about \$1.2bn of debt into equity. The marks tell the seat's story better than any quote. Ares Capital carried its \$106.2mm piece at 89.0 on December 31, 2023 and at 48.0 on June 30, 2024 (ARCC 10-Q); Blue Owl Capital Corp held the same loan at 96.8 on the December date. One loan, one coupon, two marks 7.8 points apart. From this seat the LMT question is not "will I be excluded?" but "will I end up owning the company, and is my mark telling me the truth about that?" A direct lender negotiates the blockers in Family 5 at origination, because it will not have a secondary market to sell into when it learns it should have.

### 13.2.1 The feature vector

Collect the document features $x^{\text{doc}}_i$ and add three groups the document cannot tell you. They differ mostly in how fast they move and how hard they are to get.

The **market state** $x^{\text{mkt}}_i(t)$ moves every day, and it is the easiest block to fill because somebody sells it to you. It holds the loan price `loan_px` (points), months to maturity, the liquidity cost score `lcs` (Ben Dor's measure, Chapter 8), and three-month equity momentum or, for sponsor-owned names with no public equity, the price momentum of the loan itself. On the Larkspur morning this block changed first: the mark went from 84 to a client asking about 78 before anyone had finished reading the headline. The danger of the market block is that it is so available. A model fed mostly prices learns to predict LMTs from the price fall that the rumor of an LMT causes, which is a very accurate way of being late.

The **liquidity state** $x^{\text{liq}}_i(t)$ moves monthly or quarterly and has to be built. Its main entry is `runway_months`, defined as unrestricted cash plus undrawn committed revolver, divided by forecast monthly cash burn. This is Theo's 13-week cash flow model, reborn for the third time in this book as a covariate. He builds the Larkspur version from the last 10-Q and a burn forecast that he trusts about as much as he trusts the sponsor, and it says eleven months. When `runway_months` falls below about nine and `loan_px` below about 80, the company is in the region where a sponsor *must* do something, and the only question is whether the something is pro rata. Larkspur is already through the price line at 78 and two months short of the runway line.

The **holder and sponsor state** $x^{\text{hold}}_i(t)$ is the one nobody can download. It contains `sponsor_repeat_lme` (has this sponsor done a non-pro-rata deal before?), `holder_hhi` (Herfindahl concentration of the class from CLO holdings data and Rule 2019 statements), `coop_reported` (a cooperation agreement has been reported), and `forum_ny_vs_tx` (which court is likely to hear the fight). Each entry is assembled by hand from documents that were not written for you: trustee reports, court filings, a news wire's paraphrase of "people familiar with the matter." It is also the block that answers the cold open's question about seats, which is why the vignette at the end of this section spends a whole Tuesday on how miserable it is to build.

The full state is $x_i(t) = \big(x^{\text{doc}}_i, x^{\text{mkt}}_i(t), x^{\text{liq}}_i(t), x^{\text{hold}}_i(t)\big)$. The document block is nearly static (it changes when the agreement is amended, which is itself an event). The other blocks move daily. Section 13.8 turns $x_i(t)$ into a hazard. Sections 13.3 to 13.7 compute what the hazard is a hazard *of*.

## 13.3 The Uptier as a Recovery Transfer

Start with the cleanest species and price it exactly. One secured class, face $F$, everyone pari passu. Before the deal, each lender recovers

$$
R_0(V) = \frac{\min(V, F)}{F}.
$$

The uptier has three moving parts. New money $N$ comes in as a first-out tranche. A fraction $p$ of the existing face (the participants) is exchanged into a second-out tranche at exchange ratio $x$ (new face per dollar of old face; $x < 1$ means the participants take a haircut in face to jump the line). The remaining $(1-p)F$ (the excluded lenders) stays in the old tranche, now third in line. Write $K_1 = N + xpF$ for the total face senior to the excluded lenders and $K_2 = K_1 + (1-p)F$ for the total face of the new structure. Then

$$
R^{\text{FO}}(V) = \frac{\min(V, N)}{N}, \qquad
R^{\text{SO}}(V) = \frac{\min\!\big((V - N)^+,\ xpF\big)}{xpF}, \qquad
R^{\text{excl}}(V) = \frac{\min\!\big((V - K_1)^+,\ (1-p)F\big)}{(1-p)F}.
$$

Before the algebra, a picture, because the picture is the trade.

> **Math, Gently.** *Three payoff shapes: the slice, the call spread and the digital.* Draw the excluded lender's recovery on graph paper, enterprise value along the bottom and cents on the dollar up the side. Before the deal it is a ramp: a straight line from the origin climbing at slope $1/F$ and flattening at 100 cents once $V$ covers the whole class. That is a **linear slice** of the firm. After the deal the ramp starts later, at $K_1$, because everyone now in front must be paid first, and it climbs more steeply, because the excluded lenders are a smaller group sharing each extra dollar. A ramp that starts at one strike and flattens at another is what an options desk calls a **call spread**: long a call struck at $K_1$, short a call struck at $K_2$. Shrink the excluded group and the ramp steepens into a cliff, zero just below $K_1$ and paid in full just above it. A payoff that jumps from nothing to everything at a single strike is a **digital**, or binary, option. The *difference* between the old ramp and the new one is a tent, rising with the old slice up to $K_1$ and falling away after it; as the excluded minority shrinks, the tent's right side becomes vertical, and what the lender lost is shaped like a **digital put**: it pays off against the lender in exactly the futures where $V$ falls short of $K_1$. Tiny number at Serta's terms (§13.3.1): $K_1 = 1{,}075$, so at $V = 1{,}000$ the old slice paid $1{,}000/2{,}400 \approx 41.7$ cents and the new one pays zero. The payoff algebra in full is in Chapter 6-B (§6-B.1), and the version for this deal is in "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section.

Call the excluded lender's priming loss $\Delta R^{\text{excl}}(V) = R_0(V) - R^{\text{excl}}(V)$: the transfer function of §13.1 for the excluded claim, with the sign flipped so that a loss is a positive number.

In the box's terms, the excluded lender traded a linear slice $\min(V,F)/F$ for a call spread between $K_1$ and $K_2$, and when the minority $(1-p)F$ is small that spread is nearly a *digital call* struck at $K_1$. So the priming loss is approximately

$$
\Delta R^{\text{excl}}(V) \approx R_0(V)\,\mathbf{1}[V < K_1].
$$

**The excluded lender is short a soft digital put on enterprise value, struck at the face that now sits ahead of it, with notional equal to its old recovery.** It wrote that put on the day the uptier closed. Nobody asked it to.

The participants, meanwhile, bought the same put. Per original dollar, a participant's position changed by $x R^{\text{SO}}(V) - R_0(V)$ (ignoring the new-money economics, which are usually priced generously). In low states this is large and positive. In high states it is $x - 1 < 0$: the participant paid for its put with $(1-x)$ points of face. A participant in an uptier is a lender who bought downside protection and paid the premium in principal. The excluded lender is the counterparty who wrote that protection for free.

How much is the put worth? Pricing it means asking how much weight the histogram of §13.1 puts on futures below $K_1$, and how much the excluded lender would have collected in each of them. That sum is a *partial expectation*, an average of $V$ taken over only the futures below the strike, and for a lognormal it is the put leg of Black–Scholes struck at $K_1$. Its one wrinkle is the $e^{s^2/2}$ that sets a lognormal's mean above $e^{\mu}$, the same half-sigma-squared Itô charges in continuous time (Chapter 5, §5.2.3, and Chapter 6, §6.1, have the picture).

The derivation makes this exact, but the headline fits on a napkin. To first order, the excluded lender's expected loss is that partial expectation divided by the old face, and the answer is a statement about where you think $V$ lives. Centre the lognormal at 2,000 with $s = 0.4$ and the argument of $\Phi$ is $(\ln 1{,}075 - \ln 2{,}000 - 0.16)/0.4 \approx -1.95$, so Serta's first region costs about $2{,}166 \times 0.026 / 2{,}400 \approx 2.3$ points: a company expected to be worth 2,000 rarely visits 1,075. Centre it at 1,200, closer to a mattress company whose loans were trading "far below par" in the spring of 2020, and the same term is about 13.5 points (illustrative; our arithmetic). The middle region, where the excluded lenders are partly paid, adds a correction to either. The put is cheap or ruinous depending almost entirely on one input, and that input is the one nobody can observe.

**Where value enters.** New money is not free value. If the $N$ dollars buy runway that survives to resolution, the post-deal enterprise value is $V + \kappa N$, with $\kappa \in [0, 1]$ the fraction of new money still in the firm at resolution, and every formula above should be evaluated at $V + \kappa N$ in the post-deal world. For a company burning cash, $\kappa$ is close to zero by the time anyone files. Roe and Rotaru's evidence that leverage did not fall and ratings stayed flat after LMEs is evidence that, on average, $\kappa$ is small.

#### The Full Derivation: the excluded lender's transfer

**Conservation check.** Multiply each recovery by its face and add: $N R^{\text{FO}} + xpF\,R^{\text{SO}} + (1-p)F\,R^{\text{excl}} = \min(V, K_2)$. Total face fell from $F$ to $K_2 - N = F - (1-x)pF$, which is the "deleveraging" in the press release. It is real, and it is paid for entirely by the participants, in exactly those states where nobody needed it.

**The excluded lender's transfer.** The priming loss is $\Delta R^{\text{excl}}(V) = R_0(V) - R^{\text{excl}}(V)$. It is piecewise linear with three regions:

$$
\Delta R^{\text{excl}}(V) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{V}{F}, & V \le K_1, \\[2ex]
\dfrac{V}{F} - \dfrac{V - K_1}{(1-p)F}, & K_1 < V < K_2, \\[2ex]
R_0(V) - 1, & V \ge K_2 .
\end{cases}
$$

In the first region the excluded lender loses everything it would have recovered. In the middle region the loss shrinks linearly, at slope $\frac{1}{F} - \frac{1}{(1-p)F} < 0$. In the third region the loss is *non-positive*: if $K_2 \le V < F$, the excluded lender is paid in full while it would have recovered only $V/F$ before. It gains, because the participants gave up face. (The gain is small and lives in a narrow band of states; when $V \ge F$ both recoveries are 1 and the transfer is zero.)

**The expected transfer, in closed form.** Suppose $V$ is lognormal under $\mathbb{P}$ at the resolution horizon: $\ln V \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, s^2)$. The dominant term of the expected loss is the partial expectation below the strike, which has the same form as the put leg of Black–Scholes:

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\!\big[V\,\mathbf{1}[V < K]\big] = e^{\mu + s^2/2}\,\Phi\!\left(\frac{\ln K - \mu - s^2}{s}\right).
$$

So for $K_1 \le F$, the first region contributes $\frac{1}{F}e^{\mu + s^2/2}\Phi\big((\ln K_1 - \mu - s^2)/s\big)$ to $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\Delta R^{\text{excl}}]$. The middle and upper regions add the correction terms. The code in §13.8 computes all three exactly by quadrature and checks the first against this closed form by Monte Carlo. This is the uptier's version of checking a simulated Merton spread against the formula: if your simulated transfer and your partial expectation disagree, your waterfall has a bug.

### 13.3.1 Case Study: Serta Simmons (2016–2026), in the Formula

*A replay. The cast is fictional and its dialogue invented; the dated facts come from the Fifth Circuit's opinion and the law-firm summaries cited.*

The desk keeps a binder for Serta. It is the only binder on a floor that otherwise believes paper is a legacy system, and on the Wednesday evening after the Larkspur headline Theo takes it down, because Mags said "Serta" in the cold open the way other people say "the flood." Nobody in this scene was in the room in 2020. They are reading the record: the Fifth Circuit's opinion, the remand findings as the law firms summarized them, and a decade of Mags's memory, which she insists is also a primary source.

Kaz lays a sheet of paper over the opinion and slides it down a paragraph at a time. "Nobody reads below the sheet," Kaz says. "If we know the ending we will fit to it. That is look-ahead bias, and I am told it is frowned upon even in law."

**I. 2016: the document.** The opinion supplies the parameters. In 2016 Serta Simmons Bedding refinanced with "\$1.95 billion in first-lien syndicated loans and \$450 million in second-lien syndicated loans." Theo reads the three clauses that matter in the order a model would want them. Section 2.18 is pro rata sharing, with a remedy in §2.18(c): a lender that receives a disproportionate payment must "purchase (for Cash at face value) participations" so that the benefit is shared ratably. Section 9.02(b) makes the core payment terms sacred, needing each affected lender. Section 9.05(g) lets the borrower buy back loans without offering the same deal to everyone, through a Dutch auction open to all lenders, which the agreement defines to the comma, or through "open market purchases," which it does not define at all.

"In 2016 nobody read 9.05(g) for anything but buybacks," says Mags. "Buybacks were what you did when your loans were at 85 and you had cash in the drawer. It was a cleanup clause. It was the junk drawer of the agreement."

"And the junk drawer," says Kaz, "is where the knife was."

**II. Spring 2020: two groups and an auction.** Mattress showrooms closed along with the rest of physical retail, and Serta's loans "were trading far below par." Two organized groups of lenders formed and each took a proposal to the company. One group became, in the opinion's capital letters, the Prevailing Lenders. The other group did not lose because it was principled. The opinion records that the eventual Excluded Lenders "had made an alternative recapitalization proposal involving a similar kind of debt swap" themselves. Among those who would later fight the deal in court were the LCM lenders, whose names are CLO vehicles, and a Citadel fund that challenged the plan's indemnity.

Ines reads that paragraph twice. "So it was an auction, and the company chose a bidder. The losers weren't bystanders. They were the other bidder." She looks at Theo. "You ran processes like this. What did the company want?"

"Runway, a lower face amount, and enough votes," says Theo. "It needed a bare majority of the first lien. Whichever group could deliver that most cheaply won." He stops, because he has just described §13.4's knapsack without meaning to.

**III. June 22, 2020: the deal.** Kaz slides the sheet down. "The Prevailing Lenders provided SSB with \$200 million in new financing in exchange for \$200 million in first-out, super-priority debt. The Prevailing Lenders also traded in \$1.2 billion of their first-lien and second-lien loans for approximately \$875 million in second-out, super-priority debt." The Prevailing Lenders were Barings, Boston Management and Research, Credit Suisse Asset Management, Eaton Vance and Invesco Senior Secured Management. They "held a bare majority of the outstanding first-line debt" (sic; the opinion means first-lien), 52.6% of the class on the remand findings. First-lien loans went into the second-out at 74 cents of new face per dollar, second-lien loans at 39, discounts of 26% and 61%. The deal closed on Monday, June 22, 2020.

Map it, treating the two liens as one blended class for the moment (an approximation we flag; the 2L lenders were already junior, so the blended class *understates* the 1L excluded lenders' pre-deal recovery in low states, and with it their loss, while overstating the 2L's; we unblend below):

- $N = 200$, $F = 2{,}400$ (all figures \$mm), $pF = 1{,}200$, so $p = 0.5$.
- $x = 875/1{,}200 \approx 0.729$, a face haircut of about 27 points for the participants.
- $K_1 = 200 + 875 = 1{,}075$. $K_2 = 1{,}075 + 1{,}200 = 2{,}275$.

So after June 2020, the excluded lenders recovered nothing unless enterprise value exceeded about \$1.075bn. In a state where $V = \$1.075$bn, their pre-deal blended recovery would have been $1{,}075/2{,}400 \approx 44.8$ cents. The transfer in that state is the entire 44.8 points. **Unblending the liens.** Theo's old partner would have circled the word "blended" in red, and he would have been right. The first-lien lenders were never pari passu with the second lien. Before June 2020 the \$450mm second lien stood behind them, so their pre-deal slice was the first-lien ramp, not the blended one. The remand findings give enough of the pieces to separate them. In June 2020 the first lien outstanding was \$1,887mm; the excluded lenders held \$895mm of it (47.4%) and the participants the remaining \$992mm, the "bare majority." The participants' first-lien loans became \$734mm of second-out face, which is the 74-cent exchange rate at work ($992 \times 0.74 \approx 734$). There are two honest ways to set the strike for the excluded first lien. Count only the new money plus the face the first-lien class moved ahead of its own minority, and $K_1 = 200 + 734 = 934$; that is the slice the remand court's ratable-share arithmetic works with. Count everything that now ranks ahead of the excluded first lien, including the second-out that second-lien participants received, and $K_1$ stays at 1,075, because a super-priority claim is ahead of the excluded first lien whoever held it the week before. The pre-deal slice is where the blend really misleads:

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Blended (Figure 13.1) | 1,075 | 2,400 (both liens) | 44.8 |

The blended picture understates the first-lien minority's peak loss by between three and twelve points, depending on which strike you believe and which face you divide by (our arithmetic). The shape does not change: it is still a short digital put. The notional does. The published figures do not tie exactly (the participants' \$992mm of first lien at 74 and the rest of the \$1.2bn at 39 give somewhat less than \$875mm of second-out), which is one more reason Exercise 1 asks for the sensitivity to the lien split rather than a single answer.

#### Decision point: Friday, 19 June 2020

Kaz's sheet has stopped at June 2020. Everything below it is still hidden, and Theo, who is starting to enjoy this, sets the hand up the way Ines would.

"Friday, June 19, 2020. You hold \$50mm of Serta first lien. You were in the group that lost. Assume you know the terms of the winning deal, and it closes Monday. Three choices." He writes them on the whiteboard, under Kaz's flower from §13.2. Sell into whatever bid the market will make for paper about to sit behind \$1.075bn of new claims; nobody on the desk has a verified print from that Friday, but a court would later value the loans at 25 on closing day, so call it a check for about \$12.5mm. Hold, and fund a lawsuit for as long as it takes, with New York's 9% statutory prejudgment interest running from the date of breach if you win. Or buy more, on the theory that the put the excluded lenders are about to write has a second strike, at the courthouse.

Mags goes first, because she always does when the answer involves lawyers. "Hold, and I'd look at buying more. Read 9.05(g). The Dutch auction is defined to the comma. 'Open market purchase' isn't defined at all, and that deal did not happen on any market I have ever traded in. Losing bidders sue, and this losing group is big enough to pay for it. Contract damages run from the breach. Every point that loan drops on Monday is a point of damages, plus interest."

Ines shakes her head slowly, which for her is shouting. "You're describing a hand you have to win three times. Once in a court you don't choose, reading a clause with two plausible readings. Once on appeal. Once at collection, against defendants who will still be paying lawyers in 2026. And the price on the screen already includes the lawsuit; whoever buys your loan buys whatever claim comes with it, as far as anyone can tell on a Friday. Your edge isn't legal. Mine isn't either. Sell, and stop being short a put you didn't choose to write."

"You're pricing the judge at a coin flip."

"I'm pricing my ignorance of the judge at a coin flip. That's different, and it's honest."

Theo, who spent six years on the other side of calls exactly like this, notices what neither of them has said. "The question I'd have asked in June 2020 is who advised the losing group. Losing bidders with good counsel have already built the record by the time the deal closes. That isn't an edge on the law. It's an edge on whether there will be a fight at all."

Kaz writes `p_lit ≈ 1, p_win = ?` in a box, looks at it for a moment, and lifts the sheet.

**IV. January 2023 to November 2025: the courts.** Serta filed for Chapter 11 in the Southern District of Texas on January 23, 2023, about 31 months after the uptier: longer than the year-or-so clock Mags quoted in §13.1, much shorter than the life of a loan. In 2023 the bankruptcy court read "open market purchase" for the borrower and upheld the uptier, and the plan that followed gave the participants an indemnity against the excluded lenders' claims. The plan paid the excluded lenders very little, low single-digit cents by one law-firm count (Skadden). For two years the hold-and-litigate trade was marked as a loss with a court opinion attached.

On December 31, 2024, the Fifth Circuit reversed: "the 2020 Uptier was not a permissible open market purchase within the meaning of the 2016 Agreement." It read the "deafening silence" around an undefined term against the meticulous definition of a Dutch auction a few lines away. The same morning brought New York's opposite reading of *Mitel* (§13.2, Family 3).

The other remedy matters too. Serta's plan gave the participants an indemnity for their litigation exposure. The Fifth Circuit held that this violated the equal-treatment rule of Bankruptcy Code §1123(a)(4) because "the unequal treatment in this case implicates both opportunity and result," and chose "to excise the offending indemnity." In a footnote it disposed of the argument that offering everyone the same *opportunity* is enough, with a hypothetical worth memorizing: a plan that gives every class member "a note promising payment of \$5 million in one year if the member is headquartered in Louisiana." Equal opportunity, in the sense that anyone may move to Louisiana. In the language of this section, the indemnity was the participants' hedge against the litigation leg of their put. The court cancelled the hedge and left them with the exposure.

The Fifth Circuit denied rehearing on February 18, 2025, and the Supreme Court denied certiorari on November 10, 2025, on a petition that attacked only the indemnity ruling.

**V. July 2026: the remand.** Six years later a court put a number on it. On remand, the bankruptcy court for the Southern District of Texas issued a memorandum opinion on July 7, 2026, finding certain participating lenders liable for breach of the pro rata sharing provision and awarding \$261.13mm plus prejudgment interest. As summarized by Mayer Brown (July 31, 2026): damages were measured as of the breach date, June 22, 2020; the benefit taken was the face value (\$734mm) of the first-lien second-out debt the participants received; the excluded lenders' ratable share was \$348mm (\$895mm / \$1.887bn × \$734mm); the excluded loans were valued at their 25-cent closing-date market price; the "but-for" world was worth \$484.88mm and the actual outcome \$223.75mm (that is, \$895mm × 0.25); and the difference, \$261.13mm, carries mandatory 9% New York prejudgment interest from June 22, 2020. At simple interest for about six years, that adds roughly 54%, or about \$142mm (our arithmetic, illustrative).

Notice which measure the court used. It did not compute $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\Delta R]$ over a distribution of enterprise values. It used a market price on the closing date, which is a $\mathbb{Q}$-flavored number with the risk premium baked in. For the desk this is useful twice over. It gives a hard calibration point for the expected damages term in the litigation outside option (§13.4), and it tells you that if you are long excluded paper with a live claim, the claim's value is anchored to *where the loan traded the day the deal closed*, not to where it ends up.

Kaz does the arithmetic for the hold-and-litigate path out loud. On the court's own accounting the excluded lenders are back to about 70 cents on face: the 25 cents the court says they kept, \$223.75mm, plus the award with interest, roughly \$403mm (Law360, July 22, 2026, called it "roughly \$400 million"), together about \$627mm on \$895mm of loans. That is our arithmetic, illustrative, and it comes before allocation among the defendants, collection risk and any appeal (we found none reported). But a holder's P&L is not a court's accounting. The 25 cents was a market price on a Monday, not cash in anyone's hand. A holder who rode the loan into Chapter 11 got the plan's few cents and then, if every dollar is collected, the award: about 45 cents per dollar of face (\$403mm over \$895mm). Call it the mid-to-high 40s in 2026 against 25 available in June 2020, roughly 11% a year compounded, before legal fees, and after two years of being marked as the loser.

"So I was right," says Mags.

"You were paid," says Ines. "In June 2020 your hand needed a Fifth Circuit panel to read a word the bankruptcy court then read the other way. The same conviction, applied to a document that said 'purchase' instead of 'open market purchase,' lost in New York on the same day you won in New Orleans."

Neither concedes, and the book does not referee. What the record does settle is the shape of the trade. On June 22, 2020, the excluded lender wrote a digital put on enterprise value, and kept, as a separate asset, a second option struck at the courthouse, whose payoff was measured at the closing-day price and accrued 9% a year. The market priced the first option on Monday. Nobody priced the second, because its value depended on one word, one forum and six years. Section 13.4 prices the threat of that second option as the litigation outside option; §13.9 treats the forum as what it is, a random variable.

> **Trend Watch.** *The strike moved, and so did the volume.* **Documents:** Buccola and Nini's study of 600+ leveraged loan agreements (2016–2022) found that "prior to 2020, only 40% of loans restricted uptiers, often inadvertently," while "by mid-2022, 85% of new loans explicitly blocked uptiers, with 70% requiring unanimous lender consent for subordination" (HLS Roundtable, Apr 8, 2025; *Journal of Legal Studies* 53:489, 2024). **Aggressiveness:** Octus counted 11 non-pro-rata uptiers from March 2022 to July 2024; Wesco, Mitel and Robertshaw moved 100% of value away from non-ad-hoc creditors, while the six most recent moved an average of 18% (Apex 38%, Valcour 12%, EyeCare 13%, GoTo 13%) (Octus, July 16, 2024). **Counts:** LMEs hit a high-water mark of 38 in 2024 and fell to 24 in 2025, but still accounted for about two-thirds of default activity by issuer count in 2025 and exceeded both payment defaults and bankruptcies on a rolling 12-month basis in every month of 2025 (Wachtell Lipton, *Liability Management 2026: For Better or Worse*, Feb 3, 2026). In H1 2026, 25 LMEs closed, against 24 in H1 2025, and "the coercive uptier structures that defined 2025 have all but vanished," replaced by equity handovers and consensual amend-and-extends (9fin, Aug 28, 2026). **What it means for the desk:** in the formula above, blockers drive the probability of a non-pro-rata deal down, and later deals have higher $K_1$ relative to $F$ (smaller transfers). A PRS model trained on 2020–2022 events will overstate both the probability and the severity of a 2026 uptier, unless vintage enters as a covariate. The transfer did not disappear. It migrated to structures (drop-downs, double-dips, amend-extend-and-exchange) whose clauses the 2020-vintage blockers do not cover.

## 13.4 Game Theory I: Threshold Games and the Sponsor's Auction

The waterfall tells you what the transfer is. The game tells you who ends up on which side of it.

**The threshold game.** A class has lenders $i = 1, \dots, n$ with holdings $w_i$, $\sum_i w_i = 1$. An amendment passes if a coalition $S$ with $\sum_{i \in S} w_i > \theta$ consents. Let $u^{\text{in}}$ be the per-dollar value of being inside a winning coalition, $u^0$ the value of the status quo, and $u^{\text{out}}$ the value of being primed, with $u^{\text{in}} > u^0 > u^{\text{out}}$. (From §13.3: $u^{\text{in}} - u^0 = \mathbb{E}[x R^{\text{SO}} - R_0]$ plus new-money economics, and $u^0 - u^{\text{out}} = \mathbb{E}[\Delta R^{\text{excl}}]$.)

Take an invited lender $i$ and collapse everyone else into "the rest of the class." There are three relevant situations: the rest can win without $i$, the rest can win only with $i$ (lender $i$ is *pivotal*), or no deal is possible. The payoff to $i$ is:

| | Rest wins without $i$ | $i$ is pivotal | No deal possible |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $i$ **Joins** | $u^{\text{in}}$ | $u^{\text{in}}$ | $u^0$ |
| $i$ **Holds out** | $u^{\text{out}}$ | $u^0$ | $u^0$ |

*Join* is at least as good as *Hold out* in every column, so an invited lender needs no forecast of which column it is in; game theorists say Join *weakly dominates*. Put illustrative cents in the cells, $u^{\text{in}} = 80$, $u^0 = 60$ and $u^{\text{out}} = 25$: Join pays 80, 80 and 60 across the columns, holding out pays 25, 60 and 60, never more and 55 points less in the first. That is the prisoner's dilemma Moyer describes for exchange offers, with one sharpening: in a selective deal the bottom-left cell is $u^{\text{out}}$, the primed outcome, which is strictly worse than the status quo. Holding out does not preserve your position. It hands your recovery to the people who did not hold out.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.85:* "those not participating in the exchange may be better off than those that do, but if too many hold out, all will be worse off. This is the general game theory problem known as the “prisoner’s dilemma.”"
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer's holdout problem runs the opposite way from the uptier's. In a uniform exchange offer, the *holdout* is the free rider: it keeps its old claim at full face while the participants deleverage the company and make the old claim safer, and the danger is that everyone free-rides and the deal fails. The issuer's answer was to make holding out costly, the coercive exchange of §13.1. The non-pro-rata uptier makes it *maximally* costly and then removes the option to participate from most holders, which is the payoff matrix above. Moyer asks what participation rate makes the deal work. The desk asks the §13.1 question, its invitation probability, and answering it means solving the sponsor's optimization, next.

**The sponsor's problem is a knapsack.** The sponsor (acting through the borrower) does not want every lender. It wants the cheapest set of lenders that clears the threshold. Let $c_i$ be the per-dollar concession needed to bring lender $i$ in: the exchange ratio, the new-money economics, the fees. Then

$$
\min_{S \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}} \sum_{i \in S} c_i w_i \quad \text{subject to} \quad \sum_{i \in S} w_i \ge \theta + \varepsilon .
$$

This is a 0–1 knapsack in its minimization form: the sponsor packs a bag that must weigh at least $\theta$ of the class, and every lender packed has a price. Packing the cheapest per dollar first usually works and sometimes overpays, because the last lender added may be a large one when a small one would have done; Figure 13.2's coalition is topped up by the smallest CLO for exactly that reason. The exact answer checks every bag. "NP-hard" means the count doubles with every extra holder, not that the problem is hard today: 12 holders make $2^{12} = 4{,}096$ bags, which a laptop checks before the coffee cools, and at 40 holders, about a trillion bags, you round holdings to a basis point and use dynamic programming, or notice that the bottom 28 holders are dust. The slack $\varepsilon$ is not decorative. It covers holdings that change hands between signing and closing, disputed votes, and the holders who simply do not respond.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.87:* "the “conscious” participation rate must make up for certain “innocent” holdouts."
>
> **Annotation.** In a public bond exchange the innocent holdouts are retail holders who never read the notice. In a loan uptier they are the lenders the sponsor never called. The sponsor's coalition must clear $\theta$ with a margin $\varepsilon$, and the knapsack solution is what determines who is "innocent." The modern twist is that innocence is now a choice made by the counterparty, not by the holder.

The cost vector $c_i$ is where your institutional knowledge goes, and it predicts who gets excluded. A distressed fund that can write a new-money ticket is cheap to include: it *brings* value to the coalition. A CLO is expensive, for reasons written into its own rulebook (the seat box below). A lender that has sued this sponsor before is expensive in a different way. The knapsack therefore excludes the fragmented, the constrained, and the litigious, which is roughly who the Serta and Mitel excluded lenders were: in *Serta* the appellants included CLO managers and Citadel; the Fifth Circuit noted that after the bankruptcy, the remainder of the super-priority debt was "probably held by entities like Citadel that did not participate." Figure 13.2 runs the knapsack on the nine-holder register of the §13.8 demo.

![Figure 13.2: Funds that cost 2 to 3 cents per dollar supply 50% of the class and the smallest CLO tops the coalition up to 54%, so the litigator (15 cents) and the three larger CLOs are left out. Illustrative: min_cost_coalition from Listing 13.2 on its nine-holder register (θ = 0.5, ε = 0.01).](assets/figures/ch13_sponsor_knapsack.png)

> **View From Another Seat.** *The CLO manager.* The cost vector above prices a CLO as an expensive coalition member, and the reasons are in the vehicle's own rulebook. Beyond the 7.5% CCC bucket and the lowest-market-value haircut that Theo remembered at 7:41, a loan bought at a discount stays at its purchase price until it trades above about 90 for more than 30 days, and "the median CLO operates within 4% of the most stringent covenant threshold" (Kundu, ESRB Working Paper 141, March 2023). The cushions are thin in practice: one CLO-equity investor reported market-wide CCC exposure averaging 4.3% and junior overcollateralization cushions of 3.9% (Thomas Majewski, Eagle Point Income Co. shareholder letter, Feb 18, 2026). A CLO often cannot hold equity from an exchange, and its documents may limit the new instruments it can take, so it cannot sign up for a deal that pays in odd paper. That makes the CLO look like the natural victim of an uptier, the lender too constrained to say yes. Serta is the correction: the Prevailing Lenders were large loan managers too, so loan managers sat on both sides of one deal (§13.3.1), and a CLO's seat was decided the way everyone else's was, by who called whom in May. What the CLO seat changes is the exit. A downgrade pushes the bucket over 7.5%, the excess is marked at the bucket's lowest price, and the manager sells, often its higher-priced CCC loans first, because that is what the haircut rule rewards (Kundu's "contractual arbitrage"). The forced seller often sells the wrong loan, and the desk that knows the test dates knows when the forced seller will call.

**The auction between groups.** The richer version has two organized groups, $A$ and $B$, each able to reach $\theta$ by recruiting unaffiliated holders. Each proposes a package to the sponsor. Serta's excluded lenders were exactly this, as Ines read it off the opinion in §13.3.1: the other bidder.

That fact changes the auction's economics. In an ordinary auction a losing bidder gets zero. Here a losing bidder gets primed. Let $T^{\text{win}}$ be the winning group's gain from the transfer and $T^{\text{lose}}$ the losing group's loss from being primed, and let $b$ be the concession the winner pays the sponsor (better terms for the company, more new money, a bigger haircut). Group $B$ is willing to outbid $A$ as long as winning at bid $b$ beats losing:

$$
T^{\text{win}} - b \;\ge\; -T^{\text{lose}} + \pi^{\text{lit}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
b \;\le\; T^{\text{win}} + T^{\text{lose}} - \pi^{\text{lit}} .
$$

With symmetric groups, Bertrand competition drives the winning bid to that bound. The sponsor extracts not only the winner's gain but also the loser's *fear*, less the loser's expected litigation recovery $\pi^{\text{lit}}$. This is the auction-with-externalities logic of Jehiel, Moldovanu and Stacchetti, *How (Not) to Sell Nuclear Weapons* (American Economic Review, 1996): when losing hurts, bidders pay to avoid it, and the seller can capture more than the item is worth to the winner. In LMT terms, equity captures the transfer by auctioning the right not to be its victim.

> **Math, Gently.** *Auctions where the loser gets hurt.* Picture an auction where the loser does not go home empty-handed but primed: each bidder will now pay up to its gain from winning *plus* its fear of losing. Two similar bidders undercutting each other's terms, like two identical shops in a price war (the Bertrand competition above), push the winning bid all the way to that ceiling. Tiny number: the seat is worth $T^{\text{win}} = 20$ points to the winner, being primed costs $T^{\text{lose}} = 30$, and suing afterwards is worth $\pi^{\text{lit}} = 10$. The ceiling is $20 + 30 - 10 = 40$. A group will hand the sponsor up to 40 points of concessions for a seat worth 20 to it, because the alternative costs 30, less the 10 a court might give back. The only party at the table that profits from the fear is the equity.

**The litigation outside option.** The term that disciplines the auction is

$$
\pi^{\text{lit}} = q \cdot \mathbb{E}[\text{damages} \mid \text{win}] - \text{cost},
$$

where $q$ is the probability a court finds the deal impermissible. $q$ depends on the clause and the forum, and since December 31, 2024, the dependence is sharp: high for an `open_market` exception litigated in the Fifth Circuit after *Serta*, low for a `purchase` exception in New York after *Mitel*. The damages term now has an anchor: \$261.13mm plus 9% interest on roughly \$895mm of excluded holdings in *Serta*. A higher $\pi^{\text{lit}}$ lowers the sponsor's take and, through the inequality, makes groups less willing to bid. That is the channel through which a court ruling moves the frequency of deals, not just their legality.

**Levers the sponsor pulls.** Buccola and Kahan (*Getting to Yes*, HLS Roundtable, Mar 25, 2025; SSRN abstract 5014946) catalog the sources of coercion in these deals. In the language of the matrix, every lever does one of three things. It lowers $u^{\text{out}}$ (how deeply non-participants are primed), it raises $u^{\text{in}} - u^0$ (how rich the participants' deal is), or it lowers the effective $\theta$. The third is the most aggressive, and Wesco/Incora (2022) is its purest form: issue yourself enough notes to become two-thirds, then vote as two-thirds. Section 13.9 walks the four steps and what the courts made of them.

**Robertshaw: the bag that packed itself.** The knapsack assumes the sponsor assembles the coalition and the lenders wait to be called. Robertshaw shows the coalition assembling itself, twice. A May 2023 super-priority deal had defined "Required Lender" as more than 50% of the first-out plus second-out loans, read from the agent's register. In July 2023 Invesco quietly bought enough of those loans to cross 50%, and became Required Lender without anyone calling it. An ad hoc group of the other lenders (Bain Capital Credit, Canyon and Eaton Vance) and the sponsor, One Rock, answered on December 11, 2023: they lent \$228.3mm to RS Funding, a vehicle the parent voted and Robertshaw economically owned, and the cash prepaid \$117.6mm of first-out loans. Invesco fell below 50%, and the other group became Required Lender in its place. Robertshaw filed for Chapter 11 on February 15, 2024, and on June 20, 2024, Judge Lopez held that the new group were the Required Lenders and that Invesco's remedy was a money claim against the company, not its control back. Required-Lender status is a position you can trade into and out of, at prices nobody publishes, so `holder_hhi` and every coalition input must be re-read from the register as often as the register can be had, not lifted from the last 10-Q or the last Rule 2019 statement. When the register is the battlefield, the knapsack is a snapshot of a fight, and the sponsor is only one of the people packing bags.

**Why prices drift before announcements.** One more refinement explains a pattern every loan trader has seen. Lenders do not observe $V$ exactly. Each gets a noisy signal $s_i = V + \sigma \epsilon_i$. In the global-games framework of Morris and Shin (*Unique Equilibrium in a Model of Self-Fulfilling Currency Attacks*, American Economic Review, 1998), a coordination game with noisy private signals has a unique threshold equilibrium: each player joins the coalition if and only if its signal falls below a cutoff $s^*$, so the coalition forms if and only if $V$ is below some $V^*$. The cutoff is common knowledge in equilibrium, which means *the act of organizing is itself a signal that the organizers' estimates of $V$ are low*. Loan prices drift down as groups organize, before anything is announced. For the desk, an organizing signal (counsel hired, a Rule 2019 statement, a co-op reported) is a downward update on $V$ and an upward update on the PRS at the same time.

> **Math, Gently.** *Global games in one picture.* Every lender in the class carries a thermometer, and every thermometer is slightly off. Nobody knows the company's true temperature, $V$. But each lender knows that everyone else's thermometer reads close to its own: if mine says "cold," yours probably says "cold-ish," and you know that mine does, and I know that you know. That chain of "I know that you know" collapses to a single cutoff reading. Below it you join a group; above it you don't. Nobody has to agree with anybody, and the cutoff still emerges from the noise. Tiny number: if the cutoff reading is 1,500 and thermometers err by about 100, a lender reading 1,400 joins, and it expects most others to have read below 1,500 too, which is why a group can form in a week once the first call is made.

Prices usually drift down as groups organize. Not always, and the exception teaches the rule. When the threshold is a supermajority and a minority can block it, the tranche that carries the blocking vote becomes a seat, and seats can be bid up. Wesco Aircraft's 2026 secured notes needed two-thirds consent for anything with "the effect of" releasing substantially all collateral. In February 2022 a minority group announced it held more than a third of the 2026s, enough to block the majority's plan, and the two sides raced for the marginal bond; by one market commentator's account the 2026 notes ran from about 85 to above par in the scramble. The price moved *up* because the seat, not the company, was being bought. A model that reads every organizing headline as bad news for the bond will get that tranche exactly backwards, and §13.9 tells the rest of that story.

> **Poker Table.** "The sponsor isn't a player," Ines says. "The sponsor is the dealer, and the dealer chooses who sees the flop. Every lender at the table thinks they're playing the other lenders. They're playing for a seat." She taps the screen. "So when that client offers the Larkspur loan at 78, I don't ask what the loan is worth. I ask which seat the seller has." Her hand history, replayed through markouts: suppose the desk had bought the \$15mm at 78, the uptier closed three weeks later, and the excluded tranche marked at 52 *(illustrative)*. The markout is $-26$ points, or $-\$3.9$mm, on one ticket. Across many such hands the average markout on "client sells after an organizing headline" is the market's estimate of $\Pr(\text{excluded}) \cdot \mathbb{E}[\Delta R^{\text{excl}}]$, which is exactly what §13.8 tries to forecast before the headline.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.86:* "as in poker, which is analogous because the “multiple-hand” format of the game is similar to the series of transactions in the distressed marketplace, to be effective one must have a credible “bluff.”"
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer's point is about repeated play: an investor who never holds out cannot threaten to, so investors sometimes act against their immediate interest to keep the threat credible for future deals. The LMT era made this literal. A small set of sponsors and a small set of distressed funds meet across many capital structures, and a sponsor's history of non-pro-rata deals is a public record of its type. That is why `sponsor_repeat_lme` is a feature and why it carries a positive sign. Lenders have repeated-game reputations too, and the cooperation agreement (§13.5) is the institution they built to make their threats credible collectively when no single lender's bluff is large enough to matter.

Here is the idea that reconciles them, and it arrives formally a few paragraphs below. The co-op's bargain decides the *seats*; enterprise value decides what the seats are *worth*. In Nash-bargaining terms, the joint surplus $\Pi$ was large, because there was a fiber business someone would pay \$5.75bn for, and the company's outside option $d_{\text{sp}}$ was small, because nobody else was offering to refinance \$10bn of 2027 maturities. Large $\Pi$ and small $d_{\text{sp}}$ put the deal deep inside the deal region and hand the co-op most of the surplus. But a large $\Pi$ also means the company comes out the other side with enough value to clean up its leftovers, and the cheapest way to clean up an orphaned bond is to buy it. The excluded holder's recovery was the waterfall of §13.3 evaluated at a $V$ far above $K_1$, plus a company that wanted a simpler balance sheet. Kaz's inequality had nothing to do with it.

#### The co-op in the model

**What the document does to the game.** A cooperation agreement is a set of lenders $C$, with combined holdings $s_C = \sum_{i \in C} w_i$, who promise not to negotiate separately, require any buyer of their loans to sign on, and agree on a sunset date. The number that matters is $s_C$ against $1 - \theta$. If $s_C > 1 - \theta$, any coalition $S$ that avoids the co-op satisfies

$$
\sum_{i \in S} w_i \;\le\; 1 - s_C \;<\; \theta ,
$$

so it cannot pass the amendment. Every winning coalition must contain a co-op member, and the no-separate-deal covenant turns "contains a member" into "contains the co-op." The sponsor's knapsack from §13.4 has no feasible solution that leaves the group out. The $n$-lender game, with its invitation list and its prisoner's dilemma, collapses into bargaining between two players: the sponsor and $C$.

> **Poker Table.** "A co-op," says Ines, "is the players agreeing not to play heads-up against the house. One at a time, every one of them can be dealt out. Together they're a blocking stack, and the dealer has to deal with the stack." Her hand history: the Larkspur loan, bid 78 before the co-op headline, trades at 81 the morning after *(illustrative)*. The three points are the market repricing $\Pr(\text{excluded})$, not $V$.

> **Math, Gently.** *Nash bargaining.* Two people are splitting a pie, and each has a walk-away: what they get if the talks fail. Neither will accept less than its walk-away, so the only thing really being bargained over is the pie *left over* after both walk-aways are paid. Nash's answer is that each side gets its walk-away plus a share of the leftover, the share set by bargaining strength. Tiny number: the pie is $\Pi = 100$, the co-op's walk-away is $d_C = 20$, the sponsor's is $d_{\text{sp}} = 30$, and they are equally strong, $\eta = \tfrac12$. The leftover is $100 - 20 - 30 = 50$, so the co-op gets $20 + 25 = 45$. Now let the sponsor find a better walk-away, an outside lender who will do a deal at 40. The leftover shrinks to 40 and the co-op gets $20 + 20 = 40$. Ten dollars of sponsor walk-away cost the co-op five, which is $\eta$ per dollar. That is the whole of the comparative static below. The full version is "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section.

**Nash bargaining.** Let $\Pi$ be the joint surplus from a deal (avoided Chapter 11 costs, runway, the equity's option value from an extension), $d_C$ the co-op's disagreement payoff (its litigation value or its Chapter 11 recovery), and $d_{\text{sp}}$ the sponsor's (a third-party "deal away," or Chapter 11). With $\eta \in (0,1)$ the co-op's bargaining weight, the generalized Nash solution gives the co-op

$$
\Pi_C = d_C + \eta\,\big(\Pi - d_C - d_{\text{sp}}\big),
$$

with the symmetric split at $\eta = \tfrac12$. Two boundary conditions matter. A deal exists only if $\Pi \ge d_C + d_{\text{sp}}$; otherwise each side's outside option beats the deal, and that is when you see deals away and free-fall filings. Inside the deal region, the comparative static is linear and brutal:

$$
\frac{\partial \Pi_C}{\partial d_{\text{sp}}} = -\eta .
$$

Every dollar of credible outside financing the sponsor can line up costs the co-op $\eta$ dollars at the table. This is why deals away came back in 2025, often with six-month par calls (Wachtell, Feb 2026). The par call makes the threat cheap: if the co-op folds later, the sponsor refinances the outside money at par after six months, so the cost of *executing* the threat is roughly six months of excess spread plus fees, while $d_{\text{sp}}$ rises by the full value of the alternative. The co-op's counter is to be the cheapest financing in the room. Miller (SSRN abstract 5549919) finds that "by operating collectively, incumbent lenders outbid third-party financing offers."

**Carve-out premiums are Shapley values.** Inside the co-op, members bargain with each other, and small holders have more power than their size suggests. Treat the co-op's blocking power as a weighted majority game in which a subset $T \subseteq C$ blocks if $\sum_{i \in T} w_i > 1 - \theta$. The Shapley value of member $i$ is the probability, over random joining orders, that $i$ is the member who turns a non-blocking subset into a blocking one (the summation formula is in "The Full Derivation" at the end of this section).

> **Math, Gently.** *The Shapley value.* Line the co-op's members up at the door in a random order and let them walk in one at a time. At some moment the people in the room first hold enough to block; whoever walked in at that moment is the *pivot*. Do this for every possible order and count how often each member is the pivot. That fraction is its Shapley value: its share of the power, measured by how often the group's power depends on it. Four members can line up in $4! = 24$ orders, few enough to count on a napkin. In the example that follows, A is the pivot in 10 of them, B in 10, and C and D in 2 each, so the values are $\tfrac{10}{24}, \tfrac{10}{24}, \tfrac{2}{24}, \tfrac{2}{24}$. Size is not power; being indispensable is.

Take a lien release with $\theta = \tfrac23$, so blocking needs more than a third, and a co-op of four: A holds 25%, B 6%, C and D 3% each, 37% in all. The blocking subsets are $\{A,B,C\}$ and $\{A,B,D\}$ (34% each) and all four; $\{A,C,D\}$ reaches only 31%. A and B sit in every blocking subset, so both hold vetoes. Counting orderings gives $\phi = (\tfrac{5}{12}, \tfrac{5}{12}, \tfrac{1}{12}, \tfrac{1}{12})$. B holds a quarter of A's position and an equal share of the blocking power. When B asks for a carve-out premium (a larger new-money allocation, a bigger backstop fee), it is asking to be paid its Shapley value instead of its pro rata share, and the arithmetic is on its side. A steering committee's carve-out premium is a Shapley value with a law firm attached. The code in §13.8 checks these four numbers. Figure 13.3 plots both splits.

**Three flavours, one failure mode.** Co-ops come *open* (any holder of the class may join), *closed* (membership fixed at signing), or *tiered* (anyone may join; early signers get the premium). A closed co-op that clears $\theta$ on its own is no longer a defense against a non-pro-rata deal. It *is* one, waiting for a term sheet; Miller (2025) calls the pattern "inclusive, but unequal." This is why `coop_reported` carries the split sign in the §13.2.1 table: it lowers the probability of a deal that excludes the co-op and raises the probability of *some* LMT, because a co-op that can block can also deliver.

**The antitrust attack is an attack on the commitment device.** Wachtell reports that "antitrust challenges in connection with cooperation agreements (Optimum and Selecta) are in their early days." Issuers have also found a contractual route: Seaton (SSRN abstract 5247506) reports that Warner Bros. Discovery's 2025 bonds pioneered an "anti-cartel" provision, and that issuers' equity falls on co-op news. The second fact is the Nash solution showing up in a stock price: a co-op raises $d_C$, and the sponsor's share falls by $1 - \eta$ per dollar of it.

Mags keeps a printout of the Optimum complaint on her desk, held down by a mug, and on the Friday of the Larkspur week she hands it to Theo with the air of someone lending a horror novel. The first antitrust suit had come a month earlier: on October 28, 2025, excluded holders of Selecta, the European vending company, sued the favoured holders and the company in the Southern District of New York, alleging that a cooperation agreement had been used to collude (No. 1:25-cv-08956). Optimum was different, because the plaintiff was the borrower. On November 25, 2025, Optimum Communications, the former Altice USA, sued its co-op lenders, naming in the original complaint Apollo, Ares, GoldenTree, Loomis Sayles, Oaktree and PGIM (*Optimum Communications, Inc. v. Apollo Capital Management, L.P.*, S.D.N.Y. No. 1:25-cv-09785). Those are allegations, not findings (Hogan Lovells; Crowell, via Mondaq). Defendants moved to dismiss in February 2026. #### The Full Derivation: bargaining weights and pivots

**The Nash solution.** The generalized Nash solution maximizes $(\Pi_C - d_C)^{\eta}(\Pi_{\text{sp}} - d_{\text{sp}})^{1-\eta}$ subject to $\Pi_C + \Pi_{\text{sp}} = \Pi$, where $\eta \in (0,1)$ is the co-op's bargaining weight. Take logs and differentiate in $\Pi_C$:

$$
\frac{\eta}{\Pi_C - d_C} = \frac{1-\eta}{\Pi - \Pi_C - d_{\text{sp}}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Pi_C = d_C + \eta\,\big(\Pi - d_C - d_{\text{sp}}\big).
$$

Differentiating the solution in $d_{\text{sp}}$ gives $\partial \Pi_C / \partial d_{\text{sp}} = -\eta$, and the solution is interior only when $\Pi \ge d_C + d_{\text{sp}}$.

**The Shapley summation.** For the blocking game on the co-op $C$, member $i$'s Shapley value is

$$
\phi_i = \sum_{T \subseteq C \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|T|!\,(|C| - |T| - 1)!}{|C|!}\,\big(v(T \cup \{i\}) - v(T)\big), \qquad v(T) = \mathbf{1}\Big[\textstyle\sum_{j \in T} w_j > 1 - \theta\Big].
$$

The weight $|T|!\,(|C|-|T|-1)!/|C|!$ is the fraction of joining orders in which exactly the members of $T$ arrive before $i$, and $v(T \cup \{i\}) - v(T)$ is 1 exactly when $i$ is the pivot of such an order. For the four-member example, the 12 orders whose first three members include both A and B have their pivot in third place (A or B four times each, C or D twice each), and the other 12 orders have their pivot in fourth place (A six times, B six times), which gives $(\tfrac{10}{24}, \tfrac{10}{24}, \tfrac{2}{24}, \tfrac{2}{24}) = (\tfrac{5}{12}, \tfrac{5}{12}, \tfrac{1}{12}, \tfrac{1}{12})$.

## 13.6 Drop-Downs: Collateral Leakage Arithmetic

An uptier reorders the claims and leaves the assets where they are. A drop-down leaves the claims where they are and moves the assets. Same transfer, different mechanism, and a different payoff shape.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.244:* "if the existing bank debt has a lien on substantially all the assets, there may be little or nothing to offer."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer is describing the constraint on a classic coercive exchange: the issuer wants to offer bondholders secured paper, but the banks already hold the collateral. The drop-down is the modern answer to his problem. If nothing is unencumbered, *manufacture* unencumbered collateral by moving assets out of the restricted group through the investment and unrestricted-subsidiary baskets, then grant liens on them. Moyer's next item suggests offering "a structurally senior security"; the drop-down does both at once, because debt issued by the unrestricted subsidiary is structurally senior to the parent's lenders on the assets that moved. Where the desk departs: Moyer treats the absence of free collateral as a constraint. The 2026 desk treats it as a quantity, $C^{\text{unsub}}$, that a parser can measure and a model can rank.

**The arithmetic.** Let the collateral group be worth $V$ before the deal. The borrower moves assets worth $T$ to an unrestricted subsidiary, with $T \le C^{\text{unsub}}$ as declared at the time of the investment. (Agreements often let the borrower value the investment itself, in good faith, which makes $C^{\text{unsub}}$ a cap on the *declared* value of $T$, not its economic value. Read the valuation clause before you trust the cap.) The subsidiary borrows $D_u$ against the assets, structurally senior on them, and a fraction $\phi$ of the proceeds comes back into the collateral group as cash or debt paydown. The restricted group keeps the subsidiary's equity, worth $(T - D_u)^+$ at resolution. The existing lenders now recover

$$
R^{\text{post}}(V) = \frac{\min\!\big(V - T + \phi D_u + (T - D_u)^+,\; F\big)}{F}.
$$

Sanity checks: $T = 0$ returns $R_0(V) = \min(V,F)/F$. With $\phi = 1$ and $D_u = T$ the lenders keep every dollar but swap an asset for cash, and cash is the first thing a distressed company spends (the $\kappa$ of §13.3 again). The J.Crew trapdoor is the opposite corner, $\phi \to 0$ and $D_u \to T$: the asset leaves, is levered to the hilt, and the proceeds pay a different class. Then

$$
\Delta R(V) = R_0(V) - R^{\text{post}}(V) = \frac{\min(V, F) - \min\big((V - T)^+, F\big)}{F} =
\begin{cases}
V/F, & V \le T, \\[1ex]
T/F, & T < V \le F, \\[1ex]
(F + T - V)/F, & F < V < F + T, \\[1ex]
0, & V \ge F + T.
\end{cases}
$$

Compare the shape with §13.3. The uptier's excluded lender wrote a *digital put*: it loses everything below $K_1$ and little above. The drop-down's victim sold a *forward*: it loses $T/F$ in every state where it matters, and below $V = T$ it loses everything it would have recovered, $V/F$. And for any $\phi \ge 0$ the loss is capped by capacity, because $V - T + \phi D_u + (T - D_u)^+ \ge V - T$ implies

$$
\Delta R(V) \;\le\; \frac{T}{F} \;\le\; \frac{C^{\text{unsub}}}{F}.
$$

That bound is why `unsub_capacity_pct_ev` is a feature, and why the IP blockers exist: a blocker sets $T$ to zero for the assets it names. It also tells you how the market prices the two species. A digital put is a tail problem that the market prices slowly, as the probability of a low $V$ rises. A forward is a level problem that the market prices on the day the investment is announced. Figure 13.4 puts the two shapes on one axis.

Moving 36.5% of the shares was argued to release all of the guarantee, so the effective $T$ was the guarantee's value, not the shares'. That is leverage on the transfer, and it is why the code should compute $T$ from the release clause, not from the share count. Chewy's S-1 (Apr 29, 2019) records that "PetSmart settled existing litigation with the agent for its term loan lenders regarding these transfers in April 2019," and adds that "there can be no assurance that other PetSmart creditors may not seek to challenge these transfers." Chewy listed in June 2019 at \$22. The `chewy_blocker` (no release merely because a guarantor stops being wholly owned) exists because of those two sentences.

**When steps are judged together.** In *STG Logistics* (N.Y. Sup. Ct., Jan 3, 2026), the court viewed "amendments permitting a covenant strip, a drop-down exchange, and an intercompany loan – as a single integrated plan for purposes of analyzing 'sacred rights' (i.e., unanimous lender vote) claims," denied the motion to dismiss, and "shortly following the decision, the company filed for bankruptcy" (Wachtell, Feb 2026), on January 12, 2026. A drop-down that needs only majority votes step by step can need unanimity as a whole. Section 13.9 makes that precise.

## 13.7 Double-Dips and Pari-Plus: A Waterfall Engine That Conserves Value

"I'm told the new financing is a double-dip," says Kaz. "Is that a hygiene issue?"

"At a party," says Mags. "In a capital structure, it's a structure."

The desk's working definition: a guarantee and an intercompany loan walk into the same collateral pool and order twice. Pari-plus adds a foreign subsidiary that buys the first round.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.165:* "Grants of security interests can similarly be viewed as provisions to override the general rule that property in an estate is shared pro rata among creditors."
>
> **Annotation.** Moyer frames liens (and, on the same page, guarantees and subordination) as overrides of the pari passu default. A double-dip is an override built from *claims* instead of liens: the new lender holds two claims of equal rank against the same obligor for one dollar of credit. Nothing about it is senior. It is counted twice in the pro rata denominator, and pro rata sharing does the rest. The desk's departure is computational. Moyer's overrides can be read off a priority chart. A double-dip can only be read off a *graph* of obligors, claims and equity ownership, which is why the engine below is a fixed-point solver rather than a lookup table.

**The closed form.** A special-purpose vehicle (SPV) borrows $F_2$ from new lenders and lends the cash to OpCo under an intercompany note that ranks pari passu with OpCo's existing first lien, $F_1$. OpCo also guarantees the SPV's debt, pari passu. In a pro rata distribution of OpCo's value $V$, three claims share: the first lien ($F_1$), the guarantee ($F_2$) and the note ($F_2$), at the rate $\rho = \min\big(1, V/(F_1 + 2F_2)\big)$. The new lenders collect $\rho F_2$ on the guarantee directly and, through the SPV, the $\rho F_2$ it collects on the note, capped at what they are owed. While $2\rho < 1$, that is, $V < (F_1 + 2F_2)/2$,

$$
R^{\text{DD}} = \frac{2V}{F_1 + 2F_2}, \qquad R^{\text{1L}} = \frac{V}{F_1 + 2F_2}.
$$

The double-dipper recovers exactly twice what the first lien recovers, from a claim of the same rank. With illustrative numbers $F_1 = 1{,}000$, $F_2 = 500$ (\$mm) and $V = 800$: without the dip both would get $800/1{,}500 = 53.3$ cents; with it, the first lien gets 40 and the new money 80. The \$133mm the first lien lost is exactly the \$133mm the new money gained, and on this desk that equality is the first thing anyone confirms, before the deal terms and long before the lawyers. Figure 13.5 draws the structure and the recoveries.

![Figure 13.5: Because the new lenders hold both a guarantee from OpCo and, through the SPV, an intercompany note against it, they recover twice what the first lien does below V = \$1,000mm (80 versus 40 cents at V = \$800mm, against 53 for each with no dip) and are paid in full from there. Illustrative: the §13.7 closed form with F₁ = \$1,000mm and F₂ = \$500mm, checked against the Listing 13.1 clearing engine.](assets/figures/ch13_double_dip_structure_recovery.png)

Above the kink the arithmetic turns circular, and for once Kaz is the most comfortable person at the whiteboard. Once $V$ passes $F_2 + F_1/2$, the new lenders' two claims would collect more than they are owed, so the SPV ends up holding cash it does not need, and that excess is SPV equity. If OpCo owns the SPV, the equity flows back up into OpCo. A bigger OpCo pays more on every pro rata claim, the note included, so the SPV's excess grows, so OpCo grows again. Theo draws an arrow from OpCo to the SPV, another arrow back, and stares at the loop.

"It's a dog chasing its tail," he says.

"It's a dog that runs half as far each lap," says Kaz, and starts a column of numbers. Take $V = 1{,}200$ with the same $F_1 = 1{,}000$ and $F_2 = 500$, so OpCo's three pro rata claims add up to 2,000. Guess that OpCo pays 50 cents. Then the SPV collects 250 on its note, owes its lenders the 250 the guarantee did not cover, and has nothing left over, so OpCo has just its own 1,200, which pays 60 cents, not 50. Guess 60: the SPV collects 300, needs 200, returns 100; OpCo has 1,300 and pays 65. Guess 65: the SPV returns 150, and OpCo pays 67.5. The column reads 50, 60, 65, 67.5, 68.75, and the distance to 70 is cut in half on every line: 20, 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25.

"Seventy," says Kaz. "At seventy the SPV collects 350, needs 150, hands back 200. OpCo has 1,400, and 1,400 over 2,000 is seventy. Nothing moves."

"Back in my day," says Mags, "the circularity was resolved by whoever hung up the phone last." She studies the column. "This is better. It stays in this room that I said so."

Seventy cents is the fixed point, and it has a clean meaning. The first lien collects $0.70 \times 1{,}000 = 700$; the new lenders collect 350 on the guarantee and 150 through the SPV, which is exactly the 500 they lent. In general, above the kink,

$$
\rho = \frac{V - F_2}{F_1},
$$

so the new lenders take $F_2$ and the first lien takes everything else, as conservation demands. The regimes meet at $\rho = \tfrac12$, so recoveries are continuous and monotone in $V$. Kaz's halving is not luck, and the box says why; the algebra is in "The Full Derivation: the clearing map" at the end of this section.

> **Math, Gently.** *Fixed points, and why iteration converges.* A fixed point of a rule is an input the rule hands back unchanged: feed in 70 cents, get 70 cents out. When an answer depends on itself (a recovery that depends on equity that depends on the recovery), the honest way to solve it is Kaz's: guess, apply the rule, feed the output back in. Whether that settles down depends on one number, the rule's slope, which is how far the output moves when the input moves by one. A thermostat that closes part of the gap each cycle settles; one that overshoots by less each time still settles; one that overshoots by more each time swings the room between sauna and meat locker. Slopes between $-1$ and $1$ settle, and anything bigger in size does not. Here each lap moves the payment rate by $2F_2/(F_1 + 2F_2)$ times the last lap's move, which is below 1 whenever there is any first lien and exactly $0.5$ at $F_1 = 1{,}000$, $F_2 = 500$. Every step halves the error, so an error of a dollar per dollar of face falls below $10^{-10}$ in about 33 steps ($2^{33}$ is about 8.6 billion). A slope of 0.99 would need about 2,300 steps, which is why the engine allows 10,000 passes and raises an error, rather than quietly returning a number, when it runs out. For any number of entities the same map is Eisenberg and Noe's, next.

Real structures have more entities than one OpCo and one SPV, and the general tool is the clearing framework of Eisenberg and Noe (*Systemic Risk in Financial Systems*, Management Science, 2001), extended to priority classes. It is Kaz's whiteboard column, done for every entity at once. Each debtor has a pot: its external value, plus whatever its own intercompany claims were paid, plus whatever is left over at the subsidiaries it owns. Each debtor pays its claims rank by rank, senior first, and pays every claim within a rank the same fraction of what is still owed on it. The pots depend on the payments and the payments depend on the pots, so the engine guesses, pays, recomputes the pots, and pays again, until no payment moves by more than $10^{-10}$. The invariant is conservation: payments to outside creditors plus residual outside equity equal total external value, because every intercompany payment is one entity's outflow and another's inflow. The precise map is in "The Full Derivation: the clearing map."

**Pari-plus.** Add a non-guarantor foreign subsidiary worth $V_f$ that guarantees the new money directly. Before the deal, $V_f$ reached the first lien only as equity flowing up to OpCo. After it, the new lenders take $\min(F_2, V_f)$ first and double-dip OpCo for the deficiency, so the first lien loses the foreign value and is diluted on the rest. With $V_f = 0$ the structure collapses to the double-dip, which the code asserts. Sabre's May 2023 financing is the template: a "\$665 million new senior secured credit facility" from "lenders led by affiliates of Centerbridge Partners, L.P." to Sabre Financial Borrower, LLC, "a newly created wholly-owned subsidiary of Sabre GLBL," "secured by an intercompany loan by the Borrower to Sabre GLBL" and by "direct guarantees from, and security interest in assets of, certain foreign subsidiaries of Sabre," with proceeds funding capped tenders for three series of notes (8-K Ex. 99.1, May 25, 2023).

#### The Full Derivation: the clearing map

**The double-dip fixed point.** Above the kink, let $A$ be OpCo's distributable value including the returned SPV equity $(2\rho - 1)F_2$, with $\rho = A/(F_1 + 2F_2)$. Then

$$
A = V + \Big(\frac{2A}{F_1 + 2F_2} - 1\Big)F_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\rho = \frac{V - F_2}{F_1}.
$$

Written as a map on the payment rate, one pass sends $\rho$ to $\big(V - F_2 + 2\rho F_2\big)/(F_1 + 2F_2)$. Its slope in $\rho$ is $2F_2/(F_1 + 2F_2) < 1$, so the map is a contraction on the regime $\rho \ge \tfrac12$ and the iteration converges geometrically to the unique fixed point there. Kaz's column is this map at $V = 1{,}200$: $\rho \mapsto 0.35 + 0.5\rho$.

**The general map.** For a debtor $d$ with external value $e_d$, define its distributable value as external value plus intercompany receipts plus the residual equity $E_c$ of the subsidiaries $c$ it owns. A claim $j$ of rank $r$ owed by $d$ is then paid

$$
A_d(p) = e_d + \sum_{\ell:\ \text{cred}(\ell) = d} p_\ell + \sum_{c:\ \text{parent}(c) = d} E_c(p),
\qquad
p_j = \bar p_j \cdot \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{\big(A_d - \sum_{\text{rank}(m) < r} p_m\big)^+}{\sum_{\text{rank}(m) = r} \bar p_m}\right),
$$

where the sums in the fraction run over $d$'s own claims and $\bar p_j$ is the amount still owed on claim $j$ (face, less anything already collected from a co-obligor on the same debt). Iterate $p \mapsto \Phi(p)$ until $\|p^{(k+1)} - p^{(k)}\|_\infty < 10^{-10}$. Every payment is non-decreasing in every other payment, so $\Phi$ is monotone: iterating up from $p = 0$ climbs to the least clearing vector, and iterating down from face descends to the greatest. When every loop of ownership and claims has some outside value behind it, as in the structures of this chapter, the two meet; when they do not, the first item of "Ways This Blows Up" in §13.7.1 applies.

### 13.7.1 Case Study: Trinseo (2023–2026), the Double-Dip Meets Chapter 11

*A replay, docket cut off at Wednesday, June 24, 2026, three months before Theo's first day. The cast and the illustrative stack on the whiteboard are fictional; the filings, figures and dates come from the record cited.*

Theo meets Trinseo the way restructuring bankers meet most companies, through a disclosure statement. This one is dated Monday, May 25, 2026, which was Memorial Day, and was filed the next day as an exhibit to an 8-K, alongside the petitions; Ines hands it to him in the replay binder. On September 8, 2023, Trinseo announced "a senior secured term loan facility of \$1,077 million" borrowed by two SPVs, Trinseo Luxco Finance SPV S.à r.l. and Trinseo NA Finance SPV LLC. Then came a second chapter, the one the stylized engine knows nothing about. > **Ways This Blows Up.** *Five ways a clearing engine lies.* (1) **Circular ownership with no outside value.** If entities own each other in a loop that has no external value, Eisenberg–Noe can have many clearing vectors: iterating down from face finds the greatest, up from zero the least. Run both. If they differ, the answer is a legal question, not a numerical one. (2) **Deficiency conventions.** The engine assumes a creditor may assert full face against each obligor until paid in full, collecting no more than face in total. Some documents and jurisdictions net co-obligor recoveries first, which shrinks the dip. Make it a parameter, never a constant. (3) **Substantive consolidation.** Moyer observes that "a substantive consolidation makes the two claims pari passu" (Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis p.305). Consolidate OpCo and the SPV and the guarantee and the note become one claim; the dip disappears. (4) **The challenge list.** Moyer's checklist of attacks (p.351; list items joined) reads like an inventory of the engine's hidden parameters: "Invalidity of guarantees · Substantive consolidation · Critical creditor/vendor status · Interpretation of contractual subordination language · Violations of covenants · Transfers that might be deemed fraudulent conveyances." Each one deletes or resizes an edge in the claim graph. (5) **Claims at face plus premium.** Trinseo shows that the allowed amount of an intercompany claim is itself a litigated number. Price the dip over a range of allowed claims, not at one.

## 13.8 The Priming Risk Score

"You've told me how much I lose if I'm dealt out," says Ines. "Now tell me how often the dealer deals."

Theo's first answer is a banker's answer: it depends on the company, the sponsor, the advisors, and whether anyone has called sponsor counsel yet. Ines wants a trader's answer, a number per name per year that can be compared across the book on a Monday morning without anyone reading a credit agreement. Kaz has an answer that sounds like neither.

"Every loan has a clock on it," Kaz says. "It clicks when somebody does an LMT. We can't see the clock. We can see what the loan looked like each time it clicked."

Theo pulls up what there is to see. Roe and Rotaru hand-collected 89 coercive LMEs. The desk's universe is about 1,500 loans, the size of Noor's list in §13.2.1. The documents that produced most of the 89 were written before anyone had heard of a Serta blocker, and the documents the desk is pricing were written after.

"Eighty-nine," says Mags. "In my day we'd have called that a war story, not a dataset."

She is not wrong, and the section is built around her objection. It needs three ideas, each of them small: a hazard, to say exactly what "how often" means; a logistic link, to turn a row of features into a probability; and a prior, to stop 89 examples from saying something absurd.

**From hazard to a fixed-horizon score.** The template is the proportional-hazards model Bohn and Stein use for default (Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.233), applied to the LMT event:

$$
\lambda^{\text{LMT}}_i(t) = \lambda_0(t)\,e^{\beta^{\top} x_i(t)}, \qquad
\text{PRS}_i(t, h) = 1 - \exp\!\Big(-\int_t^{t+h} \lambda^{\text{LMT}}_i(u)\,du\Big) \in [0,1].
$$

Kaz's clock is a hazard rate, the speed of a counter that clicks at random (Chapter 3 and Chapter 6, §6.1.4, build it; Chapter 5, §5.1.5, first turned the 13-week model into one). If the counter is expected to click $H$ times over the year, the chance of no click is $e^{-H}$, so a one-year PRS of 10% is a counter running at $H = -\ln 0.9 \approx 0.105$. Each feature turns a dial on that speed, and a coefficient of 0.69 doubles it, since $e^{0.69} \approx 2$. Mags's clock from §13.1 is the same counter, attached to a company after its LMT and clicking much faster.

($\lambda^{\text{LMT}}$ carries a superscript because $\lambda_t$ is reserved for default.) With the 89, a time-varying baseline is a luxury. Fit at a fixed horizon $h = 1$ year with a logistic link on standardized features $z_i$: $\text{PRS}_i = \sigma(\beta_0 + \beta^{\top} z_i)$, $\sigma(u) = 1/(1+e^{-u})$, the squasher that turns any score into a probability (the logit of Chapter 5, §5.5.3, run backwards). For small probabilities $1 - \exp(-e^{u}) \approx e^{u} \approx \sigma(u)$, so the logistic and the Cox model agree in the region where the desk lives: a score of $-2.2$ is 10.0% through the logistic and 10.5% through the clock. At a score of 0 they part, 50% against 63%, but a loan with a coin-flip chance of being primed has bigger problems than the choice of link.

**Priors, because 89 is not a sample size.** Mags's objection has a formal answer, and it is to say in advance what the desk believed before the data arrived. The desk puts $\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(\beta^{\text{prior}}, \tau^2 I)$ on the slopes and a Beta$(a,b)$ prior on the base rate $\pi_0 = \sigma(\beta_0)$, the base-rate framing of Bohn–Stein p.447. The maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate is the coefficient vector that best balances the logistic likelihood against both priors, and two consequences carry all the intuition. The intercept's first-order condition says that the mean fitted PRS equals the observed event rate plus a correction of order $\max(a,b)/n$, so the base-rate prior nudges the level and does not overrule it. And each fitted slope is, to a good approximation, a weighted average of the prior's slope and the maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE), with the weight set by how much information the data carry relative to $1/\tau^2$. The boundary conditions are the sanity checks: as $\tau \to \infty$ the prior vanishes and the fit returns the MLE; as $\tau \to 0$ the data vanish and the fit returns the prior. The code tests both limits. The objective, the first-order condition and the shrinkage matrix are written out in "The Full Derivation: the MAP estimate and its two limits" at the end of this section.

A Beta$(a, b)$ prior behaves as if the desk had already seen $a$ events and $b$ non-events, with mean $a/(a+b)$ (Chapter 5, §5.5.2, has the shelf with two knobs; Chapter 3, §3.3.4, the batting average that shrinks). The listing's default, Beta$(2, 38)$, is a 5% base rate held with the confidence of forty observations. Against a thousand borrower-years it barely moves the estimate; against 30 borrower-years with 9 events it pulls the data's 30% down to $(2 + 9)/(40 + 30) \approx 16\%$. That pull is shrinkage, and $\tau$ is the same dial for the slopes.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.447:* "the Bayesian approach answers the question, “Given that I know that I have already observed zero defaults, what is the highest probability of default that I could expect with 95 percent certainty?”"
>
> **Annotation.** Bohn and Stein pose this for portfolios with few or no defaults. LMTs are the same problem one level up: the event is rare, its definition changed in 2020, and the population that generated the history (pre-blocker documents) is not the population you are pricing. The Beta prior turns "we have seen few events like this" into an honest upper bound rather than a zero. Where the desk departs from them: the prior must be *vintage-aware*. A base rate estimated on 2020–2022 documents overstates the risk of a 2026 agreement with a Serta blocker (Trend Watch, §13.3), so the prior's mean should come from events in documents of the same vintage, and its width should admit that there are few of them.

**What the desk prices.** The score is one factor of the expected priming loss, in fraction of par:

$$
\text{EPL}_i = \underbrace{\Pr(\text{LMT})}_{\text{PRS}_i} \cdot \Pr(\text{non-pro-rata} \mid \text{LMT}) \cdot \Pr(\text{excluded} \mid \text{NPR}) \cdot \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\big[\Delta R^{\text{excl}}\big],
$$

and the quote is approximately $P \approx P_{\text{no-LMT}} - 100\cdot\text{EPL}_i - \text{risk premium}$, in points. The last factor comes from §13.3 (or §13.6 for a drop-down), the third from the knapsack of §13.4, the second from the co-op state of §13.5. **Measure discipline:** PRS is a $\mathbb{P}$-forecast of an event. The price impact is $\mathbb{Q}$-flavoured, because the marginal holder is averse to exactly this jump, and the *Serta* damages award shows a court measuring it with a market price. Calibrating PRS to prices would bake the market's fear into the forecast and then subtract it a second time in the quote, so the desk keeps the two apart: it regresses price residuals on EPL and calls the slope the premium.

**The Monday printout.** On the Monday after the model goes live, Ines prints the top forty names by score and reads down the list the way she reads a hand history, looking for the one that does not belong. A packaging company the desk has never traded has jumped from 0.12 to 0.19 overnight *(illustrative)*. She asks why. Kaz pulls the attribution, and almost all of the move is `loan_px`, which fell three points on Friday afternoon. Nothing in the document changed. Nobody new signed a co-op. The runway did not shorten. "So the price fell," says Ines, "and the score told us the price fell. That's a mirror, not a forecast." It is her first check on any move, and the reason is circular in the plainest way. The score exists to explain the price, so a score that moves only because the price moved has added nothing, and an EPL built on it would take the same three points off the quote twice. A score that moves because the parser found a new incremental-debt basket, or because a new Rule 2019 statement landed, is telling the desk something the price may not know yet.

Ines keeps the printout anyway, because ranking is what the score does well: within one vintage of documents and one forum, a name at 0.19 is more likely to see an LMT next year than a name at 0.05, and the order survives most of the ways the model can be wrong. The level does not. Vintage moves it (the Bohn–Stein annotation above), forum flips its sign (§13.9), and the holder block arrives late and dirty (Noor's Tuesday in §13.2.1; Robertshaw's quiet month in §13.4). "Use it to choose which forty documents to read first," she tells Kaz. "Multiply 0.19 by three more factors and quote the product to the second decimal, and you've mistaken a ranking for a measurement."

> **Ways This Blows Up.** *Separation.* On the training data, `loan_px < 80` and `runway_months < 9` together nearly separate events from non-events. An unpenalized logistic responds by sending those coefficients toward infinity and reporting a PRS of 1.000 for every distressed name, which is confident and useless. The Gaussian prior keeps $\hat\beta$ finite. The covariate doing most of the separating is Theo's `runway_months` (§13.2.1); without the prior, his runway would earn an infinite coefficient.

#### The Full Derivation: the MAP estimate and its two limits

**The objective.** In $\beta_0$-space, including the Jacobian, the Beta$(a,b)$ prior on $\pi_0 = \sigma(\beta_0)$ has log density $a \ln \sigma(\beta_0) + b \ln(1 - \sigma(\beta_0))$. With the Gaussian prior on the slopes, the MAP estimate is

$$
\hat\beta = \arg\max_{\beta}\; \sum_{i=1}^{n}\big[y_i \eta_i - \ln(1 + e^{\eta_i})\big] - \frac{\lVert \beta_{1:k} - \beta^{\text{prior}} \rVert^2}{2\tau^2} + a \ln \sigma(\beta_0) + b \ln\!\big(1 - \sigma(\beta_0)\big), \qquad \eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_{1:k}^{\top} z_i .
$$

**The intercept.** The first-order condition in $\beta_0$ says exactly what the base-rate prior does: $\sum_i (y_i - \hat p_i) + a(1 - \sigma(\hat\beta_0)) - b\,\sigma(\hat\beta_0) = 0$, so the mean fitted PRS equals the observed event rate plus a correction of order $\max(a,b)/n$.

**The slopes.** A quadratic expansion of the log-likelihood around the MLE (information matrix $H$) gives the shrinkage form

$$
\hat\beta_{1:k} \approx \Lambda\,\beta^{\text{prior}} + (I - \Lambda)\,\hat\beta^{\text{MLE}}, \qquad \Lambda = (H + \tau^{-2} I)^{-1}\tau^{-2},
$$

with boundary conditions $\tau \to \infty \Rightarrow \Lambda \to 0$ (the MLE) and $\tau \to 0 \Rightarrow \Lambda \to I$ (the prior). ($\Lambda$ here is the shrinkage matrix of this subsection only; the order-arrival intensity $\Lambda(\delta)$ of Part V is a different object.) The code tests both limits.

`uptier(V, F, N, x, p)` takes an array of enterprise values and the deal terms (face $F$, new money $N$, exchange ratio $x$, participation $p$) and returns four arrays of per-dollar recoveries: the old pari passu class, first-out, second-out and excluded. `priming_loss` is the old recovery minus the excluded one. The expected transfer comes from an illustrative lognormal for $V$ (median \$1,500mm, volatility 0.45). Quadrature with breakpoints at $K_1$, $K_2$ and $F$ gives about 0.22, or 22 cents per dollar of par. A 400,000-draw Monte Carlo must land within four standard errors of it, and the closed-form partial expectation of §13.3 must match its own Monte Carlo.

`min_cost_coalition(w, c, theta, eps, coop)` solves the sponsor's knapsack. It removes co-op members from the candidates, returns `None` if the rest cannot reach $\theta + \varepsilon$ (a veto, not an empty coalition), and otherwise picks the subset that clears the bar at the lowest $\sum c_i w_i$. Up to twelve candidates it enumerates every subset (at most 4,095) and is exact. Beyond that it takes the cheapest $c_i$ first, ignoring holding size, which is a heuristic only. In the demo, funds are cheap, CLOs dear and the litigator dearest: at $\theta = 0.5$ the chosen coalition (five holders, 54% of the class) leaves the litigator out, and a 36% co-op against $\theta = 2/3$ returns `None`. `shapley(w, quota)` enumerates every ordering and credits whoever tips the running total past the quota, reproducing the (5/12, 5/12, 1/12, 1/12) of §13.5. Because it visits $n!$ orderings, it suits a steering committee of eight, not a class of eighty.

One trap is silent. At $p = 1$ nobody is excluded, so `uptier` returns `nan` for the excluded recovery by design, and `priming_loss`, with any average taken over it, comes back `nan` without raising anything.

The listing runs on synthetic borrower-years so the fitter can be graded against known coefficients. `simulate` draws 400 rows of five features (blocker, unsub capacity, loan price, runway, repeat sponsor), `BETA_TRUE` generates the events, and `brentq` solves the intercept so that about 15% of rows come out positive. `BETA_PRIOR` is the "published" prior: right signs, smaller magnitudes. `runway_months` is the §13.2.1 definition, capped at 120 months when burn is zero or negative.

`fit_map(Z, y, prior, tau, a, b)` returns the intercept and slopes on standardized features. It maximizes the posterior of §13.8: the logistic likelihood, a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\text{prior}, \tau^2)$ on each slope, and a Beta$(a, b)$ on $\sigma(\beta_0)$, with defaults $a = 2$, $b = 38$, a 5% base rate worth forty pseudo-observations. Newton steps use an analytic gradient and Hessian, and each step is halved until it stops raising the negative log posterior. Setting `tau=np.inf` with `a=b=0` gives the plain MLE, which is how the listing proves both limits of the shrinkage formula: a huge `tau` reproduces the MLE, a tiny one returns the prior. The other checks: the intercept first-order condition holds to 1e-6; the mean fitted PRS stays within 1.5 points of the observed rate; a 200-resample bootstrap band covers the estimate; a blocker lowers the score and unsub capacity raises it; and on a fresh holdout the fit beats the constant base rate on Brier score.

`prs` is the one scoring entry point: it standardizes a raw feature row with the training means and standard deviations, then applies the logistic. `expected_priming_loss` multiplies the four factors of §13.8 and asserts each lies in $[0, 1]$. The last lines score the illustrative Larkspur row with 7 months of runway, a harsher burn than the eleven months Theo forecast in §13.2.1, using the excluded share from block (b), a placeholder 0.5 for the non-pro-rata probability, and the 0.22 expected transfer. On the seeded run: 70 events, PRS about 0.90, EPL about 4.6 points. The 0.90 says more about synthetic coefficients than about any real borrower.

Its one silent failure is `iters=100`: the loop returns after 100 passes without saying whether it converged. On this synthetic data that never matters. On real, nearly separated data with `tau=np.inf` it would, and the coefficients would be huge with no warning. The prior is the convergence guarantee, and a fit across document vintages needs a vintage covariate (the Bohn–Stein annotation above).

## 13.9 Autopsies: The Court Is a Random Variable

The $q$ in the litigation option of §13.4 has been the most volatile parameter on the desk since 2023. Two features carry it: the clause and the doctrine.

#### The Incora board, 28 March 2022

*A replay. The cast is fictional and the dialogue is theirs; the steps, holdings and rulings come from the court opinions and the law-firm accounts cited.*

Mags tells the Incora story the way a chess columnist annotates a famous game: every move legal, and the position lost long before anyone resigned. Theo has the district court's opinion open. Kaz has the whiteboard.

The board, as of early 2022. Wesco Aircraft, owned by Platinum Equity, had raised over \$2bn in 2020 to finance Platinum's acquisition of Wesco and its combination with Pattonair: \$650mm of 8.5% secured notes due 2024, \$900mm of 9% secured notes due 2026, and \$525mm of 13.125% unsecured notes due 2027. Two clauses in the secured indentures matter. A majority could consent to issuing more notes, pari passu. Releasing substantially all the collateral, or anything with "the effect of" doing so, needed two-thirds. By late 2021 the company needed money badly enough that its UK auditors wanted \$150–200mm injected or the UK entity faced being struck off. In December 2021 PIMCO and Silver Point, the two largest holders of the 2024 and 2026 notes, proposed a rescue. In February 2022 a minority group, which the district court later identified as led by BlackRock and JPMorgan, announced that it held more than a third of the 2026 notes. That was a blocking position against any two-thirds vote, and it set off the race for the marginal bond described in §13.4. The minority offered about \$147.5mm of financing, then \$173mm. The board chose the majority's \$250mm.

"So the minority has a third," says Theo. "The majority can't release the liens."

"Can't release them *today*," says Mags. "Watch."

Then came March 28, 2022, four steps on one day.

*Step one.* Holders supporting the deal owned more than two-thirds of the 2024 notes, more than 59% of the 2026 notes and more than half of the 2027s. A majority is enough to add pari passu notes, so they signed the Third Supplemental Indentures, which permitted \$250mm of additional 2026 notes. Nobody's lien moved. Kaz writes $v_1 = 0.59 \ge \theta_1 = 0.5$ and a check mark.

*Step two.* The \$250mm was wired, and the new 2026 notes were issued to the majority group. Kaz does the arithmetic out loud. The class used to be \$900mm, of which the group held at least 59%, about \$531mm. Now it is \$1,150mm, of which the group holds about \$781mm, or 67.9%: two-thirds, with a point to spare. The minority's \$300mm-plus, a third of the old class, is about 26% of the new one. Its block has evaporated without a single minority bond changing hands.

"The least they could have walked in with," says Kaz, "is about 57.4% of the old class. They had 59. The whole lawsuit is in that point and a half."

*Step three.* With two-thirds in hand, the group signed the Fourth Supplemental Indentures, which released the liens securing the 2024 and 2026 notes.

Kaz, reading over Theo's shoulder: "The fourth supplemental indenture. Is that the one with the new teeth?"

"It's the one that pulled them," says Mags.

*Step four.* The participants exchanged their 2024 and 2026 notes into new super-senior first-lien notes, which press accounts put at about \$1.27bn, and participating 2027 holders exchanged into 1.25-lien notes. The non-participants woke up holding unsecured paper behind, by the same accounts, more than \$1.7bn of new debt. Milbank had given an opinion that the steps complied with the indentures.

Kaz steps back from the board. Every step has a check mark: 0.59 against a half for the first, a wire for the second, 0.679 against two-thirds for the third, a voluntary exchange for the fourth. "Each move is legal."

"Each move," says Mags. "Now look at the position before the first move and ask whether they could have done the whole thing in one go."

They could not. Measured on the morning of March 28, the group held about 59% of the 2026s against a two-thirds bar, and that gap is the whole of the litigation. The excluded holders sued in state court in October 2022. Wesco filed for Chapter 11 on June 1, 2023, with an adversary proceeding the same day. At closings in June 2024, Judge Isgur said he believed "with a high degree of certainty" that the \$250mm was unauthorized, two weeks before he ruled. The plan on file before the ruling had given the participants 96.5% of the equity; at emergence on January 31, 2025, the secured noteholders took 98.4% plus \$420mm of take-back notes. Then the district court took the steps one at a time, as Kaz had, and found each one compliant.

"So who was right?" says Theo. "The bankruptcy judge or the district judge?"

"For the desk that's the wrong question," says Ines. "The right one is what the clauses made the odds. The two-thirds bar was in the document. The pari passu basket was in the document. Anyone who read both in 2021 could see that a 57.4% holder could manufacture a two-thirds holder in a day, and that some court would one day have to choose between reading steps and reading plans. That choice is $q$. Both groups were pricing it in February, when they paid up toward par for the marginal 2026 bond."

#### Steps, plans and the scoreboard

**Steps versus plans.** Write an LMT as a composition of document moves $\mathcal{D}_0 \to \mathcal{D}_1 \to \cdots \to \mathcal{D}_m$, where step $k$ needs holders with share $v_k(\mathcal{D}_{k-1})$ of the class to clear $\theta_k$. A step-by-step court asks whether $v_k(\mathcal{D}_{k-1}) \ge \theta_k$ for every $k$. An integrated court treats the composite as one move and measures support before anything happened, $v(\mathcal{D}_0) \ge \max_k \theta_k$, plus unanimity for any sacred right the composite touches. The gap $g = \max_k \theta_k - v(\mathcal{D}_0)$ is the feature `integration_gap`. When $g > 0$ the deal works only in a step-by-step forum, and $q$ becomes a bet on doctrine. On the board's numbers Wesco/Incora had $v(\mathcal{D}_0) \approx 0.59$ of the 2026s against $\theta = \tfrac23$, so $g \approx 0.08$: each step cleared its own bar, and the composite did not. *STG Logistics* (§13.6) is the same test applied to a drop-down.

#### Theo's sheet, 4:50 p.m.

*Tuesday, 4:50 p.m. The same Tuesday.*

At 7:51 that morning Ines made the call Theo could not yet make. "Tell them 75, for five." The client did not hit it. At 7:58 sales reported, in the voice sales reserves for a lost trade, that the client had done all fifteen million away at 78. Loans do not print on TRACE, so the desk learned the level the way it learns most things about loans: from a salesperson who heard it from a client who wanted them to know.

Theo spent the day doing what this chapter just did. At 4:50 he stands at Ines's desk with one sheet of paper, which is a regression from the legal pad, which was a regression from the napkin.

"Four factors," he says. "The PRS first. With this morning's headline coded as a group organizing, the model says 0.35 for an LMT inside a year. I checked what moved it, the way you do. The price falling from 84 to 78 accounts for about four hundredths of that. The headline and the document account for the rest, so it isn't a mirror."

"What's in the document?"

"An agreement signed in early 2022, before the blockers became the norm later that year. No Serta blocker. A J.Crew blocker, no Chewy blocker. And 9.05(g) says 'open market purchase,' not bare 'purchase.' After the Fifth Circuit, that wording makes a straight Serta-style exchange harder to defend, but it does nothing against an amend-extend-and-exchange. So the second factor, the chance the deal is non-pro-rata given that there is a deal, is a half. Before December 2024 I'd have said seven in ten."

"Third."

"The knapsack. The group says it holds 51.3%, and the action it needs is a bare majority, so the bag is already full. Anyone who arrives today with fifteen million is outside it by construction, and fifteen million is too small to be worth a seat even if the bag weren't full: a one-percent holder adds almost nothing toward $\theta$ and costs a chair at the table. The only way in is a sponsor who opens the deal to everyone to keep the litigators quiet, which happens more often since *Serta* than it used to. Pr(excluded) is 0.85."

"Fourth."

"The Serta-shaped put. Twenty-two cents of expected transfer, from the illustrative lognormal in the §13.8 listing, because I don't have Larkspur's terms yet. It's the factor I trust least." He writes the product, $0.35 \times 0.5 \times 0.85 \times 0.22 \approx 0.033$, about 3.3 points.

"That's for a random holder," says Ines. "Who sold at 7:41?"

"That's the part I couldn't do this morning." Theo turns the sheet over. "If the seller was in seat two, they knew they were out and the deal was close. Then the PRS is more like 0.8, the non-pro-rata chance 0.8, and they're excluded for certain: $0.8 \times 0.8 \times 1 \times 0.22$, about 14 points. If it was seat three, the CLO, it's the random-holder number, 3.3. Seat one can't sell. Given that somebody offered at 78 within a minute of the headline, I'd put four in ten on seat two. That's about 7.6 points of expected priming loss. Without any LMT risk, Larkspur would trade around 86 *(illustrative, from comparable names)*, and our residual regression says the market charges about a third on top of EPL for bearing the jump, call it two and a half points. Eighty-six, less seven and a half, less two and a half: 76. And five million, not fifteen. If they hit 76 in four seconds, I've learned which seat they had, and I paid for the lesson on a third of the size."

"In my day," says Mags, "we'd have bid seventy, hung up, and called it judgment."

"It was judgment," says Ines. "It just didn't have units."

Kaz leans over from the next desk. "And my question from this morning? Why 'excluded' means 25 in one case and 99 in another?"

"Because 'excluded' was never the variable," says Theo. "The variable is where $V$ lands against the strike when the story ends, and who gets to say the story has ended: a court, a sale, or the next filing. Serta ended in a courtroom with $V$ below $K_1$. Lumen ended with an asset sale big enough to put $V$ above everybody's strike, and so did Boardriders. Same word, different $V$, different referee."

Ines pulls up the hand history for a trade she did not do. Larkspur is quoted 74–76 at the close *(illustrative)*. The other dealer's 78 marks out at about −3, roughly \$450,000 on fifteen million. Her 75 for five, had it traded, would be flat. Theo's 76 for five would be down about \$50,000, and the desk would know something it does not know now. "The markout says the seller had a reason," she says. "Nobody followed with another offer at 78, and the bid leaked all day. Seat two, or a very nervous seat three. Write it down."

She does not say whether 76 was right, because the hand is not over. Three days later the lenders who had not been called would find each other and organize, the loan would trade at 81 (§13.5), and the same 78 would mark out at +3. Ines keeps markouts at the close, at a day and at a week for exactly this reason, and she keeps all three.

Theo writes the card that goes on his monitor.

- **An LMT is the difference of two waterfalls.** In low states it is zero-sum among creditors; the liquidity story lives in high states. Compute $\Delta_j(V)$ before you read the press release.
- **Know your payoff shape.** Excluded from an uptier: short a soft digital put struck at $K_1$. Victim of a drop-down: short a forward capped at $C^{\text{unsub}}/F$. Diluted by a double-dip: someone is counted twice in your denominator.
- **The sponsor solves a knapsack** and leaves out the constrained, the fragmented and the litigious. Model your invitation probability, not your participation decision.
- **A co-op above $1 - \theta$ turns an auction into a bargain.** Deal-away capacity costs the co-op $\eta$ per dollar; carve-out premiums are Shapley values. (Cooperation agreements signed in this book so far: 2. Antitrust complaints about them: 2, and counting.)
- **One word can be the whole loss.** The court is a random variable with a clause, a forum, a doctrine and a vintage.
- **PRS is $\mathbb{P}$; the price is $\mathbb{Q}$.** Never calibrate the score to prices. The prior keeps $\hat\beta$ finite, and Theo's runway is the covariate that separates.
- **The LMT is not the terminal state.** Only 22% of coercive-LME firms avoided both re-default and bankruptcy within two years. Price the next event.

### The Spec You Hand the Robot

Build `lmt_engine.py` (numpy/scipy; `rng = np.random.default_rng(13)`; runs top to bottom; values in \$mm, recoveries in fraction of par, runway in months, PRS at a 12-month horizon). **Schemas:** `entities` rows are `id` (str), `value` (\$mm, $\ge 0$) and `parent` (entity id or `None`); `claims` rows are `id`, `creditor` (`"ext"` or an entity id), `debtor`, `face` (\$mm, $>0$), `rank` (1 = most senior) and `reduce_by` (ids of co-obligor claims on the same debt); `features` has one row per borrower-year with the §13.2.1 columns, `buyback_exception` one-hot, and `y` = 1 if an LMT closed within 12 months. (1) `waterfall_clearing(entities, claims)`: priority ranks, pro rata within rank, intercompany receipts and subsidiary equity routed to parents, `reduce_by` for co-obligor claims on one debt, all value residual at an entity with no claims; stop at $\lVert \Delta p \rVert_\infty < 10^{-10}$, raise a convergence error after 10,000 iterations rather than return silently; assert conservation to $10^{-8}$, no payment above face, monotonicity in $V$, the double-dip closed form, pari-plus collapse at $V_f = 0$. (2) `uptier`, `priming_loss`: guard $N = 0$ and $p = 1$; assert conservation, zero loss for $V \ge F + N$, full loss for $V \le K_1$; expected loss by quadrature checked against Monte Carlo and the lognormal partial expectation. (3) `min_cost_coalition`: brute force to 12 holders, greedy beyond, `None` (never an empty coalition) when a co-op exceeds $1 - \theta$; `shapley` by enumeration. (4) `runway_months`, capped at 120 when burn $\le 0$; `fit_map`: Newton with step-halving, `logaddexp` in the likelihood, an overflow-safe logistic via `tanh`, Gaussian slope prior, Beta base-rate prior, and the prior required under quasi-separation (never `tau=inf` in production); assert the intercept first-order condition, both $\tau$ limits, the bootstrap band, signs, and a holdout Brier score below the base rate. (5) `expected_priming_loss` $=$ PRS $\times$ P(NPR $\mid$ LMT) $\times$ P(excluded $\mid$ NPR) $\times$ $\mathbb{E}[\Delta R]$, inputs in $[0,1]$. Never fit PRS to prices.
