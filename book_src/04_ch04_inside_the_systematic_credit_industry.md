# Chapter 4: Inside the Systematic Credit Industry

At 7:40 a.m. Ines Okafor puts six documents on Theo's desk in the order a dealer stacks a deck: Man Group's two papers on quantitative credit, BlackRock's note on systematic high yield, Nuveen's multi-asset credit framework, a long Barclays book, and two thin pages from Blackstone. "Tell me which of these desks we are," she says, "and which one is going to eat us."

She does not wait for an answer. Before eight she rarely does. The pile lands on the corner of the desk next to the USB stick, which still holds a 13-week cash flow model for a company that came out of Chapter 11 two years ago and no longer needs anyone's help.

Theo has read a thousand pitch books. He knows the genre. One slide shows the process as a funnel. One shows risk as a dial. One says "capacity" next to a number with no derivation and an asterisk. He opens the first paper and looks for the funnel.

There is no funnel on the first page, or the fifth. What he finds on page 19 of Man's 2022 paper, under a heading about transaction-cost modelling, is a sentence that stops his coffee halfway to his mouth. Corporate bond bid and ask prices, it says, "are indicated by dealer runs that are not firm and actionable," and so "trades that appear profitable in practice are unprofitable once transaction costs are factored in" (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.19`). A line later the paper notes, in the tone of someone who has been burned, that the market has a phrase for this: "if filled" trades.

Theo reads the paragraph twice. In restructuring he used the word "price" several thousand times a year. The firsts were at 88. The seconds were at 40. The ad hoc group bought in at 71. Every one of those numbers came off a screen, a dealer run or a call to a desk, and every one of them was, he now sees, a dealer's opinion about where it might be willing to deal, in some size, with somebody, for a few minutes. In RSSG that was fine. A recovery analysis needs a reference point, not an execution, and nobody at PJT ever had to buy \$5mm of the seconds at 40 before lunch. Here the paper is telling him that the price on the screen is a menu, and that the business is what the kitchen will actually serve you.

He writes on a sticky note, *"price" = as of when, in what size, from whom?*, and presses it to the bottom of his monitor.

The Blackstone document is two pages long, which in his experience means either a very confident firm or a very careful lawyer. It calls its platform "the largest pure-play quantitative credit operation in the world, with over \$40 billion in assets under management" (`Blackstone_Systematic_Credit p.1`). Out of habit Theo looks for the date. There isn't one. In a fairness opinion an undated number is a number you cannot use, and a second-year associate would have sent it back before it reached the partner. He circles it and writes "as of?" in the margin, and the circle stays with it through this chapter, because until someone finds its as-of date the figure is a claim rather than a data point. On the second page Blackstone gives him the number he will hear all day in different words. Systematic strategies manage "roughly 20% of global equities" and "less than 2% of the fixed-income universe" (`Blackstone_Systematic_Credit p.2`). Twenty against two. Either credit is twenty years behind, or there is something about bonds that the equity machine cannot digest. He suspects both, and that the second half is the interesting one.

Kaz rolls over with a laptop. "I already built the framework. Alpha model, risk model, optimizer. Backtested Sharpe of 3.1."

"Net of what?"

"Transaction costs are a parameter," Kaz says. "I set it to zero so it wouldn't bias the results."

Theo turns the laptop toward himself. The equity curve climbs from the lower left to the upper right with the serenity of a line drawn with a ruler: twenty-two years of monthly returns from a value sleeve, a momentum sleeve and a quality sleeve, blended by an optimizer that Kaz wrote over a long weekend and is visibly proud of.

"Zero is a bias," Theo says.

"Zero is a *neutral* bias," Kaz says, and then, because Kaz is honest when cornered, makes the case properly. The signals are slow; the value sleeve holds a bond for most of a year, and costs scale with turnover. The academic papers report factor premia gross, so a net number set against a gross literature would compare apples with oranges. Put in a cost assumption and you smuggle in an opinion; better to show the clean signal and let the execution people argue about the haircut later. "And look at the drawdowns." Kaz scrolls. "2008 is a dip. 2020 is a dip. The thing *recovers*. Equity value spent most of the 2010s underwater. The cross-section of credit is just less picked over. Isn't that the whole thesis of the Man paper? Early movers."

It is a good speech. It also has the shape, Theo notices, of the speeches he used to hear from management teams on the second day of a lender meeting: every sentence defensible on its own, and the conclusion two floors above the evidence.

Ines has come back for her pen. She reads the screen over Kaz's shoulder for about four seconds.

"Show me the hand history," she says.

"It's a backtest. There are no hands."

"Then how many of those fills did you get, and from whom?" She taps the 2008 dip with the pen. "In November 2008 your model bought single-B bonds at these prices. Who sold them to you? Which dealer, what size, how many dealers in competition? Because in November 2008 I was sitting across from people like you, and I can promise you the bid I showed and the price on that screen were not the same number."

Kaz opens a second window, scrolls, and goes quiet in a way Theo will learn to recognize. "The prices are month-end composites," Kaz says at last. "Vendor-evaluated. Bid side, where available."

"Evaluated by whom, from what?"

"A matrix. Comparable bonds, dealer quotes, some TRACE."

"Some," Ines says. "So your backtest bought bonds at the average of what dealers said they might do, in a month when most of them weren't picking up the phone." She is not unkind about it. She has the tone of a poker player explaining to a strong amateur that the hand was lost three streets ago. "It's a fine number. Nobody could have traded it. Your 3.1 is the Sharpe ratio of a market where nobody ever had to ask."

Mags walks past with a coffee and doesn't slow down. "Four white papers, one memo. Buy cheap, don't get defaulted on, and for God's sake measure your costs. Back in my day we called that a desk, not a framework."

Then she does slow down, because she has heard Ines say "evaluated," and on evaluated prices Mags has views.

"Autumn of ninety-eight," she says, to no one in particular. "Everyone's long and wrong after the Russian default. I've got three million of a telecom bond I want out of. I call a dealer. He says ninety-four, ninety-five, very friendly, how's the family. I say let me check with my PM. I call back eleven minutes later and he's ninety-one bid and he doesn't ask about the family. In eleven minutes he's worked out three things. I'm a seller. I called him first, so I don't have a better bid. And if I'm selling, maybe I know something he doesn't." She takes a sip. "The first price was the menu. The second was the meal. Every price in your vendor file is the first call." *(Mags's story is fiction; the dealer is nobody in particular.)*

"That's adverse selection," Kaz says, pleased to have a name for it.

"That's Tuesday," says Mags, and leaves.

She is right about the memo. Every paper on the pile wants cheap bonds, improving bonds, bonds that won't default, and bonds that pay you to wait. Theo can read that in twenty minutes. What takes him the morning is that the papers *disagree* about nearly everything else. They disagree about what data you are allowed to know and when you knew it, how a forecast becomes a position, how the position gets filled, how big the book can grow before the book *is* the market, and which risks you hedge and which you are paid to hold. Kaz's Sharpe of 3.1 lives entirely in the gap between the memo and the plumbing.

By eleven the four people on the desk have each, without quite meaning to, staked out a position that the rest of this chapter will test. Kaz thinks the product is the math: a better alpha model and a cleaner optimizer, with costs as an engineering detail for some Friday afternoon. Ines thinks the product is the fills. A signal you cannot get executed is a rumour, and the edge lives in knowing which dealer will show you a real price, in size, on a bad day. Mags thinks neither matters if you are wrong about who defaults, and that the people who forget it relearn it every seven years or so at their own expense. Theo does not have a position yet. He has a sticky note that says "as of when?", and the feeling, familiar from years of pitch books, that when six firms say the same thing on the front page the money is somewhere around page 19.

So he reads the rest of the pile the way he used to read a data room, looking for where each document stops talking. BlackRock's note is about defence: how a systematic high-yield book loses less than the index in the bad months without simply owning less risk. Nuveen's framework is not choosing bonds at all; it is choosing among whole sectors (high yield, loans, emerging-market debt, private credit) with something that looks suspiciously like a 13-week model scaled up to the size of an asset class. The Barclays book runs to several hundred pages and spends many of them on the things the white papers leave to footnotes: index rules, the cost of buying a new issue the day it enters the index, turnover, capacity. The second Man paper, from 2023, is about almost nothing except how the firm trades. Nobody in the pile disagrees about what a cheap bond is. They disagree about everything that happens after you find one.

By lunch Theo has a sheet of paper with six columns and a lot of blank cells. He looks at the blanks the way he used to look at a capital structure with a hole in the guarantor coverage. The hole is where the money is.

The columns do not have headers yet; writing them is the work of §4.3. The rows are the six documents, plus Kaz's laptop, which he adds at the bottom in pencil. Above the grid, where a deal team would write the client's name, Theo writes the question that has been forming since page 19: *if everyone has the same menu, what is the kitchen?*

The question this chapter answers: **on which axes do systematic credit shops actually differ, and where on that map is there room for a desk that knows what the credit agreement says?**

By the end of the chapter Theo can do six things he could not do at 7:40, and so can you. He can name the five businesses that share the label "systematic credit" and say which constraint binds each one. He can explain, with dated numbers, why credit went systematic about twenty years after equities, and why it is still under 2% of the fixed-income universe. He can lay any manager's pitch book on a six-axis map (alpha families, data stack, portfolio construction, execution, capacity, risk) and find the cells it hides. He can show that every "value" signal in the industry is a gap between a risk-neutral and a physical loss rate. He can compute effective breadth under issuer clustering, break-even turnover, and capacity under square-root impact, and explain why at the manager's preferred size the client keeps one-third of the edge. And he can specify a seeded engine that scores any row of the map, and point to the empty cells where an ex-RSSG brain is scarce.

## 4.1 The Five Businesses Called "Systematic Credit"

In restructuring you learn early that "the creditors" is not a party. A first-lien term lender, a second-lien noteholder, a trade creditor and a CDS protection buyer all hold a claim on the same company, and they want four different things. "Systematic credit" works the same way. The phrase covers at least five businesses that share a vocabulary (factors, optimizers, transaction-cost models) and almost nothing else that matters: not the benchmark, the leverage, the client, or the constraint that decides whether they make money.

Theo has met all five, though never under these names. On one of his early restructurings, a specialty retailer with \$1.2bn of unsecured notes and a borrowing base that shrank every time the inventory appraiser visited *(a fictional composite; the company and its numbers are invented)*, they turned up roughly in the order a filing calls them.

The first was an investment-grade fund that sold the notes long before anyone at PJT had heard of the company. When the second of the three rating agencies cut the retailer to junk, the bonds left the index at the next month-end, and the fund's index rules sold them for it. Every dealer on the Street could read the same rules, so the bonds were cheap by the time the selling arrived. That is row (iv) of the table below, the rules-based fund: its virtue is that you can predict what it will do, and its cost is that so can everyone else.

The second was a high-yield manager who owned the notes because they were in *her* index. She held them through the missed coupon and into the filing, not out of conviction but because selling a 0.3% index weight at 30 cents would have locked in a loss her benchmark had not yet taken. Her questions on the creditor call were about timing, never value. When would the index drop the name, and would the new paper be index-eligible? She was row (i). The constraint that decided her trades was never the signal. It was the tracking error a sale would have cost her.

The third was a hedge fund that had been short the notes since they were at 80, borrowing them in repo, and was in no hurry to cover. It wanted a quick filing and a low recovery. Its analyst asked the sharpest questions on the call, most of them about whether the notes would be deliverable into the CDS auction. Row (ii): levered, free of any benchmark, and kept awake at night by the borrow rate and by the suspicion that five other funds were short the same bond.

The fourth was the dealer who made the market in all of it. It bought from the index fund, lent the short its bonds, showed the long-only manager a bid she declined, and quoted the new notes after emergence. The dealer was nearly indifferent to whether the company survived. It cared whether its inventory turned over before the people it traded with turned out to know more than it did. Row (v).

The fifth never saw the name. A multi-asset credit allocator had cut high yield from 30% to 20% of its book three months before the filing, in favour of loans, because its sector model said high-yield spreads were too tight for the default rate it expected. The retailer was one bond among two thousand that left the portfolio in a single portfolio trade. Row (iii): it chooses sectors, and an individual credit is noise to it.

Five people, one capital structure, five ideas of what a good month looked like. The industry now calls the machine-run version of each of them "systematic credit."

Acadian, which built a systematic credit business between 2022 and 2024, put it bluntly in June 2026: the term has become an "ambiguous catch-all label." Acadian's own narrow definition is "fundamentally driven, benchmark-relative corporate bond strategies that apply purely quantitative methods" (Acadian, *Credit's Systematic Shift*, June 2026). That is one of the five businesses below. It is a good definition and a bad census.

Theo reads the table the way he once read the retailer's creditor matrix, and two things in it change how he reads every pitch book that follows.

First, the five businesses face different binding constraints, so they optimize different things and will tell you different stories about the same factor. A long-only manager running against the ICE BofA High Yield Index does not care much about whether value works as a standalone long/short spread. It cares whether value tilts can be carried at a 2% tracking error without paying away the edge in bid–offer. A long/short fund at four times leverage does not care about a benchmark at all. It cares whether it can borrow the bond it wants to short, and whether the six other funds running the same value signal will try to sell at the same moment. Same factor, different businesses, different answers.

Second, the column on the far right is thin for a reason. The industry is young, much of it sits inside larger firms that do not split out systematic assets, and the multi-strategy pods disclose almost nothing. Where the table names no pod it means it: we will not invent a pod's AUM to make the table look full.

Ines will not let "disclose almost nothing" be the last word on row (ii). *(A desk scene. The pod PM Ines describes is Dov Adler of Chapter 1-B, a fictional composite, and his positions are illustrative; the fee schedule is a consultant's published worked example, and the hires are real and dated.)*

"You can't see their books," she says, pulling the table toward her. "Nobody can. You can see who they hire and how they pay, and between the two you can see what they're buying." The hiring is public. In June 2025 Millennium added a credit-index trader from Brevan Howard and a corporate-credit partner from Laurion, following its earlier hire of LMR's former US head of credit (Hedgeweek, 2 June 2025). In August 2026 Balyasny hired a BlackRock investment-grade co-head to run a team that would "trade both long and short positions in US high-grade corporate bonds and pursue relative-value opportunities" (Hedgeweek, 20 August 2026). Ines taps the second one. "Long and short, relative value. That's the whole product description, and it came out in a press release about a hire."

The pay is where it gets interesting. On the back of the table she writes out the inputs of the worked example in Mercer's 2023 due-diligence paper on multi-manager platforms: a 1% management fee; 3% a year of operations, talent and retention costs passed through to the investors; 20% of each pod's own profit to its PM; 20% of what is left to the fund. Then she runs a good year through them, in the order Mercer's table does. Twenty points of gross. The PMs take 20% of the gross after the 3 points of pass-through, which is 3.4. The fixed charges take 4. The fund takes 20% of the remainder, $0.2 \times (20 - 4 - 3.4) = 2.52$. The client keeps 10.08. Mercer's own sentence is the one Theo copies down: "for an investor to achieve a 10% net return, a fund must generate a gross return of approximately 20% or more" (Mercer, *Multi-Manager Platforms: A due diligence perspective*, May 2023). Then she runs a mediocre year. Six points of gross leaves the client about 1.1, because the 4 points of fixed charges do not care how the year went. Nor is the 3 points a worst case: Cambridge Associates found pass-through charges running from 0.87% to 12.99% across the funds it sampled, with a mean of 5.93% (10 October 2025).

"Half the gross is gone before the client sees a dollar," Theo says.

"Which tells you what the gross has to be made of. Spread beta costs the client somewhere between a few basis points and a few tens of basis points through an index product or an ETF. Nobody pays half their gross for that. So the platform pays for the residual, and it treats any beta in a pod as something the PM brought in by accident."

Mags, reading over Theo's shoulder, says that back in her day a hedge fund charged two and twenty and you knew exactly whom to hate. Now, apparently, you hate forty people, each for their own reasons.

Ines sketches the trade the waterfall implies, using a pod PM her desk quotes most weeks. The reader met him in Chapter 1-B: Dov Adler, long \$60mm of Kestrel Roofing's 6s of 2030, short \$39mm of Halvorsen's 7.25s of 2029, two single-B building-products credits sized so their legs carry the same duration times spread. The hedge removes the high-yield market, most of the housing cycle the two share, and most of the rate risk. What remains is Kestrel's documents and maturity wall against Halvorsen's, the only part of the P&L a platform will pay 20% of. What leaks is what a pitch book leaves out: the DTS ratio drifts as the view pays, an exchange offer can gap the short up ten points, and the lender can recall the bonds in the week a vote makes them valuable. Holding it costs money every day: the long earns its coupon, the short pays Halvorsen's coupon to whoever lent the bonds plus a borrow fee that reprices when everyone wants the same short, and the long has to be financed. Chapter 1-B's box carries those lines at Dov's sizes.

Then Ines adds the leak that only exists at the level of the platform, and it is the one Theo writes into row (ii)'s constraint column. Pay is per pod; risk is per fund. If one pod makes \$10 and the pod beside it loses \$10, the first PM is paid \$2 and the investor is down \$2 on a flat year, where a classic fund would have paid nobody. And if forty pods each leave a little spread beta in the same direction, each of them is "basically hedged" and the fund is long the market. That is why Dov's platform x-rays every book overnight for beta it did not mean to hold, and why "uncorrelated return" in row (ii) is the product rather than a slogan.

Kaz asks what limits the size. Not the platform's assets, Ines says: the corner. A pair is as big as the bonds you can borrow on the short side and the depth you can buy on the long side. Dov's short is \$39mm of Halvorsen on an allocation of \$400mm, inside a platform that runs tens of billions *(illustrative)*. Theo writes three lines in the empty examples cell of row (ii): *paid on the residual; about 20 gross for 10 net; the short is the constraint.* The mechanics of the pair live in Chapter 8, the seat in Chapter 4-C §4C.3, and the pay, netting and capacity arithmetic in Chapter 28, which proves what Ines has just asserted.

A sixth group hangs over all five: the "quant-assisted" discretionary manager. Barclays QPS estimates that funds using quantitative *screening* in IG and HY hold between \$90bn and \$140bn (Mateer et al., Barclays QPS & Credit Research, 2024, cited by Impax, 19 September 2025). That is several times the dedicated systematic pool. Most of the industry's quant money is not running a model. It is running a model *past* an analyst. Theo files that number next to the USB stick. It is the strongest evidence in this chapter that an RSSG-trained analyst is not obsolete. They are the other half of the most common product.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The firm that holds two seats.* On 29–30 October 2025 Ares Management agreed to buy all of BlueCove, a systematic fixed-income manager founded in 2018 in which it had held a minority stake since 2023. BlueCove had grown from \$1.8bn of assets in 2023 to \$5.5bn at 30 September 2025, with about 60 staff. Ares renamed it Ares Systematic Credit and cited insurance distribution and "increased interest in systematic credit" (The DESK, 30 October 2025; Alternative Credit Investor, 30 October 2025); Ares announced completion in early February 2026 (Business Wire, "Ares Completes Acquisition of BlueCove to Launch Systematic Credit Strategy"). Three months after the agreement, on Ares Capital's fourth-quarter call of 4 February 2026, ARCC's president Jim Miller described the direct-lending business of the same parent. "We will walk away from transactions based on documentation terms," he said, and he spoke of the "merits of not being a benchmark-style investor, as we are able to be selective not only with the companies we are financing but also with the industries we target more generally" (ARCC Q4 2025 earnings call). Set the two beside the table and Theo sees the asymmetry. BlueCove is row (i) almost by definition: benchmark-relative, model-driven, sold on its tracking error. ARCC is on no row at all. It negotiates a covenant package line by line, holds the loan to maturity or refinancing, and treats having no benchmark as the whole point. One parent, two objective functions that are close to opposites: one is paid to stay near the index and be a little better than it, and the other is paid to stay away from any index and to read the documents. A firm that already had the second seat bought the first, which is about as clear a public statement as exists that each seat now wants the other's tools. Theo has spent his career in the second seat. He starts to wonder which way the trade in tools runs for him.

> **Desk Myth vs. Desk Reality.** *Myth:* Systematic credit is equity quant with the tickers swapped for CUSIPs. *Reality:* An equity quant trades a stock that exists once, prints every few milliseconds, and can be shorted by calling the prime broker. A credit quant trades one of the seven bonds a single issuer has outstanding, three of which printed last week. Shorting any of them means borrowing it in repo, and that bond may be cheapest-to-deliver into a CDS auction or held by an ad hoc group that is about to sign a cooperation agreement. The signal library transfers. The plumbing does not.

### Quant-Shop Life: Three CUSIPs and the Breadth That Went Up

*(A fictional composite of a mid-sized systematic long-only credit manager. The routine is typical of the work; the firm, the issuer, the people and the numbers are invented and illustrative.)*

The morning risk report lands at 6:55 a.m., before anyone is in, as a PDF and as a table that three dashboards read. On the Thursday of Theo's third week the table says something pleasant. Effective breadth on the high-yield book, the number the firm quotes to clients as the count of independent bets it is running, has gone *up* by four overnight. Nobody traded. If anything the universe shrank: an issuer the book owned had closed an exchange offer the evening before, and two of its old bond lines were retired.

Theo is the only person who notices, partly because he is new enough to read the whole report and partly because he knows the issuer. Not personally; professionally, the way you know a building you once surveyed. It is a packaging company that spent the summer running a distressed exchange. Holders of the old unsecured notes could swap into new 1.5-lien notes at a discount to par, sitting behind the first-lien term loan but ahead of everyone who stayed put. A small group took a holding-company PIK note instead. The holdouts kept a stub of the old unsecured bonds, smaller now, with most of its covenants stripped by exit consents. Theo has spent months of his life on transactions shaped exactly like this one, and he knows what the three pieces are: one company, one enterprise value, cut into three claims of different seniority.

The security master does not know that. When the new CUSIPs arrived in the vendor's overnight file, two of them carried issuer identifiers that matched nothing on record, because the 1.5-lien notes were issued by a newly formed finance subsidiary and the PIK note by a holding company that had never issued public debt. The stub kept the old identifier. The firm's mapping table, which ties every CUSIP to an ultimate parent so that risk can be added up by issuer, had no row for either new entity, and so the default rule fired: an unmapped bond is its own parent. The breadth engine, which counts issuers and then discounts the bonds within each issuer for their correlation with one another (the arithmetic of that discount is the subject of §4.3), saw three unrelated companies where an hour of reading would have found one company in three seats. Breadth went up on the day the book's diversification went down.

The model is fine. A table with 41,000 rows is not, and Theo spends the next four hours inside it. The fix for the two new entities takes ten minutes. What takes the rest of the morning is the question of whether this has happened before. It has. A query for every CUSIP whose ultimate parent is itself, and whose name shares a first word with another issuer in the table, returns 212 rows. Most are harmless: two unrelated companies that both start with "National." Nineteen are not. They are subsidiaries left over from old exchanges and spin-offs, a captive finance arm treated as a stranger to its own parent, and two holdco/opco pairs that have been counted as separate issuers for years. Each one has been inflating breadth by a sliver, and understating issuer concentration by the same sliver, in every risk report since it was created.

Then an hour on the phone with the data vendor, arguing about what "issuer_id" means. The vendor's position, delivered politely, is that the field identifies the legal entity named on the indenture, which is exactly what its documentation says, and that the firm's notion of an "ultimate parent" is the firm's own business. The vendor is right. Theo, who has built guarantor-coverage tables at 3 a.m., also knows that the entity on the cover of the indenture is often the least informative fact about a bond. What matters is who guarantees it, what collateral it shares and where it sits in the waterfall, and none of that is in the feed. He writes a ticket for the data team asking for the mapping to ultimate parent plus the guarantor set and lien rank, each dated as of the document that created it, with a new row every time a transaction changes them.

At 2 p.m. the head of risk asks the question risk committees have asked for as long as there have been risk committees: why is this number different from yesterday? Theo has the answer, nineteen more answers nobody asked for, and a draft rule under which any unmapped CUSIP blocks the risk run until a person maps it. The rule is rejected, because it would have blocked the run on six mornings last quarter. It is replaced by a softer one. The run proceeds, but every unmapped CUSIP is flagged on page one and counted as a member of the largest issuer it plausibly belongs to until somebody decides otherwise. It is conservative by construction and mildly annoying, which is how everyone can be sure it will be noticed.

None of this is the work Theo pictured when he took the job, and it turns out to be most of the job. Man Numeric's credit co-heads, describing their own week on the March 2024 episode of Man Group's *Long Story Short* podcast ("Extra Credit: Algorithmic Advantage"), described earnings season in much the same spirit: a supplier reports before its customer, the result moves the signal on the customer as well, and the work multiplies across hundreds of issuers. The glamour of systematic credit is in the signal. The hours go into the tables that tell the signal what it is looking at.

Three threads from that Thursday run through the rest of the chapter. The breadth that went up is the effective-breadth calculation of §4.3, which is only as honest as the issuer map underneath it. ## 4.2 Why Credit Lagged Equities by Twenty Years

Equity quant had its industrial revolution in the 1990s and its reckoning in August 2007. Credit quant spent most of that time as a research topic. The reason is not that bond people are slower. The reason is that the four inputs a systematic process needs arrived late in credit, and some still have not fully arrived.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.44:* "the successful implementation of a scientific investment approach generally requires four primary inputs: rich and relevant data availability, high breadth of investable instruments, sophisticated markets enabling long and short positioning, and a growing body of research"
>
> **Annotation.** This is the Barclays QPS team's diagnosis, and it reads like a checklist of credit's handicaps. On a 2026 desk each input has a specific status. *Data*: TRACE began in July 2002 and was not fully disseminated until years later, so a clean bond-level history starts in the mid-2000s, and even that history needs the bias corrections in §4.5. *Breadth*: high in bond count, much lower in independent bets, as §4.3 derives. *Long and short*: still the weakest input, because single-name CDS liquidity concentrated in a small set of names after 2008 and shorting cash bonds runs through repo. *Research*: the factor literature arrived in a rush between about 2013 and 2018, and part of it failed replication between 2023 and 2026. The modern desk departs from the passage in one way: it treats *execution data* as a fifth input. Without a transaction-cost model you can trust, the other four only tell you what you would have earned in a market that does not exist.

Theo takes the four inputs in turn the way he would take four covenants, asking of each one what it permitted and from when.

**Data.** A restructuring banker lives on data that is excellent and late: a 10-K, a compliance certificate, a 13-week cash flow forecast that is accurate to the dollar and three weeks stale. Equity quants live on data that is mediocre and instant. Credit quant needs both, and for twenty years it had neither at scale. Bond prices came from dealer runs and matrix pricing. Fundamentals came from the same filings equity analysts used, but the bond-level details that matter in credit — which entity issued it, what it is guaranteed by, where it sits in the waterfall — were in offering memoranda, not databases. The security master is still the least glamorous and most valuable system on a quant credit desk.

**Breadth.** There are many more bonds than stocks. Man counts over 10,000 US corporate bonds from about 2,000 issuers, against roughly 100 US single-name CDS that trade regularly (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.10–11`). That ratio is the industry's founding argument for cash bonds over synthetics. It also hides a trap, which we derive in §4.3: five bonds of one issuer are not five bets.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.11:* "we believe that cash bonds will be the approach of choice for most quantitative investors, above all because breadth is key for quant strategies and is simply not available in synthetic form yet."
>
> **Annotation.** Man's argument is the fundamental law of active management (Grinold) in one sentence: the information ratio scales with the square root of breadth, and CDS has about a hundred liquid names while cash has thousands. Two things changed between 2022 and 2026. Listed credit-index futures (CME and ICE launched products in 2024; Cboe IBIG and IBHY block trades topped \$1bn a month in July and August 2026, according to exchange data reported by The DESK, 16 October 2026) give systematic books a cheap *beta* layer, so the cash book can be built purely for selection. But the single-name breadth problem in synthetics is no better. The modern desk departs from the passage in reading "breadth" as *effective* breadth, which is much smaller than bond count once you account for issuer clustering. That correction moves every capacity and IR estimate in this chapter.

**Long and short.** In equities you short a stock by borrowing it. In credit you short a bond by borrowing it in repo, if anyone will lend it at a rate that does not eat your edge, or by buying single-name CDS, which exists for about a hundred names that trade regularly. Ironically, the only market where shorting credit is truly easy is the one RSSG bankers dread: distressed names where the CDS is liquid because everyone is waiting for the auction. A long/short quant credit book is therefore usually a long book of cash bonds, hedged with index products, plus a short book that is smaller, more expensive and more crowded than the long. That asymmetry matters for D6 in §4.9. A long-only manager can't express a negative view beyond underweighting to zero, so "defense" has to come from what you avoid.

**Research.** The canonical factor papers in credit are recent. Jostova, Nikolova, Philipov and Stahel's "Momentum in Corporate Bond Returns" appeared in the *Review of Financial Studies* in 2013. Houweling and van Zundert's "Factor Investing in the Corporate Bond Market" appeared in the *Financial Analysts Journal* in 2017. Israel, Palhares and Richardson's "Common Factors in Corporate Bond Returns" appeared in the *Journal of Investment Management* in 2018. By the standards of equity factor research, which has the Fama–French three-factor model from 1993, credit had about a fifteen-year research lag. Then, as §4.5 shows, part of the new literature turned out to rest on contaminated TRACE data.

Add the four lags together and you get an industry that was technically possible by about 2015, commercially credible by about 2020, and is still tiny relative to its universe.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Blackstone_Systematic_Credit p.2:* "While systematic strategies manage roughly 20% of global equities, they account for less than 2% of the fixed-income universe."
>
> **Annotation.** This is the single most quoted ratio in the industry, and it has held for at least five years: Bloomberg reported the same 20%-versus-under-2% split for 2025 (BBH, *Introducing Systematic Fixed Income*, 2 March 2026). A ratio that holds while assets grow tells you the denominator grew too. The fixed-income universe expanded with post-2020 issuance, so systematic credit has been running to stand still in share terms. For a desk this cuts both ways. A low penetration rate means the classic factors are less arbitraged than in equities, the early-mover argument (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.9`). It also means the evidence base for those factors is mostly simulated, and allocators know it. The modern desk departs from the pitch-book reading of this ratio. "Room to grow" is a fact about the market. It is not a fact about your strategy's capacity, which §4.8 computes separately and which is usually much smaller.

Theo shows the Man line to Mags because he knows she will enjoy it, and she does. Over the quarters in which Man's systematic long-only credit went from \$2.0bn to \$3.1bn, the same firm's discretionary long-only credit went from \$19.8bn to \$37.5bn. Most of the electronic revolution of §4.7 was already in place by then: portfolio trades, all-to-all venues, a TRACE flag that marks portfolio trades as such, algorithms answering RFQs at a large asset manager's desk. The plumbing was built, and the water flowed to the people with analysts.

"So the machines paved the road," Mags says, "and the humans drove on it." She lets herself enjoy that for a moment. Then, because she is fair, she asks the question that matters. "Why?"

Theo has five answers by the end of the afternoon, and he ranks them by how much evidence he can find for each. The best supported is that clients came for yield rather than alpha (BlackRock's note says that in 2022 and 2023 "all in yields looked attractive in corporate credit," `p.3`), and a discretionary manager with a good relative-performance record absorbs that kind of demand at least as easily as a quant, and with a longer live record to show. The second is that the growth sits in the middle: Barclays's \$90–140bn of quant-screened money is a hybrid product, and it dwarfs the dedicated pool. The third is that the factors themselves became contested, for reasons §4.5 tells as a story. The fourth is capacity: much of high yield still trades by voice, and in stress the algorithms are among the first participants to step back, which caps how big a systematic high-yield book can get (§4.7–§4.8). The fifth is distribution. The two best-known acquisitions in the field, DCI by Blackstone and BlueCove by Ares, were bought by platforms with insurance clients, and a systematic engine sells best to an insurer as a machine for satisfying its constraints, not as a standalone alpha fund (§4.10).

"In my day," Mags says, "we had a word for a strategy that works in the backtest, has the infrastructure, and can't raise money."

"What was the word?"

"Early." She finishes her coffee. "Sometimes it was also 'wrong.' You don't find out which for about five years."

> **Poker Table.** Ines draws the penetration ratio on a napkin. "Two percent of the table is playing with a heads-up display and the other ninety-eight are playing by feel. The question isn't whether the HUD wins. It's how long before the ninety-eight notice, and how much smaller the pot gets when they do." Early-mover alpha is real, and it is a depreciating asset. The pot shrinks two ways. Competitors arrive and trade the same signals, so the gross edge $\alpha_0$ falls. And your own success grows your book until impact eats what is left. That second effect has a closed form, (4.2) in §4.8, and it is the one managers talk about least.

## 4.3 The Six-Axis Framework Map

Theo's sheet of paper has six columns because every systematic credit process, from a ten-person team to a \$40bn platform, has to answer the same six engineering questions. He does not start with six. He starts with one, and the pile hands him the other five over the afternoon, each in a sentence that some author probably thought was a footnote.

The first header he writes straight after lunch, because it is the one every paper leads with: **alpha families**, the question of where the forecast comes from. All six documents fill it, and they fill it alike. That is Mags's memo, and he gives it the column on the far left, where pitch books put the things they want you to see first.

The second arrives on page 31 of the Man paper, in a sentence an RSSG banker could have written. "Quant strategies are only as good as the data upon which they base their investment decisions," it says, and the pricing data "needs to be supplemented with a complete and up-to-date database of issuer and security-specific information" so that the models "specify the correct bonds" and can map datasets "at both issue- and issuer-level" (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.31`). Theo, fresh from the packaging company's three CUSIPs, writes **data stack** above column two, and under it the question from his sticky note: what are you allowed to know, and when did you know it?

Between the alpha model and the trade list sits the optimizer Kaz wrote over a long weekend, and in Man's version the same step is three layers, which the paper calls guard rails, autopilot and GPS (p.17). The phrase makes Theo picture a car being driven by a committee, which is not far wrong. **Portfolio construction**: how does a forecast become a position?

The fourth is on page 3 of Man's 2023 paper, the migration §4.7 tells in full. A firm rebuilt how it trades, wrote a paper about it, and did not change a signal. **Execution**: how do you get filled, and what does it cost?

The fifth is from the Barclays book, page 207, where the authors concede that the strategy returns reported in an earlier chapter "implicitly assumed that the capital invested in any of the strategies would be sufficiently small relative to the aggregate supply of fallen angels to not affect their pricing" (`Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.207`). The sixth is from BlackRock, page 3. In eVestment data for 2005–2025, the average high-yield manager "tended to participate in 89.56% of down markets and 92.24% of up markets," which the note reads as building a defensive exposure "largely by creating a portfolio that has a lower beta than the broad index" (`BlackRock_Systematic_High_Yield_Credit p.3`). Defence bought by owning less of the market is not really defence; it is a smaller position wearing a helmet. **Risk**: what do you hedge, and what are you paid to hold?

### Scoring the breadth column: the fundamental law with issuer clustering

Grinold's fundamental law of active management says that an information ratio is skill times the square root of the number of independent bets, scaled by how much of the forecast survives into the portfolio:

$$
IR = IC \cdot \sqrt{BR} \cdot TC,
$$

where $IC$ is the information coefficient (the correlation between forecast and realized residual return), $BR$ is breadth (independent bets per year), and $TC$ is the transfer coefficient (the correlation between the ideal and the actual active weights). The word doing the work is *independent*. Equity quants can mostly pretend that 3,000 stocks are 3,000 bets after removing factors. Credit cannot, because bonds come in families.

> **Math, Gently.** *What a distribution is, and the first one you meet.* A probability distribution is a list of the things that can happen with a weight on each, where the weights add up to one; Chapter 3 met it as a raffle. The simplest distribution on a desk is a coin with a slightly bent edge. Every bet a systematic book makes is a flip of such a coin: the forecast says "this bond beats its peers next month," and it either does or it does not. Call the chance that it does the hit rate $p$. One flip is a *Bernoulli trial*. Flip the coin many times and count the heads, and the count has a distribution of its own, the *binomial*. With $N$ independent flips the average count is $Np$, and the typical miss around that average (its standard deviation) is $\sqrt{Np(1-p)}$. Draw the binomial for a large $N$ and it looks like a bell, the first of many times the normal curve will turn up in this book without being invited. Now the tiny number. With $p = 0.52$, a coin bent two points your way, 100 flips give 52 heads on average with a spread of about 5, since $\sqrt{100 \times 0.52 \times 0.48} \approx 5$. Your edge over a fair coin is 2 heads and the noise is 5, so an honest observer could not tell your coin from a fair one. Flip it 10,000 times and the average is 5,200 heads with a spread of about 50. The edge is now 200 heads against noise of 50, four standard deviations, and nobody mistakes that for luck. The edge grew in proportion to $N$ and the noise only in proportion to $\sqrt{N}$, so the ratio of edge to noise grew as $\sqrt{N}$: a hundred times the flips bought ten times the ratio. That ratio is the information ratio, and "it grows with the square root of the number of independent flips" is the fundamental law. Paleologo's version, $IR = (2p-1)\sqrt{252\,N_e}$, is the same coin flipped every trading day (see the source extraction at the end of this subsection). Everything above assumed that each flip knew nothing about the others. The full version, without that assumption, is the derivation of (4.1) below.

That assumption is exactly what fails in credit, and the second building block shows how.

> **Math, Gently.** *Why variances add, and how correlation spoils it.* Variance is the average squared surprise (Chapter 3). Add two independent surprises and their variances simply add, because a positive surprise in one is as likely to meet a negative one in the other as a positive one, and the cross terms average to zero. Average two independent bets and the variance of the average halves. That is diversification in one sentence. Now put two thermometers in the same room. Each has a small error of its own, but most of what each one reads is the temperature of the room, and when the room warms up they both rise. Averaging them barely improves your estimate of the room, because their surprises are mostly the same surprise. Two bonds of one issuer are two thermometers in the same room, and the within-issuer residual correlation $\rho$ measures how much of their surprise they share. The cross terms no longer vanish; every pair of sibling bonds adds $\rho$ to the total. The tiny number: with $\rho = 0.7$ and $m = 2.25$ bonds per issuer, clustering inflates the variance of the average by a factor of $1 + (m-1)\rho = 1 + 1.25 \times 0.7 = 1.875$, so 1,800 bonds carry the diversification of $1{,}800/1.875 = 960$ independent bets. The full version is the derivation below.

Put the two boxes together and the correction writes itself. Take a universe of $N$ bonds issued by $M$ issuers, so $m = N/M$ bonds per issuer, and measure each bond's residual return $\epsilon_i$ after stripping rates, spread beta and factors (the IDO component). Give those residuals a common correlation $\rho$ within an issuer and none across issuers. The number of independent bets the universe is really worth, its effective breadth, is

$$
BR_{\text{eff}} = \frac{N}{1+(m-1)\rho}, \qquad IR = IC\,\sqrt{BR_{\text{eff}}}\;TC. \tag{4.1}
$$

Check the boundaries. If $\rho = 0$, bonds of the same issuer are independent and $BR_{\text{eff}} = N$. If $\rho = 1$, every bond of an issuer moves together and $BR_{\text{eff}} = N/m = M$: forty-seven bonds of one issuer are one bet with forty-seven CUSIPs. The formula is the design-effect correction from survey sampling, and it is the first thing ROBO gets wrong (see the spec box in §4.8).

> **Math, Gently.** *The design effect.* Pollsters who interview several people per household know that members of a household tend to answer alike, so 1,800 interviews in 800 households carry less information than 1,800 strangers would, and the factor $1 + (m-1)\rho$ by which that clustering inflates the variance is what survey statisticians call the design effect; (4.1) is bond count divided by it.

A worked example *(illustrative)*. A US HY universe of $N = 1{,}800$ bonds from $M = 800$ issuers has $m = 2.25$. With within-issuer residual correlation $\rho = 0.7$, $BR_{\text{eff}} = 1{,}800/(1 + 1.25 \times 0.7) = 960$. With $IC = 0.03$ and $TC = 0.5$, the naive IR from bond count is $0.03 \times \sqrt{1{,}800} \times 0.5 = 0.64$. The honest one is $0.03 \times \sqrt{960} \times 0.5 = 0.46$. The difference is about 28% of the headline IR, and it comes from nothing but a family tree.

#### The Full Derivation: effective breadth under issuer clustering

Suppose the universe has $N$ bonds issued by $M$ issuers, $m = N/M$ bonds per issuer, and the residual returns $\epsilon_i$ (after stripping rates, spread beta and factors, i.e. the IDO component) have unit variance, correlation $\rho$ between two bonds of the same issuer, and zero correlation across issuers. Take the equal-weighted average of all $N$ bets. Its variance is the sum of $N$ variances plus all the within-issuer covariances. Each issuer contributes $m(m-1)$ ordered pairs, each with covariance $\rho$, and there are $M$ issuers, so the covariance total is $Mm(m-1)\rho = N(m-1)\rho$:

$$
\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \epsilon_i\right) = \frac{1}{N^2}\Big[N + N(m-1)\rho\Big] = \frac{1+(m-1)\rho}{N}.
$$

A set of $N_e$ truly independent unit-variance bets averages to variance $1/N_e$. Matching the two gives the effective breadth $N_e = BR_{\text{eff}} = N/[1+(m-1)\rho]$, and substituting it for $BR$ in the fundamental law gives (4.1).

#### When effective breadth is honest, and when it lies

Kaz builds the first version of the breadth engine on a Wednesday, and it is wrong in three instructive ways before Friday.

The first version is ROBO's, more or less: breadth is the number of rows in the holdings file. Theo, who spent Thursday morning of the previous week inside the security master, points out that this is the number the packaging company's exchange offer made go *up*. Every capacity slide that says "a universe of 10,000 bonds" is quoting $N$, not $BR_{\text{eff}}$. So Kaz estimates $\rho$ from residual returns within issuer, and the desk's report now carries both numbers side by side, which makes the slide less flattering and the risk report more truthful.

The second version estimates $\rho$ over the previous three years of residuals, which happen to be calm, and gets a number comfortably below one. Ines asks what that number was in March 2020. Kaz reruns it on the stress months and finds that within an issuer the residuals moved very nearly together: when a company is in trouble its bonds stop being separate opinions and become one opinion at several maturities. That is the cruel part of (4.1). The calm-window $\rho$ is the one the book was levered to, and the stress-window $\rho$, close to one, arrives on exactly the day the leverage matters. A seasoned risk manager's first question about any breadth figure is therefore "estimated over which months?", and the defensible answer reports $BR_{\text{eff}}$ at a stressed $\rho$ alongside the average one.

The third version is honest about $\rho$ but still too generous, for two reasons that live in the other two letters of the law. The transfer coefficient is computed on the portfolio you *hold*, not the one you *wanted*. In credit an RFQ can come back with no responses, so a fill probability below one lowers $TC$ directly. Man warns that an unfilled order leaves "unintended and potentially unmodelled risk exposures" (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.19`), and a strategy with $IC = 0.03$ and a 60% fill rate on its most informative trades is not a $TC = 0.5$ strategy. And $IC$ is not constant across breadth: the 1,801st bond is usually a bond the desk knows less about. Paleologo makes the point that skill is not uniform across a universe, so $IC$ falls as you widen it, and a law that multiplies a falling $IC$ by a rising $\sqrt{BR}$ can peak well before the whole universe is in the book.

Mags, reading over Kaz's shoulder, adds a fourth way it breaks, and it is the one Part IV is built on. In a distressed name the issuer is the wrong unit. The first-lien loan and the unsecured notes of a company heading into an LMT are not two thermometers in the same room; they are two people in the same room, one of whom is about to be handed the other's chair. Their correlation can turn *negative* on the day of a priming transaction. The breadth engine treats the issuer as the cluster because, for investment grade and most of high yield, it is. Below that, the cluster is the *class*, and the arithmetic of (4.1) has to be run on classes (Chapter 13 builds the priming side of this; Chapter 12-B the class structure).

Where the law works is where its assumptions hold: liquid IG and BB residuals that are honestly IDO, an issuer map that is right, a $\rho$ measured across regimes, and fills good enough that the held book resembles the wanted one. In that world (4.1) is the single most useful sanity check in this chapter, and it is the first number Theo now asks for on any pitch book.

>
> **Annotation.** Paleologo writes for equity long/short pods, and his binomial version of the law, $IR = (2p-1)\sqrt{252\,N_e}$ with hit rate $p$ and $N_e = 1/\sum_i w_i^2$, is (4.1) dressed for daily bets. The sentence is the right mental model for credit with one amendment: in credit the multiplier is the *effective* number of bets, and issuer clustering shrinks it. Multiply zero skill by a large breadth and you get zero; multiply real skill by a breadth you overstated and you get a sizing error, because you will lever the book to a target volatility that assumed diversification you do not have. Where the modern credit desk departs from the equity version is the unit of the bet. It is the *issuer*, with bonds as noisy, partly redundant expressions of the same view, and the choice of which bond expresses the view is itself a decision (see "negative selection," §4.11).

## 4.4 Alpha Families: What Each Shop Thinks It Is Harvesting

The alpha column of the map reads like a restaurant chain's menu: the same dishes everywhere, renamed by each franchise. Here they are, with what each one actually harvests, because the frameworks disagree less about *which* signals to use than about *what those signals are paid for*.

Start with the signal everyone calls "value," because it organizes the rest of the chapter.

### Value is the gap between Q and P

The credit triangle says that a spread compensates for expected loss under the pricing measure. For a bond with risk-neutral default intensity $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}_i$ and loss given default $L_i = 1 - R_i$,

$$
s_i \approx \lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}_i L_i .
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *Two probabilities for one default.* There are two ways to put a number on the chance that a single-B company defaults this year, and they answer different questions. The first is the actuarial table: of all the single-B issuers in the agency default studies, what fraction actually defaulted in a typical year? That is a frequency you could count, and it lives under the physical measure $\mathbb{P}$. The second is the insurance premium: what does the market charge, per year, to take the default risk off your hands? That price contains the actuarial table, but it also contains fear (defaults arrive in recessions, when losses hurt most), illiquidity (you cannot easily sell the risk back) and the insurer's margin. Probabilities backed out of prices, as if nobody minded any of that, live under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$ (Chapter 3 met the same two measures as a raffle ticket). The credit triangle above is just the insurance premium written per year: if a fraction $\lambda$ of issuers default each year and each default costs you $L$ per unit of face, the fair annual charge is about $\lambda L$. The tiny number is the one the desk uses below. Theo quotes the long-run agency studies from memory, single-Bs defaulting at a few percent a year, more in recessions and less in booms, and settles on 2.5% for the example *(illustrative)*. Kaz backs the market's number out of a 380bp spread with a 60% loss given default: $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}} = 0.038/0.6 \approx 6.3\%$ a year. Both numbers are true at once. The market charges as if 6.3% of such companies defaulted every year, and history says about 2.5% do. The gap, $(6.3\% - 2.5\%) \times 0.6 \approx 230$bp, is not a free lunch. It is a price paid to whoever bears something the actuarial table leaves out, and the whole question of systematic "value" is which something. The full treatment of $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ and $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}$ is in Chapters 5–6.

A systematic manager builds its own estimate of default risk under the *physical* measure $\mathbb{P}$, from a structural model (Blackstone/DCI), a PD model (BlackRock), or rating-agency history. Call it $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_i$. The "default-adjusted spread" is what is left of the spread after paying for the losses you actually expect:

$$
V_i = s_i - \lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_i L_i \approx \big(\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}_i - \lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_i\big) L_i = \underbrace{\text{risk premium}_i}_{\text{paid for bearing default risk}} + \underbrace{\text{liquidity}_i}_{\text{paid for immediacy}} + \underbrace{\text{mispricing}_i}_{\text{what you hope is there}} . \tag{4.5}
$$

That identity is the second aha of the chapter. **Every value signal in the industry is a Q-minus-P gap.** DCI's structural-model comparison, BlackRock's default-adjusted spread, Man's spread-to-leverage ratio and Barclays' spread-versus-peers are all estimates of (4.5) with different models for $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ and different peer sets. The frameworks differ in which of the three pieces on the right they think they are harvesting. A manager who says it harvests *risk premium* is selling carry with a seatbelt, and will lose money in a spread-widening crisis, because that is what a risk premium is paid for. A manager who says it harvests *mispricing* is claiming a better $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ than the market's, and owes you the evidence in default avoidance (§4.9). A manager who harvests *liquidity* is a part-time market maker, and the fair place to judge it is the execution axis. Chapters 5–6 build $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ and $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}$ properly. Chapter 9 turns (4.5) into a production signal.

A worked example *(illustrative)*. Bond A is a single-B issuer at $s = 380$bp with $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}} = 2.5\%$ a year and $L = 0.6$, so expected loss is 150bp and $V_A = 230$bp. Bond B is a BB issuer at $s = 210$bp with $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}} = 0.5\%$ and $L = 0.6$, so $V_B = 180$bp. On raw spread, A is 170bp cheaper. On default-adjusted spread, it is 50bp cheaper. After the next step, which compares each bond with its rating and sector peers, A might not be cheap at all.

> **View From Another Seat.** *Apollo, looking at the same single-B.* The desk looks at Bond A and asks what it will do over the next twenty days against its factor loadings. Apollo's credit business, which the firm describes as its largest asset management strategy by assets (apollo.com, credit page, October 2026), would look at the same issuer and ask whether it would rather have written the loan itself. Apollo's chief executive Marc Rowan framed the firm's whole proposition on its fourth-quarter 2025 call (9 February 2026) as "excess return per unit of risk," and described the house attitude as "a principal's mindset" that "approaches every asset and every asset class as if they're going to own it for the long term because they do" (Apollo Q4 2025 earnings call). The "because they do" is the funding. Much of Apollo's credit sits against the annuity liabilities of its insurance affiliate Athene, which has to earn a spread over what it owes policyholders after a regulatory capital charge, and Rowan gave the recipe in one line on the same call: "Spread is created by having an asset origination machine, by having a liability origination machine at a low cost, and by having a low OpEx." Read (4.5) from that seat and a different term on the right-hand side is being harvested. The systematic desk hopes to own the *mispricing* term and rents the *risk premium* for as long as its 20-day horizon lasts. A holder with long, sticky liabilities collects the *liquidity* term almost by construction, because it never needs immediacy, and it collects the *risk premium* to maturity, the one horizon over which the gap between $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}$ and $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ is actually paid out. Its question about Bond A is less "is this cheap to its peers this month?" than "could a structure turn this single-B risk into something with a lower capital charge?" The two seats do meet, and usually on bad days. Apollo describes its lowest-LTV credit as "dislocation-driven" (apollo.com, hybrid value page), and when spreads gap that makes it a natural buyer of exactly the blocks a systematic book is trying to sell.

That next step is cross-sectional neutralization. Man describes a three-step pipeline: raw value, then risk-adjusted, then peer-relative (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.24`). Regress the risk-adjusted score on a vector of exposures $\mathbf{x}_i$ (rating, sector, spread duration, size) and keep the residual:

$$
\tilde V_i = V_i - \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i, \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta} \sum_i w_i \big(V_i - \boldsymbol\beta^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i\big)^2 .
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *Residuals, or handicapping.* A regression of $V$ on rating, sector and duration is doing what a golf handicap does. A handicap says how many strokes a player like you, at your level, usually takes on this course, and your net score is how you played *against people like you*. The fitted value $\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i$ is the handicap: the spread a bond with this rating, sector and duration typically carries. The residual $\tilde V_i$ is the net score. The weights $w_i$ just let some players' rounds count for more than others' (larger or more liquid bonds, usually). Tiny number: two B2 retailers trade at 450bp and 380bp, and the peer fit for a B2 retailer at their duration is 400bp. Their residuals are $+50$bp and $-20$bp. On raw spread both look like high yield; against their own peers one is cheap and the other is rich, and only that comparison is a bet on the company rather than on the asset class. The full version, with the weights and the choice of exposures, is in Chapter 8.

Neutralization is what turns "high yield is cheaper than investment grade" (a beta bet you could make with an ETF) into "this B2 retailer is cheap relative to other B2 retailers" (an IDO bet). IDO or it didn't happen.

Now the edge cases, because a value score is a ratio and ratios have denominators. Man's raw value measure takes the form $\text{OAS}_i / \max(\text{NetDebt}_i/\text{EBITDA}_i,\ 0.1)$ (`p.24`). The clip exists to stop division by zero. Consider what it does to three companies *(illustrative)*:

- a BB issuer at 4.0x leverage and 250bp scores $250/4 = 62.5$;
- a net-cash IG issuer at 90bp has negative net debt, so its leverage is clipped to 0.1 and it scores $900$;
- a CCC issuer with EBITDA of $-\$20$mm, net debt of \$600mm and an OAS of 1,500bp has leverage of $-30$x, also clipped to 0.1, and scores $15{,}000$.

Congratulations: the model's favorite bond is the one with a restructuring adviser. Theo, who has *been* that adviser, recognizes the name.

"I've met this bond," he says.

"It's illustrative," says Kaz.

"So was mine, legally speaking." *(What follows is a fictional composite; the company is invented.)* A couple of years before he left PJT, a building-products distributor lost its largest customer, swung to negative EBITDA within two quarters, and hired his team when the revolver was nearly fully drawn. Its unsecured notes went out to about 1,500 over and then more or less stopped trading. Every input on the third line above was true of it: roughly \$600mm of net debt, EBITDA of about minus \$20mm, and a leverage ratio that came out negative and meant nothing. A screen clipping that ratio to 0.1 would have ranked those notes the cheapest bond in high yield by more than a factor of ten. What no screen could see was what the deal team spent four months negotiating: a new-money first-lien facility that primed the notes, an exchange offer for the unsecured at a deep discount, and a recovery for the holdouts that ended in the low teens.

A single clip has merged three populations that have nothing in common: very low leverage, net cash, and negative EBITDA. The spec that comes out of the conversation splits them into separate branches and sends the negative-EBITDA branch to a distressed model rather than a value screen.

#### When the value identity works, and when it breaks

(4.5) earns its keep where its inputs are believable: liquid IG and BB bonds, issuers with positive and reasonably stable earnings, a $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ estimated from a long history or a well-calibrated structural model, and a peer set dense enough that the neutralization step has something to compare against. In that world a large positive $V_i$ after neutralization is genuinely interesting, and the three pieces on its right-hand side can even be told apart, by watching how the bond behaves when spreads widen.

It breaks in three places, and each has a check that an experienced PM runs before reading anything else. The first is mispricing that is really risk premium. A $V_i$ that is mostly risk premium looks like alpha until the first widening, when it behaves exactly like beta, and the desk finds out which one it owned on the worst day to find out. The check is to look at the signal's returns in the index's worst months: a mispricing harvest should be roughly indifferent to them, and a premium harvest loses in them. The second is a $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ that is not a default rate at all. Backed out of equity volatility without the Merton leverage mapping (Chapter 6), it is a volatility, and the check is whether the model's $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ for a BBB utility and a BBB software company differ for reasons a credit analyst would recognize. The third is the clip, and every ratio whose denominator can cross zero has one. Its check is the cheapest in this chapter: print the twenty highest-scoring names and read them. Kaz does, on Theo's insistence, and finds two companies in active restructuring and one that had already filed.

### The rest of the menu

**Momentum and the equity-to-credit lead–lag.** Credit momentum on its own is weak and mostly a high-yield phenomenon (Jostova et al. 2013 find it in HY but not IG). The strong version is *cross-asset*: equity returns lead bond spread changes, because the equity market prices news about firm value first and bond prices, which trade less often, catch up. In regression form,

$$
\Delta s_{i,t+1} = -\beta\, r^{E}_{i,\,t-k:t} + \epsilon_{i,t+1}, \qquad \beta > 0,
$$

so a stock that rallied predicts the bond's spread will tighten. Barclays reports equity-momentum-in-credit long–short excess returns of 6.34% a year in IG and 15.9% in HY over 1993–2017, *before costs*, with information ratios above 1.5 (`Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.628`). That is a large number, and it is momentum, the family with the highest turnover (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.19`). The cost model will take much of it back, as §4.7 shows. Chapters 9–10 own the full derivation.

**Quality and low risk.** Robeco's Patrick Houweling has run a Conservative Credits strategy built on the low-risk anomaly since 2012 (Funds Society, 18 June 2015): short-maturity, highly rated bonds of low-risk issuers earn more per unit of risk than the market. It is the credit cousin of the equity low-volatility anomaly, and it is the factor least likely to embarrass you in a crisis and most likely to embarrass you in a rally.

**Carry.** Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen and Vrugt ("Carry," *Journal of Financial Economics*, 2018) show that carry predicts returns across asset classes, and Man cites Sharpe ratios of roughly 0.4–0.5 for carry strategies (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.26`). In credit, carry is spread plus roll-down, and it is the purest risk-premium harvest on the menu: the $\text{risk premium}_i$ term of (4.5) with no pretense of mispricing.

**Size.** The literature disagrees. Houweling and van Zundert (2017) find a size premium: bonds of smaller issuers outperform. Barclays finds none in its own tests (`Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.516`). A desk should read the disagreement as a liquidity question in disguise. Small issuers' bonds are less liquid, so any size premium is partly a liquidity premium, and whether you can harvest it depends on your execution axis, not your alpha axis.

**Informed investor.** Man's fourth family uses positioning data such as equity short interest (`p.27–28`). It is the closest thing on the menu to Ines's question, "who at this table knows more than you?" It is also the weakest family in Man's own simulation.

**Components, not factors.** Acadian deliberately avoids the word "factor." Its inputs are default rates, value, credit deterioration and rating migration (Institutional Investor, 1 June 2023). The rename is not cosmetic. A "factor" invites you to harvest a premium; a "component" invites you to forecast an outcome. Acadian is telling you it thinks it harvests the mispricing term.

Here is what the families look like in one manager's simulation, with a caveat in bold because the chart's footnotes are where pitch books hide their bodies.

| Family (Man 2022, US IG and HY) | Simulated annualized IR of decile portfolios |
| :--- | :--- |
| Value | 2.15 |
| Momentum | 1.69 |
| Quality | 1.55 |
| Informed investor | 0.79 |
| Final combination | 2.35 |

*Source: `Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.28`.* **These are gross, simulated, decile-portfolio information ratios. They are not net of transaction costs, not capacity-adjusted, and not live.** Treat them as $IC\sqrt{BR}$ before $TC$ and before the cost model.

The table holds a small puzzle that teaches something. If four strategies with information ratios $\theta_k$ have return correlation matrix $C$, the best mean–variance combination has

$$
IR_{\text{combo}}^2 = \boldsymbol\theta^{\top} C^{-1} \boldsymbol\theta ,
$$

which for uncorrelated strategies is $\sum_k \theta_k^2$. With Man's four IRs, $\sqrt{2.15^2 + 1.69^2 + 1.55^2 + 0.79^2} = \sqrt{10.51} = 3.24$. The reported combination is 2.35. So either the families are substantially correlated, or the "final" signal is not weighted mean–variance optimally, or both. Under the simplest assumption, equal weights on four equal-volatility portfolios with a common pairwise correlation $c$, the combination's IR is $\sum_k \theta_k / \sqrt{4 + 12c}$. Setting that equal to 2.35 gives $\sqrt{4 + 12c} = 6.18/2.35 = 2.63$, so $c \approx 0.24$ *(our inference under stated assumptions, not Man's number)*. That is plausible for credit factors, which share a common exposure to the credit cycle. Henke, Kaufmann, Messow and Fang-Klingler (*Journal of Index Investing*, 2020) estimate pairwise credit factor correlations directly. The desk lesson is that diversification across *factors* is limited by the same thing that limits diversification across *bonds*: a common driver you cannot fully strip out.

Before you build the most sophisticated version of all this, note a survey Man cites. PGGM's survey of credit factor managers after the 2020 shock split them into "KISS" managers and sophisticated ones (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.30`). Keep It Simple, Stupid is a philosophy that has never once been applied to a credit agreement. It is, however, a good prior for a factor model fitted on fifteen years of sparse TRACE data, which brings us to the data stack.

## 4.5 The Data Stack and the Replication Crisis

Restructuring bankers have a sense of time that equity quants lack. A 10-Q for the quarter ending 30 June is not known on 30 June. It is known when EDGAR timestamps the filing, typically 40 to 45 days later. The compliance certificate arrives when the credit agreement says it arrives. The 13-week cash flow forecast is as of a Friday and wrong by the next one. Theo has spent a career asking "as of when?" It turns out to be the most valuable question in quantitative credit, and the one a large part of the academic literature forgot to ask.

Man's sketch of the data a quant credit process needs (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.31–32`) runs from TRACE and a security master, through equity data, to alternative data such as supply-chain linkages (Revere), bond short interest and text-mined analyst notes. Each layer has its own trap. TRACE public dissemination caps the reported size of large trades (an IG print over \$5mm shows as "5MM+", an HY print over \$1mm as "1MM+"), so a block happened but you are not told how big it was. The security master has to map every CUSIP to its issuer, its guarantors and its place in the waterfall, and to keep doing so after a drop-down moves the collateral (Theo's Thursday in §4.1 was one morning of that job). Equity data arrive fast and clean but describe a different claim. Alt data arrive with vendor load dates that have nothing to do with when anyone could have traded on them.

The discipline that ties them together fits in one line. Give every field $x$ an event time $t^{\text{ev}}(x)$ (the quarter end, the trade time) and a knowledge time $t^{\text{kn}}(x)$ (the EDGAR acceptance timestamp, the TRACE dissemination time, the vendor's load time). A signal used to trade at $t$ must be $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable, which in database terms means

$$
s_{i,t} = f\Big(\{\,x : t^{\text{kn}}(x) \le t - \ell\,\}\Big),
$$

for a processing lag $\ell \ge 0$.

> **Math, Gently.** *The sealed envelope.* Picture every number the desk will ever use arriving in a sealed envelope with two dates on it. The first, printed on the contents, is the *event time*: the quarter the 10-Q describes, 30 June. The second, stamped by the post office, is the *knowledge time*: the moment the envelope could first be opened, which for that 10-Q is when EDGAR accepts the filing, typically 40 to 45 days later. A backtest is a replay of history in which the desk may open only envelopes postmarked before the trading date. That is all "$\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable" means: the signal at $t$ is built from envelopes you could have opened by $t$. The symbol $\mathcal{F}_t$, the *filtration*, is simply the pile of opened envelopes at time $t$, and the pile only grows. The tiny number: a signal that uses second-quarter leverage on 31 July is reading a letter that will not be delivered until mid-August. In a backtest that is two weeks of foresight every quarter, and in a factor that rebalances monthly it is plenty to make a mediocre signal look brilliant. The full version, with the processing lag $\ell$ for the time it takes to read the letter, is the formula just above.

There are two ways to break it. A *lead error* joins on event time when it should join on knowledge time. An *ex-post filter* drops observations because of returns realized after $t$. The second is subtler, and a picture shows what it does.

> **Math, Gently.** *The bell curve, and what trimming a tail does to its average.* The normal distribution is the bell: most outcomes near the middle, fewer and fewer farther out, symmetric on both sides, and described completely by its centre $\mu$ and its width $\sigma$. About two-thirds of outcomes land within one $\sigma$ of the centre and about 95% within two. Its density $\varphi$ is just the height of the bell at each point. Now take a pair of scissors and cut off the worst 1% of outcomes, everything more than about $2.33\sigma$ below the centre. The bell no longer balances. You removed weight only from the left, so the balance point of what is left slides to the right. By how much? Not much, about $0.027\sigma$, because the slice was thin. But it is pure manufacture: nothing about the bond changed, only what you agreed to look at. On a bond with 4% monthly volatility, $0.027 \times 4\% \approx 11$bp a month. One warning matters more than the arithmetic. Credit returns are *not* bell-shaped. Their left tail, where defaults and distressed exchanges live, is far heavier than the bell's, so the slice the scissors take is fatter and the shift is larger. Fat tails, and the lognormal that suits quantities that cannot fall below zero, get their own box in Chapter 5. The full version of the 0.027 is "The Full Derivation: the mean of a truncated normal" below.

In backtest terms: suppose a bond's monthly return $r$ has mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, and a "data-cleaning" rule deletes the worst fraction $q$ of next-month returns as presumed errors. For $q = 1\%$ the mean of what survives rises by $0.027\sigma$. With a bond-level monthly volatility of 4% *(illustrative, HY)*, that is about 11bp a month, or 1.3% a year, of return created by deleting things. Credit returns are not normal. The left tail holds the defaults and distressed exchanges, so the true bias is larger. Nor is the bias symmetric across a factor. Trimming the left tail of every bond helps the leg with more left tail, which in a "downside risk" or "high-minus-low credit risk" factor is the long leg. The filter does not just add noise. It manufactures the premium.

#### The Full Derivation: the mean of a truncated normal

Suppose a bond's monthly return $r$ is normal with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, and a "data-cleaning" rule deletes the worst fraction $q$ of next-month returns as presumed errors. Write $r = \mu + \sigma u$ with $u$ standard normal, and let $z_q = \Phi^{-1}(q)$ be the lower quantile, so the rule keeps $u > z_q$, an event of probability $1 - q$. Because the standard normal density $\varphi(u) = e^{-u^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ satisfies $\varphi'(u) = -u\,\varphi(u)$, the integral of $u\,\varphi(u)$ is $-\varphi(u)$, and the conditional mean of $u$ is

$$
\mathbb{E}[u \mid u > z_q] = \frac{1}{1-q}\int_{z_q}^{\infty} u\,\varphi(u)\,du = \frac{1}{1-q}\Big[-\varphi(u)\Big]_{z_q}^{\infty} = \frac{\varphi(z_q)}{1-q}.
$$

Scaling back to returns, for a normal distribution with lower quantile $z_q = \Phi^{-1}(q)$, the mean of what survives is

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[r \mid r > \mu + z_q\sigma\big] = \mu + \sigma\,\frac{\varphi(z_q)}{1-q},
$$

where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density. For $q = 1\%$, $z_q = -2.326$ and $\varphi(z_q) = 0.0267$, so the bias is $0.0267/0.99 \approx 0.027\sigma$. With a bond-level monthly volatility of 4% *(illustrative, HY)*, that is about 11bp a month, or 1.3% a year, of return created by deleting things.

### Case Study: The Day the Factor Zoo Got Audited (2019–2026)

In 2019 Bai, Bali and Wen published "Common risk factors in the cross-section of corporate bond returns" in the *Journal of Financial Economics*. It proposed a four-factor model for bonds, was cited more than four hundred times, and became a standard benchmark for quant credit research. Alexander Dickerson, Philippe Mueller and Cesare Robotti then tried to replicate it. They found that two of the factors had been built with a one-month lead error, one with a lag error, and that left-tail losses had been trimmed ex post (Warwick Business School, 9–10 September 2024). Their own paper, "Priced risk in corporate bonds" (*JFE* 150(2), 2023), concluded that beyond traded liquidity the proposed bond factors add nothing to the bond market factor. It won the Fama–DFA Prize. The *JFE* retracted the original paper, which Warwick described as the first retraction of its kind at the journal.

The audit then went wholesale. In April 2026, Dickerson, Robotti and Rossetti ("The Corporate Bond Factor Replication Crisis," arXiv:2604.07880) re-examined 108 bond signals in nine clusters. They report that "the majority of previously documented factors do not produce statistically significant bond CAPM alphas after correction" for TRACE price measurement error and asymmetric ex-post return filtering. Their Open Source Bond Asset Pricing project now publishes the corrected TRACE panel and the bias-corrected factors, so any desk can rerun its backtests on the cleaned data.

In equities a one-month timestamp error is a bug. In credit it was a four-factor model, four hundred citations, and a prize for the people who found it.

**Tuesday.** Tuesday is the boring day, and the boring day is where the money goes. Kaz reads the pipeline's early stages line by line, because Kaz does not trust code they have not read, and Theo reads it with them because he is beginning to suspect that this is what the job is.

Those stages do to TRACE what a good associate does to a data room. It applies the cancellations, corrections and reversals that dealers file after the fact, so that a trade later withdrawn does not stay in the history as if it happened. It removes the double count when a trade done on an agency basis is reported by both sides. It runs a decimal-shift corrector, because some fraction of reported prices are off by a factor of 10 or 100, typed by a person at the end of a long day. It runs a bounce-back filter that flags a print which jumps by a large amount (the threshold is 35 points) and comes straight back within a few rows. It drops trades reported after the bond matured. Later on, the pipeline marks the month's price as the last daily volume-weighted average in the final five business days of the month, rather than a single month-end print.

Kaz's vendor composites did none of this, or did some of it in ways the vendor does not document. That alone is not a scandal. The scandal, if there is one, is in a footnote of the April paper that Theo reads three times and then draws on the whiteboard.

Suppose a bond's true value never changes and its true return is zero, but the price you record each month-end is off by a little noise: half a percent too high or half a percent too low, at random. A value signal that sorts on price sees the bond printed half a percent too low and decides it is cheap, so the bond goes into the long leg. The bond printed half a percent too high looks rich and goes into the short leg. Next month the noise is drawn fresh. The "cheap" bond's return is measured from a base that was too low, so on average it gains about half a percent as the error washes out. The "rich" bond's return is measured from a base that was too high, so it loses about half a percent. Long minus short: about 1.00% a month, out of a true zero, from noise of plus or minus 0.50%. The paper calls this *latent implementation bias*, because the same error enters both the signal and the return, and it is invisible unless you go looking for it.

"So the signal isn't predicting the bond," Theo says. "It's predicting the typo."

"It's predicting the typo's correction," Kaz says, with the precision of the newly humbled. The fix is in the paper too, and it is almost insultingly simple: build the signal from a price one to ten business days *before* the formation date, or measure the return from a price at the start of the next month, so the same error cannot sit in both. Either gap breaks the link between the error in the sort and the error in the return. Kaz adds a signal gap to the notebook and reruns overnight.

Not smaller. Gone. On the clean panel with a signal gap, the price-momentum sleeve that had carried a third of Kaz's Sharpe produces an alpha that is statistically indistinguishable from zero. Kaz's first response, which Theo will later recognize as the first of the classical stages, is to blame the pipeline. Their filter kills momentum, Kaz says. Of course it does: momentum lives in the right tail, in the bonds that rallied hard, and a filter that throws out big moves throws out the signal.

Theo looks for the place in the paper where the authors deal with exactly that objection, and finds it. They test momentum under many trimming choices. When the trimming is done *ex ante*, with thresholds a trader could have known at the time, the six-month momentum alpha is indistinguishable from zero under every choice they try. It comes back only under *ex post* trimming of the right tail, chopping each month's biggest returns after the fact. In other words, the momentum premium appears when you decide what counts as an error by looking at the answer.

Then Kaz opens their own cleaning code, and goes quiet in the way Theo has learned to recognize. Two years ago, building the vendor panel, Kaz winsorized returns at the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles *of the full sample*, from 2002 to the present, a perfectly standard step that every statistics course teaches. It means that a return in 2009 was clipped using percentiles computed with data from 2020. That is look-ahead, the second bias in the paper, and it falls hardest on momentum, which the authors find needs the right-tail winsorization to look significant at all. Kaz's momentum had been fed from the future in two ways at once.

> **Math, Gently.** *432 lottery tickets: t-statistics and false discoveries.* A *t-statistic* is a signal measured in units of its own noise: the average alpha divided by its standard error. A $t$ above 1.96 is the conventional bar because, if the true alpha were zero and the noise roughly bell-shaped, you would clear it by luck only about 5% of the time. That is a fine bar for one idea. It is a bad bar for 432. Test 432 strategies that are all worthless, and about 5% of them, around 22, will clear 1.96 in one direction or the other anyway. A researcher who reports only those 22 has found nothing and published 22 papers. The fix is to raise the bar with the number of tickets. The *false discovery rate* procedure of Benjamini and Hochberg, which the April 2026 audit uses, sorts the results from strongest to weakest and asks each one to clear a hurdle that tightens the more tests were run, so that among the results you call discoveries, no more than about 5% are expected to be flukes. The tiny number from the audit: 65 of 432 specifications clear $t > 1.96$, well above the roughly 22 that luck alone would produce, so something is there; but only 26 survive the stricter procedure. The full version, including the deflated Sharpe ratio and the thresholds of Harvey, Liu and Zhu, is in §7.8.

## 4.6 Portfolio Construction: Guard Rails, Autopilot, GPS

A recommendation becomes a plan of reorganization only after it survives the constraints: absolute priority, the voting thresholds, the cash actually in the account. Portfolio construction is the same step for a quant book. The alpha model says what you would like to own. The constraints decide what gets confirmed.

### The risk unit: DTS

Every framework on the map budgets risk in Duration Times Spread, and the reason is a single assumption about how spreads move.

> **Math, Gently.** *Points or percent?* A spread can move in two ways. It can move in *points*: everyone widens by 20bp, so the 100bp bond goes to 120 and the 400bp bond to 420. Or it can move in *percent of itself*: everyone widens by 10%, so the 100bp bond goes to 110 and the 400bp bond to 440. Credit history looks much more like the second than the first, for the same reason a \$10 stock and a \$400 stock do not move by the same number of dollars on a bad day: the size of a move scales with the size of the thing that moves. If moves are proportional, a bond's spread change is its spread times a common percentage, and its price loss is its spread duration (the percentage price change per unit of spread change; Chapter 4-B builds it) times that spread change. So the loss is duration times spread times the common percentage, and duration times spread is the only number that tells two bonds apart. That product is DTS. The tiny number is Ben Dor's, quoted below: a portfolio at 200bp with two years of spread duration and one at 100bp with four years both carry $200 \times 2 = 100 \times 4 = 400$ bp-years, and a common 10% widening costs each of them about 0.4% of price ($2 \times 20$bp in one case, $4 \times 10$bp in the other). The same "moves in proportion to its level" idea is what makes a lognormal, rather than a normal, the natural picture for quantities that cannot go below zero, such as spreads and share prices; Chapter 5 has that box. The full version is the derivation just below.

A bond's excess return from a spread move is, to first order, $r_i \approx -D^{\text{spr}}_i\,\Delta s_i$. If spread changes are proportional to spread levels, $\Delta s_i = s_i\,\delta_i$, where the relative change $\delta_i$ has a volatility $\sigma_\delta$ that is roughly common across bonds, then

$$
\sigma(r_i) \approx D^{\text{spr}}_i\,s_i\,\sigma_\delta = \text{DTS}_i\,\sigma_\delta ,
\qquad
\sigma\Big(\sum_i w_i r_i\Big) \approx \sigma_\delta \sum_i w_i\,\text{DTS}_i \quad \text{when the } \delta_i \text{ move together.}
$$

Systematic spread risk adds up in DTS, so DTS is the unit you budget in. Man's statistical factor risk model is DTS-adjusted (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.15`), and Barclays uses DTS as its risk unit throughout.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.50:* "a portfolio with a weighted spread of 200 bps and spread duration of two years is equally risky as a portfolio with a spread of 100 bps and spread duration of four years."
>
> **Annotation.** The arithmetic is $200 \times 2 = 100 \times 4 = 400$ bp-years, and the claim behind it is the proportional-spread-change model above. For an RSSG reader it overturns an instinct. A short-dated B3 bond at 400bp *(illustrative)* feels riskier than a ten-year BBB at 150bp, but with spread durations of 2 and 7 its DTS is 800 against 1,050, so in a broad widening the BBB loses more. The modern desk departs from the passage at both ends of the spread distribution. At very tight spreads, moves are closer to parallel than proportional, so DTS understates the risk of the tightest bonds. At distressed levels, bonds stop trading on spread and start trading on price and expected recovery, so spread duration loses its meaning. That is one more reason the distressed cell of the map is empty (§4.11).

#### When DTS is the right ruler, and when it bends

The proportional model is an empirical claim, and like most empirical claims in credit it holds best in the middle. Across the broad body of investment grade and the BB and single-B parts of high yield, through ordinary months and ordinary widenings, spread changes scale with spread levels well enough that DTS is the right unit for a risk budget. It has one property no other spread measure has: it adds. A PM can give each sleeve a DTS budget, sum them, and know roughly what a 10% widening will cost the book.

At the edges it bends, and the two edges bend in opposite directions. Theo sees the first one in the desk's own risk report in a week when spreads are near their tights. Kaz's DTS model says the tightest decile of the investment-grade book, bonds around 60bp, carries the least risk on the sheet. Ines, who has traded those bonds, does not believe it. Kaz plots that decile's daily spread changes against the index's and finds that on the days the index widened by about 10%, the 60bp names did not widen by 6bp. They widened by something closer to 10bp, the same *points* as their neighbours. Near the floor, spreads move more in parallel than in proportion, because no bond trades much below the compensation that liquidity and funding alone demand, and so DTS understates the risk of the tightest bonds. The check an experienced risk manager runs first is the one Kaz just ran: what is the spread regime, and is the tightest decile moving in points?

The second edge is the one Theo knows from his old job. At distressed levels bonds stop trading on spread and start trading on price and expected recovery. A bond at 45 cents does not "widen by 10%"; it moves when the market's view of the recovery waterfall moves, and its spread duration, computed as if it will pay to maturity, measures something that is not going to happen. DTS at that point is a number with units and no meaning, which is one more reason the distressed cell of the map is empty (§4.11).

### Man's three layers, and the cost of changing your mind

Man describes its process in three layers (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.17`). The **guard rails** are hard and soft constraints on volatility, rating, sector and issuer exposure. The **autopilot** is an objective function that pushes the portfolio toward the efficient frontier while minimizing concentration risk, unintended factor exposures and transaction costs. The **GPS** is the ongoing process that refines the portfolio over time, keeping it diversified and limiting the sources of risk.

> **Math, Gently.** *What an optimizer is.* An optimizer is a very patient person packing a suitcase. Every item has a value (the bond's forecast alpha). Every item also adds to a penalty for straying from the packing list your client gave you, the benchmark, and that penalty grows with the *square* of how far you stray, so a small tilt costs almost nothing and a large one costs a great deal. And every item you take out of the suitcase or put into it costs a fee at the airport counter (the transaction cost). The optimizer tries every combination, in effect, and keeps the one with the most value after penalties and fees. Its weakness is that it believes every number it is given, including the values, which are forecasts. The tiny number: you hold a bond with a forecast alpha of 30bp and could swap it for one at 50bp. The new bond is better by 20bp. But selling the first costs 20bp and buying the second costs another 20bp, so the swap costs 40bp to gain 20bp, and a sensible optimizer leaves the suitcase alone. The full version is the objective function below.

In symbols, the autopilot solves something like

$$
\max_{\mathbf{w}\in\mathcal{C}} \;\; \hat{\boldsymbol\alpha}^{\top}\mathbf{w} \;-\; \frac{\gamma}{2}\,(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{b})^{\top}\Sigma\,(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}^{b}) \;-\; \sum_i c_i\,\lvert w_i - w^{0}_i\rvert ,
$$

where $\mathbf{w}^{b}$ is the benchmark, $\mathbf{w}^{0}$ the current holdings, $\Sigma$ a DTS-scaled factor covariance plus issuer-level specific risk, $c_i$ the one-way cost per unit traded, $\gamma$ the risk aversion, and $\mathcal{C}$ the guard rails.

The last term is where Man's "headroom" for alpha uncertainty lives: the objective "differentiate[s] whether the security is already held or not" (`p.17`). Take the simplest decision, swapping a held bond $j$ for a candidate $k$, with $\hat\alpha$ measured over the expected holding period. The swap pays in expectation only if $\hat\alpha_k - \hat\alpha_j > c_j + c_k$. But $\hat\alpha_k - \hat\alpha_j$ is an estimate with standard error $\sigma_\Delta$, so add a headroom of $z\,\sigma_\Delta$.

> **Math, Gently.** *Standard errors, and the z-score.* A forecast alpha is a reading from a noisy instrument, like a bathroom scale that wobbles by a pound or two each time you step on it. The *standard error* is the size of the wobble: the typical distance between what the instrument reads and the truth. A *z-score* is a distance measured in wobbles, so $z = 1$ means "one standard error." If the scale says you lost half a pound since yesterday and it wobbles by two, you have learned nothing, and a sensible person does not change their diet. The headroom rule is the trading version of that good sense: it refuses to act on a reading that sits inside the instrument's tolerance. Tiny number: the swap costs 60bp round trip and the estimate of the alpha difference wobbles by 50bp. If the true difference is zero, a desk that trades whenever the reading beats the cost alone ($z = 0$) still trades 11.5% of the time, purely on wobble. Demand one extra standard error of margin ($z = 1$) and the pure-noise swaps fall to 1.4%. The full version is "The Full Derivation: the probability of a pure-noise swap" below.

With round-trip costs of 60bp and $\sigma_\Delta = 50$bp *(illustrative, HY)*, a desk with no headroom ($z = 0$) makes a pure-noise swap 11.5% of the time. With $z = 1$ that falls to 1.4%. Barclays reaches the same answer with rules rather than a formula: turnover buffers and turnover budgets (`Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.724`). Either way, the cheapest trade in credit is the one you talk yourself out of.

#### The Full Derivation: the probability of a pure-noise swap

Suppose the true alpha difference between the candidate $k$ and the held bond $j$ is zero, and the estimated difference $\hat\alpha_k - \hat\alpha_j$ is normally distributed around that zero with standard deviation $\sigma_\Delta$. The desk trades when the estimate clears the round-trip cost plus the headroom. If the true difference is zero, the probability that you trade anyway is

$$
\mathbb{P}\big(\hat\alpha_k - \hat\alpha_j > c_j + c_k + z\,\sigma_\Delta\big) = 1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{c_j + c_k}{\sigma_\Delta} + z\right),
$$

which follows from dividing both sides of the inequality by $\sigma_\Delta$, so that the left side becomes a standard normal variable, whose probability of exceeding a level $a$ is $1 - \Phi(a)$. With $c_j + c_k = 60$bp and $\sigma_\Delta = 50$bp the level is $1.2 + z$: $1 - \Phi(1.2) = 0.115$ at $z = 0$ and $1 - \Phi(2.2) = 0.014$ at $z = 1$.

#### When headroom helps, and when it costs you the trade

Headroom is built for slow signals, and for them it is nearly free. A value score that changes a little each month, on a bond the desk expects to hold for most of a year, loses almost nothing by waiting for a clearer reading: if the alpha is real it will still be there next month, a little better measured. That is the world the formula assumes, a gap that persists while the instrument wobbles around it.

Event signals break the assumption. When a bond falls out of the investment-grade index on a downgrade, the forced selling is concentrated in a few days around the index exit, and most of the opportunity lives in those days. When an issuer launches an exchange offer, the early-tender deadline decides who gets the better consideration and who is left holding a stub stripped of its covenants, as the packaging company's holdouts learned in §4.1. In both cases the cost of *not* trading is the whole move. A headroom of one standard error, calibrated on monthly value scores, would sit politely on its hands through the only week that mattered.

Theo learns the distinction from a near miss. The desk's monthly rebalance, headroom and all, would have waited a month to sell a bond whose issuer had announced an exchange the day after the rebalance. Ines catches it because the RFQ flow in the name has tripled. The fix the desk settles on is not a cleverer $z$. It is a label on every signal saying what kind of clock it runs on: slow signals go through the headroom rule, and event signals bypass it and go to a person with a calendar of deadlines. The first question to ask of any turnover control is how quickly the alpha it is protecting decays.


### Nuveen's identity, or the 13-week model with a covariance matrix

The multi-asset allocator works one level up, at the sector index, and its forecast is an accounting identity.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Nuveen_Multi_Asset_Credit_Quant_Framework p.3:* "Forward return estimates = yield – estimated credit loss – ∆rates MTM – ∆spread MTM"
>
> **Annotation.** Nuveen defines yield as yield to worst where available, credit loss from default-rate forecasts and expected recoveries, and the two MTM terms from forecast rate and spread changes using sector duration and spread duration. Its worked example for US high yield (five-year forward, as at 19 November 2025, Bloomberg US Corporate High Yield Index) reads, in our reading of the figure, $6.97 - 2.55 - 0.37 - 0.49 = 3.56\%$. The components sum exactly. The modern desk departs from the identity in two places. Yield to worst already prices the issuer's call option, so a spread-MTM term that assumes bullet behavior double counts the optionality in callable HY. And the high yield index is not the same animal over time (51% BB at year-end 2024 against 37% in 2004, `p.4`), so a spread "mean" estimated over twenty years mixes two different indices.

In the book's notation the identity says that a sector's expected return is its yield, minus the credit losses expected at the *physical* default rate, minus the mark-to-market from the expected moves in Treasury yields and in the sector's own spread. The last piece needs a model of how spreads move over time, and Nuveen's choice of model is the chapter's first stochastic process.

> **Math, Gently.** *A random walk, and a walk on a leash.* A *random walk* is a drunk leaving a bar: each step is random, and the next step has no memory of where he is. Ask where he will be in an hour and the best guess is where he is now, and the longer you wait, the wider the circle he might be in. Equity prices are often modelled this way. Spreads are not, because a spread that has blown out to twice its usual level does not stay there forever; companies refinance, default or recover, and the spread drifts back. Credit spreads look more like the same drunk on an elastic leash tied to a lamppost. Each step is still random, but the farther he wanders from the lamppost, the harder the leash pulls him back. That is an *Ornstein–Uhlenbeck* process. The lamppost is the long-run spread $\bar s$, and $\kappa$ is the stiffness of the leash: the fraction of the remaining gap that is pulled in per year. Because the pull is always proportional to the gap that is left, the expected path closes the gap like a half-life in chemistry, quickly at first and then more and more slowly, and it never overshoots the lamppost. The tiny number: with $\kappa = 0.35$ the half-life is $\ln 2/\kappa \approx 2$ years, so over a horizon of $h = 2$ years the forecast closes $1 - e^{-0.35 \times 2} = 1 - e^{-0.7} \approx 50\%$ of the gap between today's spread and $\bar s$, and over any horizon, however long, never more than 100%. One thing is deliberately missing. Stochastic calculus has a famous correction term, Itô's $\tfrac12\sigma^2$, that appears whenever you take a curved function of a random process. Nothing in this chapter needs it, because the forecast here is the expected path of the spread itself, which is a straight-line calculation; Itô arrives with Merton in Chapter 6. The full version of the expected path is "The Full Derivation: Nuveen's identity in the book's notation" below.

The measures matter here. The yield is a market quantity, and the loss rate is the *physical* one. A risk-neutral loss rate backed out of the spread would make every sector's forecast excess return zero by construction, which is a correct answer to a question nobody asked.

Theo reads the identity twice and laughs. Yield in, losses out, mark-to-market on top: it is his 13-week model, with a five-year horizon and a covariance matrix. He puts the USB stick back in his pocket, slightly vindicated.

Nuveen then feeds these forecasts and a recency-weighted sector covariance into an index-level optimizer with sector bounds. Its best efficient portfolio has a forward Sharpe ratio of 0.40, at a 6.20% return and 6.29% volatility, with high yield constrained to 20–60% and direct lending to 0–35% (`p.5–6`). That implies a cash assumption near 3.7% (our arithmetic). Nuveen leaves execution to the portfolio managers' judgment, including liquidity and "scalability limits" (`p.3`). That is the right call for a sector allocator. It is also why row (iii) of §4.1 has huge capacity and almost no breadth: there are only a handful of sectors to be right about.

#### The Full Derivation: Nuveen's identity in the book's notation

Written with the book's notation, for sector $j$ over horizon $h$ years, annualized,

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[R_j] \approx y_j \;-\; \underbrace{\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_j L_j}_{\text{expected credit loss}} \;-\; \frac{D^{\text{mod}}_j\,\Delta y^{\text{TSY}} + D^{\text{spr}}_j\,\Delta s_j}{h},
\qquad
\Delta s_j = \big(1 - e^{-\kappa_j h}\big)(\bar s_j - s_j), \tag{4.4}
$$

where $\kappa_j$ is the speed at which spreads revert to their long-run level $\bar s_j$ (the expected path of an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck spread, so the forecast cannot overshoot the mean however long $h$ is). The expected path comes from writing the spread as $ds_t = \kappa_j(\bar s_j - s_t)\,dt + \sigma_j\,dW_t$. Taking expectations removes the random term, because a Brownian increment has mean zero, so the expected spread $m(t) = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[s_t]$ satisfies the ordinary differential equation $m'(t) = \kappa_j(\bar s_j - m(t))$. Its solution is $m(h) = \bar s_j + (s_j - \bar s_j)e^{-\kappa_j h}$, and subtracting today's spread gives $\Delta s_j = m(h) - s_j = (1 - e^{-\kappa_j h})(\bar s_j - s_j)$, the second half of (4.4). The first half is the Nuveen extraction term by term: yield in, expected credit loss $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_j L_j$ out, and each mark-to-market term written as duration times the expected change, divided by $h$ to annualize.

#### When the identity works, and when it breaks

The identity is at its best doing what Nuveen built it for: choosing among a handful of credit sectors over a multi-year horizon, where yields are observable, default and recovery forecasts can be argued over in a committee, and the optimizer's job is to keep the allocation sensible rather than to find hidden gems. It is transparent in a way factor models rarely are. Every number in $6.97 - 2.55 - 0.37 - 0.49 = 3.56\%$ can be challenged by someone who has never written a line of code, which is exactly what an investment committee wants.

It breaks in three places, all already flagged in passing. The first is the measure. Plug in the risk-neutral loss rate and the forecast excess return of every sector is zero by construction, so a seasoned allocator checks first where the $\lambda_j$ came from: a default study, or the spread itself. The second is optionality. Yield to worst has already priced the issuer's call, so a spread-MTM term computed as if callable high-yield bonds were bullets counts the same option twice, and the error is largest exactly when spreads are tight and calls are likely. The third is that the lamppost moves. The high-yield index was 37% BB in 2004 and 51% BB at year-end 2024 (`Nuveen_Multi_Asset_Credit_Quant_Framework p.4`), so a long-run $\bar s$ averaged over twenty years ties the leash to a lamppost that has since been carried across the street. A $\bar s$ estimated on a rating-mix-matched history, or a $\bar s$ by rating bucket, is the repair.

## 4.7 Execution: The Revolution That Made the Industry

A dealer run is a price the way a restaurant's menu is a meal. Man says it directly: runs "are not firm and actionable" (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.19`). A credit backtest priced off runs or composites is therefore a backtest of a market where every menu price is guaranteed, the kitchen never runs out, and the waiter does not notice that you only order the specials. Man breaks the realized cost of a trade into three pieces (`p.21`):

$$
\text{TC}_i = \tfrac12\big(P^{\text{ask}}_i - P^{\text{bid}}_i\big) + \text{slip}_i(\text{size}, \text{characteristics}) + \text{calib}_i(\text{dealer fade}),
$$

the inside half-spread, the slippage that grows with clip size and bond characteristics, and a calibration term for the gap between the quotes a model sees and the levels dealers actually honor. The third term is the one an equity quant forgets. It is the dealer reading your order flow, the hand history Ines keeps telling Theo about.

> **Math, Gently.** *A cost is a function, not a number.* Back to the restaurant. The half-spread is the price on the menu: what anyone pays for one plate. Slippage is the surcharge for ordering for forty people at once, because the kitchen has to buy more fish and the price of fish goes up when it does. The fade is the kitchen noticing that you only ever order the specials, the dishes it priced to clear before they spoil, and quietly charging you more for them. Each piece depends on something different: the first on the bond, the second on the size of the order, the third on *who you are*. That is why a single "cost" number is never right for long. The tiny number *(illustrative, HY)*: a half-spread of 25bp, slippage of 10bp at a \$2mm clip and a fade of 15bp add up to 50bp a trade, which is about what a slow value signal hopes to earn on a bond over its entire holding period. The zero that Kaz typed into the cost parameter at 7:40 a.m. was not a simplification. It was the whole edge. The full version of each piece is in Chapters 18–19, where the fade becomes adverse selection with a formula.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.19:* "We cannot overstate the importance of transaction cost and liquidity modelling for quant credit participants, particularly in a market where spreads are at such universally tight levels and where transaction costs can easily wipe out the potential profitability of a trade."
>
> **Annotation.** This sentence explains the silent cells in the execution column of the map. A shop that says nothing about costs either has no cost model or considers it its moat, and in credit the second is often true. The modern desk departs from the passage in one respect: costs are no longer a constant to estimate but a state variable to forecast. They rise in stress, when dealers pull balance sheet and the electronic liquidity layer thins, which is exactly when a contrarian systematic book wants to trade. Man also notes that the bid–ask estimates still used in many academic papers, Chen, Lesmond and Wei's 25bp for AAA and 180bp for CCC (2007), are stale (`p.20`). A 2026 cost model starts from the desk's own fills, not from a 2007 table.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The dealer, and the non-bank liquidity provider.* From the other side of the RFQ, the three terms of Man's cost formula are not costs. They are the dealer's revenue, its insurance premium and its memory. The half-spread pays for the balance sheet a bank desk commits when it takes a bond into inventory. The slippage is what a larger clip costs to warehouse and lay off. And the calibration term, the fade, is the dealer's running estimate of whether the client on the other end knows something. A credit-trading desk at a large dealer (the seat that firms such as Jefferies and Bank of America occupy; this box attributes no view to either) keeps that estimate client by client, from markouts: where each bond traded an hour, a day and a week after the fill. In recent years the bank desks have had company. Non-bank liquidity providers, the electronic market-making firms that grew up in equities and Treasuries, earned about 10% of US credit market-making revenue in 2024 (Coalition Greenwich, January 2025). In October 2026 Citadel Securities said in a whitepaper that, by its own estimates, it held a 14.2% share of investment-grade credit block risk transfer (The DESK, 8 October 2026; self-reported, and the rankings it cites are not public). What the buy side says about the newcomers is more conditional. At a FILS Europe panel reported by The DESK in mid-October 2026, buy-side traders said that platforms and electronic liquidity providers "help more in quiet markets," and a trader at Swiss Life Asset Managers described how "when it becomes very stressful, you see clearly a concentration to the usual banks" (The DESK, 14–15 October 2026). From the dealer's seat a systematic book is a client with a recognizable pattern: many small lines, rebalances on predictable dates, and a habit of being right about value slowly. Each habit has a price, and the price shows up in the fade.

"Here's what they see when they look at us," Ines says, and draws it on the back of a routing printout. It is the four scores Man assigns its dealers, turned around. *Availability*: do we show up when a dealer needs to lay off risk, or only when we want something? *Quality*: an hour after we trade with them, has the price moved against them? If it has, we are informed, and informed flow gets wider prices. *Completion*: when we send an RFQ, do we trade, or do we use their quote to price someone else? *Size*: do we send \$2mm clips that fit their book, or \$40mm blocks they have to warehouse? "Every dealer has a version of this card for every client," she says. "Ours says we're slow, polite and right about cheap bonds. That's worth a lot on a quiet day. On a bad day the polite clients are the first ones they stop answering."

### Break-even turnover

Let $T$ be one-way annual turnover: the fraction of the book sold, and replaced, each year. Every dollar of turnover is a sale and a purchase, so with average cost $\bar c$ per dollar traded the annual cost drag is $2T\bar c$, and

$$
\alpha_{\text{net}} = \alpha_{\text{gross}} - 2T\bar c \;\ge\; 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T \;\le\; T_{\max} = \frac{\alpha_{\text{gross}}}{2\,\bar c}. \tag{4.6}
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *Two haircuts per dollar of turnover.* Turnover is how much of the book you replace in a year. Replacing a dollar of bonds means selling a dollar (one haircut) and buying a dollar of something else (a second haircut), so every unit of turnover costs twice the average one-way cost. The edge has to pay for all the haircuts and still have something left. Tiny number *(illustrative)*: a strategy with 4% a year of gross alpha and a one-way cost of 40bp pays $2 \times 0.40\% = 0.80\%$ for every full turn of the book, so it can afford $4\% / 0.80\% = 5$ turns a year before the haircuts take everything. At 6 turns it pays the dealers to run it. The full version is (4.6) itself, which is that sentence in symbols.

Boundary checks: with free trading, $T_{\max} = \infty$, and with no edge, $T_{\max} = 0$. Barclays gives a realistic calibration. Its combined value and equity-momentum IG book earns 2.66% a year gross and 1.78% a year net of rebalancing costs over 2007–2019, so costs take 0.88% a year, about a third of the edge. Tracking error barely moves (`Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.711`), so the information ratio falls in proportion, from $2.66/1.41 = 1.89$ to $1.78/1.41 = 1.26$. Costs were 1.15% a year in 2007–2012 and 0.64% in 2013–2019. The signals did not change between the two periods. The market got cheaper to trade.

Now apply (4.6) to the momentum family, which has the highest turnover on the menu (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.19`). A long-only HY momentum tilt with 4% gross alpha and $\bar c = 40$bp *(illustrative)* breaks even at $T_{\max} = 0.04/0.008 = 5$, or 500% one-way a year. A monthly-rebalanced signal with short holding periods can turn over more than that. This is how the 15.9% before-cost figure in §4.4 becomes a much smaller number, or a negative one, in a live book.

#### When break-even turnover is a ceiling, and when it is a mirage

(4.6) is the most useful single line in a strategy review because it turns an argument about costs into a number anyone can check. Its weakness is the bar over the $c$. The formula assumes an *average* cost, constant through the year, and credit costs are not constant. They are low in calm months and can double or worse in stressed ones, and a systematic book does not trade evenly across months either; a contrarian or momentum book trades most when spreads move most, which is when costs are highest. Barclays' own record shows the drift at the scale of years, 1.15% a year of costs in 2007–2012 against 0.64% in 2013–2019 for the same signals. Within a single bad half-year the swing can be larger, as the case study below shows. The check an experienced PM runs is to recompute $T_{\max}$ with the cost of the months in which the strategy actually traded, weighted by how much it traded in each, rather than the calendar average.

The second weakness is the $T$. The formula treats turnover as a choice, and much of it is not. Bonds mature, are called, are tendered for, fall out of the index on a downgrade or enter it at issuance, and a benchmark-relative book has to follow its benchmark through all of it. Barclays' chapter on waiting to buy new issues exists because index inclusion alone forces trades at predictable, and therefore expensive, moments. A strategy whose *voluntary* turnover sits comfortably below $T_{\max}$ can still breach it once the involuntary turnover is added, and the involuntary part arrives on dates everyone else can also read in the index rules.

### Case Study: Man Numeric's Execution Migration, 2021–2023

The cleanest public record of a firm moving along the execution axis is Man's own. In its telling, 2021 was the year of portfolio trading: tier-one and tier-two dealers offered high fill rates and aggressive pricing on lists. In the first half of 2022, as quantitative tightening and volatility hit, portfolio-trading costs rose. Dealers pulled balance sheet, and the custom ETF creation baskets that had let them warehouse lists cheaply were no longer available. Electronic venues held up best (`Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023 p.2–5`).

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023 p.3:* "We have therefore moved from having roughly 30% of our order flow in high yield and 60% in investment grade going through electronic venues, to over 90% of our order flow going through electronic-trading venues."
>
> **Annotation.** Note what did *not* change: the signals. Man's alpha families are the same in the 2023 paper as in the 2022 one. What changed was the router and the cost model, and that moved the firm further along the execution axis than any signal research could have. The routing analytics Man describes score each RFQ counterparty on availability, participant quality, completion rate and the size it can digest (`p.5`). That is a markout table with a business card attached. The modern desk departs from the passage on one point. A shift that was rational in the calm of 2023 deserves a stress test, because buy-side traders now report that platform and non-bank liquidity "help more in quiet markets" and that flow returns to the banks in stress (The DESK, 14–15 October 2026).

Ines reads the routing list and nods. "Availability, quality, completion, size. That's how you seat a poker table. Who shows up, who's good, who folds, and how big a pot they can stand. The difference is that the dealers are keeping the same notes on you."

#### The year portfolio trading broke

*(A fictional composite. The desk, its rebalance, its quotes and its numbers are invented and illustrative; the market events, the Man papers and the regulatory dates are real and cited.)*

Ines tells the story the way she tells every story that cost money: as a hand history, with the file open. On Theo's fourth Tuesday she pulls up the desk's archive and finds the rebalance for June 2022.

"Before you were here," she says. "Before Kaz trusted me. Sit."

The background is the part Theo half-remembers from the other side of the street. In 2021 the portfolio trade had been the best deal in credit. A manager could send a dealer a list of a few hundred bonds, buys and sells mixed, and get one price for the whole basket, often tighter than the sum of what the same bonds would have cost one at a time. Man's 2023 paper calls 2021 "the year of portfolio trading" (`Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023 p.5`), and the reason dealers could afford to be generous was plumbing. A dealer who bought a basket of corporate bonds could, in the right conditions, deliver many of them into a bond ETF's creation basket in exchange for ETF shares and sell the shares on an exchange the same afternoon. The ETF was a pressure valve. A dealer with a valve can take risk it does not want, because it knows where the exit is.

There had been one warning that the valve could jam. In March and April 2020, when the bond market seized, bond-ETF tracking error, normally about 0.7bp, rose above 200bp for some funds: the ETFs strayed from their benchmarks, because the arbitrage that normally keeps ETF and bonds together needs dealers with balance sheet, and dealers had none to spare (Todorov, "The anatomy of bond ETF arbitrage," *BIS Quarterly Review*, March 2021). The valve worked in reverse for a few weeks, and then the Federal Reserve arrived and everyone forgot.

In the first half of 2022 the valve narrowed again, more quietly. The Federal Reserve began shrinking its balance sheet, volatility rose, and in Man's account the new environment "may also have left dealers inclined to commit less of their balance sheets to bond trading," while "the loss of customised baskets for the ETF creation/redemption process is likely to have been another aggravating factor" (`Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023 p.3`). Man's credit co-heads, looking back on their firm's podcast in March 2024, described the same moment in plainer words: the change in the ETF process made it harder for dealer desks to recycle risk, and transaction costs generally went up substantially. A manager who had been sending baskets all through 2021 noticed this as a number on a screen, and that number is what Ines has open.

"Here's the hand," she says.

The desk's June rebalance is a list of 400 lines, about \$600mm of notional, two-thirds investment grade and one-third high yield, roughly balanced between buys and sells. The model's expected gain from making the trades, the extra alpha of the target portfolio over the current one across the holding period, is about 45bp of the notional traded. In April the desk had sent a list of similar size and shape as a portfolio trade and paid about 20bp all in. In June the same kind of list, sent to the same three dealers, comes back at about 60bp. The cheapest of the three is three times April's price, and one dealer declines to quote at all.

"Three numbers," Ines says. "Forty-five to gain. Sixty to trade. You don't need Kaz to do that subtraction."

But Kaz, in June 2022, had done a different subtraction, and Ines makes Theo hear it out properly, because it was not stupid. Kaz's argument was about decay. The signals that generated the list do not keep forever. The value scores are slow, but part of the list was there because of the momentum sleeve and an equity-to-credit lead–lag signal, and those are fast: if the information in them is half gone in a month, the alpha of the rebalance shrinks every day the desk waits. The 60bp is a one-off; the forgone alpha compounds. And unfilled orders are not neutral. Man warns that they "can lead to unintended and potentially unmodelled risk exposures" (`Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.19`), and a half-executed rebalance, with the sells done and the buys not, leaves the book short duration and long cash against a benchmark that is neither. "Pay it," Kaz had said. "It's ugly, but it's one number, and we know what it is."

Ines's own argument in June 2022 was the one she still makes. The basket price is a dealer's price for *all* the risk on the list, which means it is priced for the worst lines on the list. The IG half of the book was a set of liquid, recently issued bonds that several dealers wanted, and on the RFQ venues those bonds were still trading at something close to April's costs. It was the high-yield tail, sixty or seventy lines of bonds that traded a few times a week, that the dealers were charging for. So: break the basket. Send the liquid lines one at a time, or in small groups, as RFQs to the counterparties who score best on the four things Man's routing analytics measure: whether the bond is actually available, whether the counterparty is a high-quality participant, how often its responses turn into completed trades, and whether the size is digestible in RFQ form (`Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023 p.5`). Keep the hard lines for targeted requests to the two dealers who had been axed in them that month, and accept that some would not fill.

Mags's argument was the shortest. "Wait a week." The value signals are slow, she said, the market in June 2022 was frightened rather than broken, and frightened prices come back. "I've sold bonds into a frightened market. You get a price. You don't get a good one. Nobody ever got fired for doing a rebalance on the eighth instead of the first."

Ines closes the file. "So. You're me, in June 2022. Pay, slice, or wait?"

**Decision point.** The rebalance gains about 45bp if it is done. A portfolio trade costs about 60bp today, three times April's price. Slicing the list into RFQs costs less on the liquid lines and leaves an uncertain number of the illiquid ones unfilled, with the risk exposures that come with them. Waiting costs whatever the fast signals lose in a week, and bets that the cost spike is temporary. What would you do, and why?

Theo thinks about it the way he would think about a tender offer with a consent deadline. Before answering, he wants three things: how much of the 45bp sits in the fast signals and how much in the slow ones; what each half of the list costs to trade by itself; and how much of the book's tracking error a partial fill leaves behind. Ines grins, because those are the right questions and because she had none of those numbers in June 2022.

What she had was a spreadsheet and an afternoon, and what she built with them is the shape of the answer that this section and §4.8 make formal. She split the list in two. The IG lines, about 270 of them, carried about two-thirds of the notional and about 60% of the expected gain, and on the RFQ venues they cost roughly 15bp to trade. The HY lines carried the other 40% of the gain and cost, by her estimate, 60 to 80bp one at a time, which is to say the basket price was mostly their price. On the IG lines, a 15bp cost against an expected gain of about 40bp per unit traded was an easy yes. On the HY lines the average expected gain, a little over 50bp per unit, was underwater at any protocol, so the desk traded only the HY lines whose expected gain was large enough to clear their own cost, about a third of them, mostly the ones driven by fast signals, and let the rest wait for July. The book's tracking error rose a little for a month, which the risk budget could absorb. The total cost came to about 25bp on what was traded, against about 60bp for the basket. The desk gave up perhaps a fifth of the rebalance's expected gain and saved more than two-thirds of its cost; sent as one basket, the same rebalance would have been expected to lose money *(illustrative)*.

"So, slice," Theo says.

"Slice *and* wait," Ines says. "And pay, a little, for the lines that were worth it. Kaz was right about the fast signals and Mags was right about the slow ones. The mistake would have been treating a 400-line list as one decision."

Two things about that afternoon matter more than the answer, and both turn up again before the chapter ends. The first is that the cost of trading was not a parameter. It was a state of the market, and it had tripled in two months without any change in the bonds. A cost model that carries one number per bond, calibrated on 2021, would have told the desk that June 2022 was a normal month to rebalance. The second is that the decision ran bond by bond, on alpha per unit of cost, which is break-even turnover (4.6) applied line by line rather than to the book as a whole. A line is worth trading when its own expected gain clears its own round-trip cost, and the June numbers made that test bite on the illiquid lines first.

The real firm in this story drew the same conclusion at a larger scale. Man reports that it moved from about 30% of its order flow in high yield and 60% in investment grade going through electronic venues to over 90% (`Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023 p.3`), and that "the vast majority of our trades are now sent as RFQs, leading to significant savings in transaction costs versus portfolio trades" (`p.5`). Its 2023 paper calls 2022 "the year of electronic trading," and notes that "some of the biggest jumps in electronic trading have come during the most stressful times in credit trading, such as 2020 and 2022" (`p.5`). The signals did not change. The router did.

A year later the market itself started keeping score. From 15 May 2023, FINRA required a portfolio-trade modifier on TRACE reports for trades between two parties covering at least ten unique issues at a single basket price (FINRA Regulatory Notice 22-12). For the first time a desk could see, in public data, how much of the market was trading in baskets and at what prices, and could go back to its own June-2022-shaped months and check whether its basket quotes had been fair. Ines did. They mostly were. The dealers had not been gouging. They had been pricing a valve that no longer opened as wide.

## 4.8 Capacity: How Big Before You Are the Market

Alpha is a hot tub. The more people in it, the less it is about the water. Every pitch book has a capacity slide, and almost none has a capacity derivation. Here is one.

Let $\alpha_0$ be gross alpha per dollar per year, $T$ one-way annual turnover, $c_0$ the half-spread cost per dollar traded, and let market impact per dollar traded be $\eta\sqrt{2TA/\mathcal{L}}$. Here $A$ is AUM, $2TA$ is the dollar volume the strategy trades in a year, and $\mathcal{L}$ is the annual dollar liquidity available *in the names the signal actually selects*, so $2TA/\mathcal{L}$ is the participation rate and $\eta$ is the square-root impact coefficient.

> **Math, Gently.** *Why impact goes with the square root.* Selling a bond in size moves its price against you, and the obvious guess is that twice the size does twice the damage. The evidence says otherwise, most carefully measured in equities and, in the thinner evidence available, in credit as well: the damage per dollar grows roughly with the *square root* of the size. Doubling an order raises the impact per dollar by about 41% ($\sqrt 2 \approx 1.41$), not 100%, and quadrupling it doubles the impact. One way to see why: the first few million of an order find the natural buyers who were already looking for the bond; the next few million have to coax out people who were not, at a lower price; the last dollar has to persuade someone who had no plans to buy at all. Each extra dollar costs more than the one before, but as the price moves the pool of potential buyers deepens, so the cost rises more slowly than the size. The square root is the curve that fits that shape. Tiny number *(illustrative)*: with $\eta = 0.02$, trading 1% of a year's available liquidity in your names costs about $0.02 \times \sqrt{0.01} = 0.2\%$ per dollar traded, and trading 4% costs $0.02 \times \sqrt{0.04} = 0.4\%$: twice the cost per dollar for four times the volume. §11.9 puts the same curve to work on systematic high yield, and Chapter 19 prices it into portfolio trades.

Net alpha and dollar alpha are

$$
\alpha(A) = \alpha_0 - 2T\Big[c_0 + \eta\sqrt{2TA/\mathcal{L}}\Big], \qquad \Pi(A) = A\,\alpha(A).
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *Finding the top of a hill with a derivative.* Picture the manager's dollar alpha $\Pi(A)$ as a hill, plotted against assets. A tiny book earns almost the full edge on almost no money, so it makes few dollars. A huge book has impacted most of the edge away. Somewhere in between, the dollars peak. A derivative is the slope of the hill under your feet: positive on the way up, negative on the way down, and exactly zero at the top. So the size that makes the most dollars is the one where the slope $\Pi'(A)$ is zero: one more dollar of assets adds exactly as much from the bigger base as it costs in extra impact on everything already in the book. Tiny number, from the worked example below: the top of the hill is at about \$1.85bn, where net alpha is 40bp, a third of the 120bp a very small book would earn; for a fast momentum signal in the same universe the hill peaks at about \$134mm. The full version is "The Full Derivation: the capacity maximum under square-root impact" below.

Write $g \equiv \alpha_0 - 2Tc_0$ for the gross alpha left after the spread, which is what a very small book earns. The top of the hill, derived step by step below, is at

$$
A^{*} = \frac{\mathcal{L}\,g^{2}}{18\,T^{3}\eta^{2}}, \qquad \alpha(A^{*}) = \frac{g}{3}. \tag{4.2}
$$

Ines keeps a pitch book on the corner of her desk for exactly this formula, a glossy one from a manager she will not name, with a capacity slide that says "\$5bn+" next to an asterisk *(the pitch book is fictional; the asterisk is universal)*. When Theo shows her (4.2), she opens the book to that slide and asks it three questions, in the voice she uses on dealers.

The first question is who gets paid on what, and the answer is the one-third rule. At the size that maximizes total dollar alpha, the client keeps one-third of the spread-net edge. A manager paid a share of dollar alpha chooses $A^{*}$. A manager paid only on assets is happy to grow past it, all the way to where net alpha hits zero. That happens when impact equals $g$, at $A_0 = \tfrac94 A^{*}$. The client, who cares about net alpha per dollar, would prefer $A \to 0$. "So the asterisk," Ines says, "is where the three of us stop agreeing." That principal–agent gap sits behind every "capacity" asterisk.

The second question is what the turnover is, and here the answer is a cube. Holding $g$ fixed, capacity scales as $T^{-3}$: double the turnover and the book can be one-eighth the size. Ines flips to the strategy page, finds a momentum sleeve, and draws a line through the \$5bn. This is why the high-turnover momentum family is a small-capacity business even when its gross numbers are spectacular.

The third question is liquidity *in what*. The slide's footnote divides by index ADV, and index ADV is the wrong denominator. Signals select the bonds that are cheap, which skews toward bonds that are cheap *because* they are hard to trade. Paleologo warns that liquidity can invalidate a sizing analysis (`Paleologo_Advanced_Portfolio_Management p.145`). In credit that is the default, not the exception. "Show me the volume in the bonds the signal actually buys," Ines tells the pitch book, which does not answer.

A worked example *(illustrative)*. An HY long/short multi-factor book has $\alpha_0 = 150$bp, $T = 0.6$, $c_0 = 25$bp, $\eta = 0.02$ and $\mathcal{L} = \$20$bn a year. Then $g = 120$bp, $A^{*} = 20{,}000 \times 0.012^2 / (18 \times 0.216 \times 0.0004) \approx \$1.85$bn, net alpha at $A^{*}$ is 40bp, and net alpha reaches zero at about \$4.2bn. Give the same universe an equity-momentum signal with $\alpha_0 = 4\%$ and $T = 2.5$ and $g = 2.75\%$, more than twice as much, yet $A^{*}$ falls to about \$134mm. The hot tub got hotter and much smaller.

#### The Full Derivation: the capacity maximum under square-root impact

With $g \equiv \alpha_0 - 2Tc_0$, dollar alpha is $\Pi(A) = gA - 2T\eta\sqrt{2T/\mathcal{L}}\,A^{3/2}$. Setting $\Pi'(A) = g - 3T\eta\sqrt{2T/\mathcal{L}}\,A^{1/2} = 0$ gives $A^{1/2} = g/\big(3T\eta\sqrt{2T/\mathcal{L}}\big)$, and squaring, $A^{*} = \mathcal{L}g^2/(18T^3\eta^2)$, the first half of (4.2). The second result follows because impact at $A^{*}$ is $2T\eta\sqrt{2TA^{*}/\mathcal{L}} = 2T\eta \cdot g/(3T\eta) = 2g/3$, so net alpha is $g - 2g/3 = g/3$. Net alpha reaches zero when the impact term equals $g$, which requires $\sqrt{A}$ to be $3/2$ times its value at $A^{*}$, so $A_0 = \tfrac94 A^{*}$. The second derivative, $\Pi''(A) = -\tfrac32 T\eta\sqrt{2T/\mathcal{L}}\,A^{-1/2} < 0$, confirms that $A^{*}$ is the top of the hill and not the bottom of a valley.

#### Measuring capacity from your own fills

Every symbol in (4.2) is easy to write and hard to know, and the two hardest are $\mathcal{L}$ and $\eta$. No vendor screen shows the liquidity available in the particular bonds a signal will select next quarter, or the impact coefficient of a particular desk trading them. So Ines stops estimating them from the market and starts measuring them from the desk, with three series that are each boring and each kept every day.

The first is impact by size bucket, from the desk's own transaction-cost analysis. Every fill is compared with the mid just before the RFQ went out and with the prints an hour and a day later, and the costs are grouped by clip size, from under \$1mm up to blocks of \$10mm and more. A square-root curve fitted to those buckets is the desk's own $\eta$, for its own bonds, in its own months, and it is usually not the number in the pitch book. The second is the participation rate: the desk's traded volume over a window divided by TRACE volume in the same bonds over the same window, which is $2TA/\mathcal{L}$ measured rather than assumed. The third is a "we are the market" detector, the desk's share of TRACE volume in each name it traded that month. When that share gets large, the desk's marks start to be its own trades, and the next rebalance moves the very price it will be measured against. Next to the three series the desk keeps the crowding signals of §9.10 as a monitored series of their own, because capacity is shared with everyone running the same signal.

The formula is good for planning: it tells a manager roughly where the hill is and how fast it falls away. It is weak in exactly the places the measurements are strong. $\mathcal{L}$ is a state variable, like the costs of §4.7, and it shrinks in stress, which moves the top of the hill toward zero on the days the book most wants to trade. And $\mathcal{L}$ is shared. A strategy's capacity is not its own; it is what is left after every other book chasing the same cheap bonds has taken its share. The question an experienced risk manager asks of any capacity number is therefore not "how big can we get?" but "how many days of our own measured volume would it take to get out, in a month like June 2022?"

#### Barclays' discrete method: the ownership cap

Barclays offers a discrete method you can audit, which is rarer than it should be.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit p.207:* "we assume that the dollar holding in any issuer is no more than 5% of its market value while the sizes of the fallen angels' portfolios vary from \$125mn to \$2bn."
>
> **Annotation.** Write the rule as weights. With $n$ eligible issuers, an issuer limit $w_{\max}$ and a cap of $c$ times issuer market value $MV_i$,
>
> $$w_i = \min\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{n},\; w_{\max},\; \tfrac{c\,MV_i}{A}\Big), \qquad f_{\text{cash}}(A) = 1 - \sum_i w_i . \tag{4.3}$$
>
> The portfolio depends on $c/A$ alone, so a \$500mn book at a 5% cap is the same book as a \$250mn one at 2.5%. As $A$ grows, the book drifts from equal weights toward market-value weights and the uninvested fraction rises. Barclays finds a \$1bn three-month-reversal fallen-angel book only 53% invested in August 2004, against 98% for a buy-all version (`p.207–208`). The modern desk departs from the passage in one respect. The 5% cap is a holdings constraint, and (4.2) is a trading-cost constraint, and a real book hits both. The cap also binds hardest in exactly the issuers where an RSSG reader would want to be large: small, stressed and mispriced.

> **Math, Gently.** *The smallest of three ceilings.* The $\min(\cdot)$ in (4.3) just says that each issuer's weight is capped by three ceilings at once (equal weight, the issuer limit, and 5% of the issuer's bonds) and the lowest one wins: with $n = 100$ issuers, $w_{\max} = 2\%$ and a \$400mm issuer in a \$2.5bn book, the ceilings are 1%, 2% and $0.05 \times 400/2{,}500 = 0.8\%$, so the cap binds at 0.8% and the missing 0.2% sits in cash.

### Specification for AI Implementation

**What ROBO built from the vague version.** Spec: "maximize breadth." ROBO bought all 47 bonds of one issuer, reported $BR = 47$, and filed a note that diversification was excellent. Precise spec: breadth is $BR_{\text{eff}}$ from (4.1), with $\rho$ estimated from within-issuer residual returns, and both $N$ and $BR_{\text{eff}}$ are reported.

**Module:** `capacity_breadth_engine.py`, numpy only, seeded, four blocks that run in order. It scores any row of the §4.3 map.

- **Units.** Money in \$mm. Rates, alphas and costs are annual decimals (0.015 is 150bp). `turnover` is one-way. `cost0` is the half-spread per dollar traded. `eta` is impact per dollar traded at 100% participation. `liquidity` is annual dollar volume in the names the signal selects, not index ADV.
- **Shapes.** `mv: float64[n]` issuer market values; `aum_grid: float64[G]`; curve functions return dicts of arrays of length `G`.
- **Guards.** Raise if `n_bonds < n_issuers` or `rho` is outside $[0, 1]$; floor `liquidity` and `aum` at `1e-12`; take `sqrt(max(., 0))`; return $A^{*} = 0$ when $g \le 0$ (costs eat the edge at any size); combine caps with `np.minimum.reduce`; floor $f_{\text{cash}}$ at zero against rounding.
- **Sanity asserts.** (a) $\rho = 1$ gives $BR_{\text{eff}} = M$ and $\rho = 0$ gives $N$. (b) The numeric argmax of $\Pi(A)$ is within 1% of (4.2), and $\alpha(A^{*}) = g/3$ to 1e-9. (c) $\alpha(A \to 0) \to g$. (d) Tiny AUM gives equal weights and no cash; huge AUM gives weights proportional to market value. (e) Weights depend only on $c/A$.

Return to ROBO and its forty-seven bonds. Told to "maximize breadth," it counted CUSIPs, found a very large family of them under one issuer, and filed a note praising the diversification. A restructuring banker would never make that mistake, because years of watching one capital structure trade as a single claim on a single enterprise value leave a reflex: same issuer, same bet. ROBO has no reflexes, only the spec. So the reflex has to be written down as a function, one that states the discount for family ties and refuses inputs that make no sense. A desk wants that function early and in one place, because every later number in the pipeline, from the information ratio to the sizing of the book, inherits whatever breadth it is given.

Block 1 holds two small functions. `effective_breadth` takes the bond count, the issuer count and the within-issuer residual correlation `rho`, and returns equation (4.1): $N/(1+(m-1)\rho)$, with $m$ the average number of bonds per issuer. Two guards run first. It raises if there are fewer bonds than issuers, which no real universe allows and which in practice means a broken feed, and it raises if `rho` falls outside zero to one, so a correlation stored as 70 instead of 0.7 stops the run instead of producing a negative breadth. `fundamental_law_ir` then multiplies the information coefficient, the square root of effective breadth (floored at zero before the root) and the transfer coefficient. Both outputs are annual and gross of costs. The four asserts at the bottom are the boundary checks of §4.3 made executable: `rho` of one collapses 1,800 bonds to 800 issuers, `rho` of zero leaves 1,800, ROBO's 47 bonds of one issuer are one bet, and `rho` of 0.7 returns the 960 of the worked example.

Use it to score a row of the map before arguing about its alpha. Do not mistake it for an estimator: `rho` is an input, and the listing does not fit it from residual returns, so the number that moves the answer most is the one you must bring. The edge that bites is the word "average" in $m$. The formula treats every issuer as having the same number of bonds. Real universes are lumpy, with a few giant issuers carrying dozens of bonds and most carrying one or two. Within-issuer covariance grows with the square of family size, so lumpiness makes the true clustering worse than the average implies, and the function overstates breadth. Read its output as a ceiling, and for a lumpy universe sum $m_j(m_j-1)$ issuer by issuer instead of using the mean.

## 4.9 Risk: Defense Without Beta

Every HY manager claims to be defensive. BlackRock's systematic HY note checks the claim with capture ratios. Define upside and downside capture over months $t$ in which the market return $r_{m,t}$ is positive or negative:

$$
UC = \frac{\sum_{t:\,r_{m,t}>0} r_{p,t}}{\sum_{t:\,r_{m,t}>0} r_{m,t}}, \qquad DC = \frac{\sum_{t:\,r_{m,t}<0} r_{p,t}}{\sum_{t:\,r_{m,t}<0} r_{m,t}} .
$$

> **Math, Gently.** *A line through the months.* Plot every month as a dot, with the index's return along the bottom and the manager's return up the side. If the manager mostly owns the market with a twist, the dots form a cloud around a straight line, and laying a ruler through the cloud is all that $r_p = a + \beta r_m$ means. The slope of the ruler, $\beta$, is how much of the market's move the manager takes; a slope of 0.9 means 90%. The height of the ruler where the market return is zero, $a$, is what the manager earns in a month when the market does nothing, which is the only honest name for selection alpha. Capture ratios read that same ruler on the two halves of the picture separately, the up months to the right of zero and the down months to the left. A ruler sloping at 0.9 and passing slightly above zero gives an up-capture a little above 0.9, because the alpha helps in up months, and a down-capture a little below 0.9, because the alpha cushions the down months. Tiny number: BlackRock's average manager, at 92.24% up and 89.56% down, sits on a ruler with a slope of about 0.91 and a height of about 2.1bp a month (the annotation below does the arithmetic). Nearly all of the "defense" is the slope. The full version is "The Full Derivation: capture ratios under a linear model" below.

If the manager earns $r_{p,t} = a + \beta r_{m,t}$, with $a$ the selection alpha per month, the up months average $\bar u > 0$ and the down months average $-\bar d < 0$, the two capture ratios and the average excess return over the index come out as

$$
UC = \beta + \frac{a}{\bar u}, \qquad DC = \beta - \frac{a}{\bar d}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[r_p - r_m] = a + (\beta - 1)\,\mathbb{E}[r_m]. \tag{4.7}
$$

A pure beta tilt ($a = 0$) has $UC = DC = \beta$. It looks defensive in every down month and loses $(1-\beta)\mathbb{E}[r_m]$ on average, because a positive credit premium means up months win the long run. Defense that survives the rebound has to come from $a$, which in HY mostly means not owning the defaulters.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *BlackRock_Systematic_High_Yield_Credit p.3:* "the average manager tended to participate in 89.56% of down markets and 92.24% of up markets."
>
> **Annotation.** Feed the two numbers into (4.7). The paper's window (2005–2025) has 168 up months and 72 down months (`p.3–4`). Assume average up and down months of +1.3% and −1.9% *(illustrative)*. Subtracting the capture equations gives $a(1/\bar u + 1/\bar d) = 0.0268$, so $a \approx 2.1$bp a month and $\beta \approx 0.91$. The market averages about 0.34% a month on those assumptions, so the beta drag is about 3.2bp a month and the average manager nets roughly −1bp a month against the index. The modern desk reads the passage as BlackRock intends: the average active HY manager's "defense" is mostly a smaller long. A 0.9 beta is not defense, and with it stripped out, about 2bp a month is what is left. IDO or it didn't happen.

The selection that is left, in BlackRock's telling, is default avoidance. Its PD model "has successfully identified 77% of the defaulted bonds in the ICE BofA High Yield Index within 24 months of its default since 2010" (`BlackRock_Systematic_High_Yield_Credit p.6`). Theo reads this the way he would read a lender's recovery analysis: 77% is a *recall*. A screen that flags every bond in the index has a recall of 100% and costs you the whole spread of the innocent. The number that has to come next is the flag rate, the share of non-defaulters excluded, because every innocent excluded is carry forgone. Theo has learned to ask for both before being impressed.

> **Math, Gently.** *Recall, and the flag rate.* "77% of defaulters caught" is half of a number. A smoke alarm that rings every day catches every fire, and nobody calls it a good alarm, because a false alarm has a cost; here, every bond flagged and excluded is carry the book did not earn. Two numbers describe any screen. *Recall* is the share of the real defaults it flagged. The *flag rate* is the share of the whole universe it flagged, innocents included. Tiny number *(illustrative)*: in a universe of 1,800 bonds with 30 defaults a year, a model that flags 300 bonds and catches 23 of the 30 has a recall of 77%, and it also sidelines 277 bonds that did not default, along with their carry. Whether that is a good trade depends on what the 7 missed defaults cost against what the 277 innocents would have paid, and that comparison is the number worth asking for. The full version, with the four-cell table and precision alongside recall, is in §7.5.

Blackstone adds a third idea that belongs on the risk axis, not the alpha axis: contrarian rebalancing (`Blackstone_Systematic_Credit p.1`). An optimizer with fixed risk targets mechanically adds spread risk after spreads gap wider and trims it after they tighten. That is a policy, not a forecast, and what it harvests is the liquidity term of (4.5): the premium for buying when forced sellers are selling. It is also a crowded trade, because every systematic book with a DTS budget wants to rebalance on the same day.

#### The Full Derivation: capture ratios under a linear model

Let the manager earn $r_{p,t} = a + \beta r_{m,t}$, where $a$ is selection alpha per month. With $n_u$ up months averaging $\bar u > 0$ and $n_d$ down months averaging $-\bar d < 0$, the up-capture sums the manager's returns over the up months and divides by the market's: $UC = \sum_{\text{up}}(a + \beta r_{m,t}) / \sum_{\text{up}} r_{m,t} = (n_u a + \beta n_u \bar u)/(n_u \bar u) = \beta + a/\bar u$. The same steps over the down months give $DC = (n_d a - \beta n_d \bar d)/(-n_d \bar d) = \beta - a/\bar d$. The excess return over the index is $r_p - r_m = a + (\beta - 1) r_m$, and taking expectations gives the last term of (4.7). Subtracting the two capture equations eliminates $\beta$ and leaves $UC - DC = a(1/\bar u + 1/\bar d)$, which is the step the BlackRock annotation uses to back out $a$; substituting $a$ into either equation gives $\beta$.

#### When capture ratios tell the truth, and when they flatter

Capture ratios are a good first question to ask a high-yield manager because they are hard to fake over a long window and easy to read. They need two things to tell the truth. The first is enough down months. BlackRock's twenty-year window has 72 of them, spread across several separate sell-offs, which is enough for the slope and the height of the ruler to be told apart; a five-year track record with fifteen down months can show almost any capture pair by luck. The second is that "up" and "down" are defined before anyone looks at the manager. Split the months by the index, as BlackRock does, not by some regime the manager picked afterwards, or the ruler is being laid through a cloud the manager chose.

Mags adds the part that capture ratios cannot see. "Down months aren't all the same month," she says. A manager can have a lovely down-capture across seventy-two ordinary bad months and still own the one bond that goes to 20 in the one month that matters. Default avoidance is a claim about the left tail of individual bonds, and capture ratios average it away. Hence the recall-and-flag-rate question above: it is asked bond by bond.

Contrarian rebalancing has its own judgment call. It works because it harvests the liquidity term of (4.5), buying from forced sellers when spreads gap. It breaks when the forced sellers are the other systematic books: when every DTS-budgeted optimizer in the market rebalances on the same day, the contrarian is no longer providing liquidity but joining a queue. An experienced PM's first check on any rebalancing rule is who else is running it, and on which day of the month.

## 4.10 Case Study: From KMV to Blackstone Corporate Bond Strategies (1990s–2026)

The largest disclosed row on the map started as a default model.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.123:* "In the early 1990s, Stephen Kealhofer, John Andrew ("Mac") McQuown, and Oldrich Vasicek extended this model framework and provided a practical implementation of it."
>
> **Annotation.** "This model framework" is Merton's: equity as a call on firm assets, default when assets fall below the debt. KMV turned it into a commercial expected default frequency, which is a $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ in the language of (4.5), estimated from equity prices and balance sheets rather than from spreads. That makes the default model the natural engine of a *value* strategy: compare the market's $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}$ with your structural $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ and buy the gap. The modern desk departs from the 1990s version in calibration, not concept. Chapter 6 maps equity volatility to asset volatility properly, and the private-company problem below is where the concept needed new data.

> **Math, Gently.** *Merton in one picture.* Draw a company's assets as a line wandering up and down over time, and draw its debt as a horizontal bar beneath it. If on the day the debt falls due the assets are above the bar, the shareholders pay the lenders and keep the rest; if the assets are below it, the shareholders walk away and the lenders take the company. That is a call option: equity is the right to buy the firm's assets from the lenders at a strike price equal to the debt. Default is the option finishing out of the money. The picture turns into a number by asking how far the assets sit above the bar, measured in units of how much they typically wander in a year. That is the *distance to default*. Tiny number *(illustrative)*: assets worth \$100, debt of \$70, assets that wander by about 20% a year. The cushion is \$30 and a typical year's wander is \$20, so the company sits about 1.5 standard deviations above default. The trick that made KMV a business was that a firm's assets cannot be seen but its equity can: the share price and its volatility, read through the option formula, reveal the asset value and asset volatility behind them, every trading day. KMV's commercial step, as it is usually described, was then to map each distance to default to the default frequencies actually observed among firms at that distance, rather than reading a probability off the bell curve, whose thin tails would understate it. The full treatment, including the volatility correction that this picture leaves out, is in Chapter 6.

The timeline, with dates and sizes as reported:

- **Early 1990s.** Kealhofer, McQuown and Vasicek build KMV's commercial implementation. Moody's acquires KMV in 2002 (`Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.123`).
- **2004.** Kealhofer and McQuown, with David Solo, found DCI to run systematic corporate credit portfolios (Institutional Investor, 30 November 2020).
- **30 November 2020.** Blackstone agrees to acquire DCI, which manages about \$7.5bn across IG, HY and EM corporate credit, long-only and long/short. Blackstone Credit then manages about \$135bn (Institutional Investor, 30 November 2020).
- **May 2021.** The rationale, as reported: scale liquid credit without proportionally more analysts, and apply the model to private issuers using a Blackstone database that covers about 85% of the private leveraged-loan issuer universe. Roughly 40% of the HY market consists of private companies (Institutional Investor, 5 May 2021).
- **Today.** Blackstone describes the business as "currently the largest pure-play quantitative credit operation in the world, with over \$40 billion in assets under management" (`Blackstone_Systematic_Credit p.1`; the document is undated). Its parent segment, Blackstone Credit & Insurance, reports \$443.0bn of AUM at 31 December 2025 and lists systematic strategies within liquid corporate credit (Blackstone FY2025 10-K).
- **Coda, 2025–2026.** The template repeats. Ares agrees in late October 2025 to buy all of BlueCove, a systematic fixed-income manager founded in 2018 with about \$5.5bn of AUM and about 60 staff at 30 September 2025, and to rebrand it Ares Systematic Credit, citing insurance distribution (Alternative Credit Investor, 30 October 2025; The DESK). Ares announced completion in early February 2026 (Ares press release, Business Wire).

#### The story behind the timeline

Theo reads the timeline aloud, mostly to himself, and Mags, who is eating lunch at her desk, stops him at the first line.

"I remember the first one of those I ever saw," she says. "An EDF." *(Her story is fiction; the company in it is invented.)* "Mid-nineties. I was pricing a bond in a regional airline the way we priced everything then, by phone and by grudge: one dealer who owed me a favour, one who didn't, and the truth somewhere in between. Rated double-B, bonds in the low nineties, nobody worried. A friend who ran a bank's loan book faxed me a page. Their new model had the airline's default probability going from about one percent to about six over three months. The rating hadn't moved. The bond had barely moved. I threw the fax away." She eats a carrot. "Four months later they announced an exchange offer. The model had been reading the stock price every day while the rest of us were reading the rating every quarter. The machine knew before the phone did."

"So you started using it."

"I started hating it," Mags says. "Then I started using it. That's the usual order."

What the fax had in it was the idea in the box above, industrialized. Merton's 1974 picture of equity as a call on the firm's assets had been in the textbooks for two decades. What Kealhofer, McQuown and Vasicek did in the early 1990s, in Bohn and Stein's account, was to make it practical: read each public firm's equity price and volatility daily, back out the asset value and its wander, compute the distance to default, and sell the result as a commercial default probability (`Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management p.123`). For a lender the point was timing. A rating is an opinion revised a few times a year; an equity price is an opinion revised every second, and a structural model turns the second kind of opinion into the units of the first.

Moody's bought KMV in 2002, which made the number a product of the institution whose ratings it had been built to anticipate. Two years later two of KMV's founders, with David Solo, founded DCI to do the thing a model vendor cannot do: run money on the model's output (Institutional Investor, 30 November 2020). That is the step from selling a $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ to trading on the gap between it and the market's $\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}$, which is (4.5) as a business plan.

Then, for about sixteen years, DCI stayed a specialist. When Blackstone agreed to buy it on 30 November 2020, it managed about \$7.5bn across investment-grade, high-yield and emerging-market corporate credit, long-only and long/short. The chapter's reading of why the engine stayed small for so long is the argument of §4.7 rather than anything the firm has said: through most of those years the plumbing that lets a model trade a thousand bonds cheaply did not yet exist, and a structural model can find the cheap bond but cannot buy it.

Blackstone's stated rationale, as reported in May 2021, had two parts, and each is a column of the §4.3 map. The first was capacity: scale liquid credit without hiring analysts in proportion to the assets. The second was data. Merton needs an equity price, and roughly 40% of the high-yield market consists of private companies with no equity price at all. Blackstone's answer was a database of private issuers covering about 85% of the private leveraged-loan universe (Institutional Investor, 5 May 2021). Theo reads that line twice. A private company's distance to default cannot be read off a stock screen; it has to be built from private financials and covenant packages, the documents that sit in data rooms, the documents he used to read for a living.

By the time of the two-page Blackstone document on Theo's desk, the business described itself as the largest pure-play quantitative credit operation in the world, with over \$40 billion of assets. Theo has circled that number twice now, once at 7:40 a.m. and once here, because the as-of date is still missing. The parent segment is not in doubt: Blackstone Credit & Insurance reported \$443.0bn of AUM at 31 December 2025, with systematic strategies listed inside liquid corporate credit (Blackstone FY2025 10-K). The word in the segment's name that matters to this story is the last one.

The coda is the same story told faster. Ares, which had owned a minority of BlueCove since 2023, agreed to buy the rest in October 2025 and renamed it Ares Systematic Credit, citing insurance distribution; the deal closed in early February 2026. Two of the largest alternative-credit platforms in the world, five years apart, each bought a systematic engine rather than building one, and both sell to insurance clients, which Ares said outright when it announced its deal.

**How a systematic desk reads it.** Three things scaled DCI from \$7.5bn to over \$40bn, and none of them was a better factor. The first is the engine. A physical default model is what makes "default-adjusted spread" a measurable quantity rather than a slogan. The second is distribution. An insurance-heavy credit platform wants liquid, rating- and capital-aware corporate books, and an optimizer that respects Solvency II charges is a product an insurer will buy at size (`Blackstone_Systematic_Credit p.1–2`). In §4.8 terms, IG-heavy long-only books have the largest $A^{*}$ of any security-selection row, and that is where this platform sits. The third is data. If 40% of HY issuers are private, the moat is not TRACE, which everyone has. It is private financials and covenants, which almost nobody can feed to a model. An RSSG analyst has read more private-company credit agreements than most quant teams have parsed.

> **View From Another Seat.** *The insurer who buys the optimizer.* To a life insurer a corporate bond is less a price that moves than a yield that will be booked for years and a capital charge that will be held against it. Much of a US life insurer's investment-grade bond portfolio is carried at amortized cost in its statutory accounts, so a bond bought at a 5.5% yield books roughly 5.5% a year whatever its market price does, until it is sold, downgraded into a worse bucket, or defaults. What moves the insurer is the rating, because capital charges are set by rating category: the NAIC's risk-based capital system in the United States, and Solvency II in Europe, both charge more for lower-rated and riskier assets. That is the seat Blackstone describes optimizing for, an algorithm that can "dynamically adjust portfolios to minimize capital charges for insurance clients under regulatory frameworks like Solvency II" (`Blackstone_Systematic_Credit p.2`). From that seat a manager's information ratio is the second question. The first is spread per unit of capital. The seat is large and often attached to asset managers: Federal Reserve economists reported in March 2025 that life insurers with affiliated asset managers held general account assets of "\$4.1 trillion, accounting for about 72 percent of the industry" at the end of 2023, and that life-insurer exposure to CLOs had reached "a record \$212 billion by the end of 2023" (Carlino, Foley-Fisher, Heinrich and Verani, "Life Insurers' Role in the Intermediation Chain of Public and Private Credit to Risky Firms," *FEDS Notes*, 21 March 2025). The public template of the combination is Apollo and its annuity affiliate Athene, whose net invested assets were about \$292bn at year-end 2025 (Apollo Q4 2025 earnings call, 9 February 2026), and whose economics Rowan summarized in the line quoted in §4.4 about an asset origination machine and a liability origination machine. Seen from the insurer's chair, a systematic credit engine is above all a machine for satisfying constraints at once and at scale: rating buckets, capital charges, duration matching and ESG screens. That is a product even before its factors are proven, and it explains the buyers in the story above better than any backtest does.

> **[SOURCE EXTRACTION]** *Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022 p.8:* "there could be an element of negative selection in the construction of quant credit portfolios whereby the least liquid bonds of an issuer are referenced."
>
> **Annotation.** An issuer-level signal has to be expressed through one of the issuer's bonds, and an optimizer that ranks bonds on cheapness will tend to pick the cheapest one. In a capital structure the cheapest bond is often cheap for a reason: it is the least liquid, the most structurally subordinated, sitting at the entity that is about to lose its guarantee, or carrying the covenant package that permits the drop-down. This is cheapest-to-deliver into your own optimizer. The modern desk departs from the passage by treating bond choice within an issuer as a separate decision with its own model. That model is a capital-structure model, and building one is what RSSG analysts do for a living.

Theo puts the pencil down and reads the question he wrote above the grid on the first day: *if everyone has the same menu, what is the kitchen?* He has an answer now, and it comes in two parts. The kitchen is the plumbing. It is the knowledge-time stamp on every field (§4.5), the cost model that knows June 2022 was not April (§4.7), the router that knows which dealer will answer on a bad day, the capacity measured from the desk's own fills (§4.8) and the issuer map that does not mistake one company for three (§4.1). Every shop on the map has some of that kitchen, and the good ones do not talk about it, which is why the execution and capacity columns are silent. The second part is the one room in the kitchen that nobody on the map has built. Every framework chooses *whether* to own an issuer; none has a model for *which bond* expresses the view. The 2026 factor audit found that 25 of its 26 surviving specifications were bets within a single firm's capital structure, and the negative-selection warning above says that left to itself an optimizer will pick the wrong bond in that structure for the wrong reason. The room is a capital-structure model, and the people who know how to build it are the people who have read the credit agreement.

He thinks of Ines's question from that first morning: which of these desks are we, and which one is going to eat us? He still could not tell her which desk they are. He can tell her where the room is that nobody else has built, and why a desk standing in it would be hard to eat.

What Theo tapes to his monitor that evening is shorter than the day was.

- **"Systematic credit" is five businesses with five binding constraints:** capacity and costs for long-only, financing and crowding for long/short, breadth for allocators, rebalance predictability for ETFs, and adverse selection for algorithmic dealers. Ask which constraint binds before you ask about factors.
- **The alpha column is one memo. The money is in the plumbing.** Every value signal is a Q-minus-P gap (4.5). Know whether you are harvesting risk premium, liquidity or mispricing, because each one loses money in a different crisis.
- **Bonds are not bets; issuers are.** $BR_{\text{eff}} = N/(1+(m-1)\rho)$. Forty-seven bonds of one issuer are one bet with forty-seven CUSIPs.
- **Costs decide.** Break-even turnover is $\alpha_{\text{gross}}/(2\bar c)$. Barclays' realistic IG book gave up a third of its edge to costs, and the momentum family gives up more.
- **At the manager's favorite size the client keeps a third.** Capacity scales with $\mathcal{L}g^2/T^3$, and net alpha is zero at $\tfrac94 A^{*}$. Ask which size the capacity slide is quoting.
- **A 0.9 beta is not defense.** It is a smaller long. A default screen's recall is half a number until you know its flag rate.
- **Much of the published factor zoo did not survive cleaning.** Pin every field to its knowledge time, ban ex-post filters, and label every number gross or net, simulated or live.
- **The empty cells are distressed, LMT-aware signals, loans and private credit, securitized, and bond choice within the issuer.** They are where an RSSG brain is scarce.
