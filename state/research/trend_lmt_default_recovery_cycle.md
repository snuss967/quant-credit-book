## 1. Headline Trends

1. **The distressed exchange is now the modal default.** It accounted for about 2/3 of Moody's corporate defaults in 2024–2025, against an S&P 2010–2023 baseline of 32%, and was reported as 94% of downgrades to D/SD in the 12 months to February 2026.
2. **LMT volume peaked in 2024 (38 deals) and recalibrated in 2025 (24), but its share of default activity did not fall.** The technique migrated to amend-extend-and-exchange, deals away and drop-downs rather than retreating.
3. **Re-default is the base case, not the tail.** About 46% of 2023 distressed exchanges had re-defaulted, mostly within a year, and only 22% of coercive non-pro-rata LMEs avoided both bankruptcy and re-default within two years. The re-default hazard is front-loaded: more than 70% of eventual hard defaults arrive within 24 months.
4. **Recovery has become bimodal, conditional on participation.** First-lien DE recoveries reached 71% in 2024, but exclusion transferred up to 100% of value in the early uptiers. Serta's excluded loans were valued at 25 cents, and ConvergeOne's excluded minority recovered about 30% less within the same class.
5. **Courts have made LMT outcomes a function of contract tokens, and litigation risk now carries a dollar figure.** "Open market purchase" (Serta) vs. "purchase" (Mitel), step-by-step (Wesco district court) vs. integrated (STG Logistics), and a $261.13m damages award plus 9% interest against Serta's participating lenders (July 2026).
6. **Cooperation agreements have become the creditor counter-technology and the next legal battleground.** Examples are the Optimum co-op holding about 99% of $21.8bn of debt, tiered "carve-out" co-ops, borrower anti-co-op clauses (WBD, 2025), and antitrust challenges (Optimum, Selecta).
7. **Default measurement has fractured.** Headline and ex-DE default rates differ by about 3× (Moody's HY 5.8% vs. 2.1%, June 2025). Private-credit "shadow default" measures range from 4.0% to 5.8% (Moody's CE) up to about 19% (PIMCO BDC), so the default label a model trains on is now a modeling choice.
8. **The 2027–2029 maturity wall sets up the next LMT wave.** It totals $580bn of loans and $625bn of HY bonds, and Caa-equivalent private borrowers account for more than half of 2027–28 CE maturities. Private credit is both the deal-away financier and a new LMT venue.

## 3. Narrative: How the Trend Evolved, Its Drivers, and Where It Heads

### 3.1 Four phases

**Phase 0, the trapdoor era (2016–2019).** J.Crew's IP drop-down and PetSmart/Chewy showed that investment baskets and unrestricted-subsidiary capacity could move collateral beyond creditors' reach. Lenders' first response was the "J.Crew blocker," but the Buccola–Nini sample shows only about 40% of pre-2020 loans restricted uptiers at all. Distressed exchanges at the time were still mostly the classic Moyer-style voluntary exchange offer: a deal with a bondholder committee, often paired with a prepack. Their textbook comparison with Chapter 11 (Franks & Torous, 1994) assumed creditors in the same class were treated equally.

**Phase 1, winner-take-all (2020–2022).** COVID liquidity needs, cov-lite share above 90%, and holder bases fragmented across hundreds of CLOs set the stage for Serta (June 2020). It turned the undefined "open market purchase" exception into a weapon. Boardriders, TriMark, Mitel (2022), and Wesco/Incora (March 2022) followed. Value transfer was total: Octus measured 100% of value moved away from non-ad-hoc creditors in Wesco, Mitel and Robertshaw. Lenders responded by drafting, and 85% of new loans blocked uptiers by mid-2022, but the legacy stock of loose documents stayed outstanding.

**Phase 2, peak volume and the creditor counter-technology (2023–2024).** The rate shock pushed interest coverage on floating-rate, loan-only, sponsor-backed capital structures toward 1×. LMT counts hit their high-water mark of 38 in 2024, and Moody's recorded its highest-ever annual DE share of defaults. S&P had DDEs at about 50% of US defaults for three straight years, against 32% for 2010–2023. Two counter-forces then emerged:

- **Cooperation agreements.** Optimum's co-op signed in June 2024 and now claims about 99% of CSC Holdings' debt. Co-ops turned the lender prisoner's dilemma into a cartel.
- **Litigation.** Robertshaw found a breach but awarded only money damages, and Isgur signaled in Wesco that he would unwind the deal.

LMTs became "inclusive but unequal" (Miller, Dec 2025). The six most recent uptiers in the Octus sample transferred about 18% of value on average, not 100%.

**Phase 3, legal pricing and technique migration (2025–2026).** The twin New Year's Eve 2024 decisions (Serta reversed, Mitel affirmed) did not end LMTs. They made outcomes depend on exact contract wording. Within a month, amend-extend-and-exchange structures appeared that bypass "open market purchase" by putting the majority group in its own class. LMT counts fell to 24 in 2025, which looks like recalibration rather than retreat, since LMTs still made up about two-thirds of default activity.

The legal frontier then fragmented:

- The Wesco district court (Dec 2025) blessed step-by-step analysis, but STG Logistics in New York collapsed the steps into one integrated plan.
- ConvergeOne (Sept 2025) applied *Serta* and *LaSalle* to in-court exclusive backstops. This removed some of Chapter 11's appeal for majority groups and arguably pushed more activity *out* of court.
- The Serta remand (July 2026) put a number on breach: $261.13m plus six years of 9% interest. Exclusion-risk litigation can now be modeled as a claim with an expected value, not just a tail scenario.

Deals away came back (Optimum, Altice International, Liberty Puerto Rico, KIK), often with six-month par calls, a sign that they are leverage in negotiation with the co-op rather than an exit from it. Antitrust challenges to co-ops (Optimum, Selecta) opened a front that could take away creditors' main defense.

### 3.2 Drivers

1. **Contract supply.** Cov-lite at more than 90%, unrestricted-subsidiary capacity, and undefined exceptions. New-issue blockers shrink this supply only as legacy documents mature or are amended, so the stock of loose documents decays slowly.
2. **The cost of Chapter 11.** It is described as "exorbitantly expensive." LME debtors that later file spend 2–3× longer in bankruptcy than prepacks (Roe & Rotaru), and ConvergeOne made in-court non-pro-rata deals harder.
3. **Sponsor option value.** An LMT keeps equity's option alive. Sponsors and boards treat it as runway, and the "show me the incentive" logic (Wachtell) guarantees continued supply. Roe & Rotaru and Miller both find that value tends to shift to owners and favored creditors even when the firm is no better off.
4. **Creditor incentives.** Participating creditors get "the inside track" in the next restructuring and better returns even when the LMT fails. Participation is often privately rational whatever happens to the firm.
5. **Holder structure.** CLO-dominated, fragmented holder bases enable majority-of-the-room deals. Concentrated holders with co-ops enable cartel-style outcomes.
6. **Private credit as financier.** It funds deals away and drop-down financings, and it is itself hosting LME-like events: PIK amendments, extensions and debt-for-equity swaps.

### 3.3 Where it is heading (2026–2028)

- **The re-default wave from the 2024 peak cohort is due now.** Moody's finds more than 70% of eventual hard defaults within 24 months, and the 38 LMTs of 2024 are in that window. This is the "Chapter 22" (repeat-filing) channel, which the Mitel, Wesco and Serta histories illustrate. Watch for headline default rates staying range-bound (S&P forecasts 3.75% by June 2027) while the *mix* moves toward second events.
- **The maturity wall concentrates distress.** It totals $580bn of loans and $625bn of HY over 2027–2029, and Caa-equivalents hold more than half of 2027–28 private CE maturities. Expect LMT technique to keep migrating (extend-and-exchange, deal-away plus par call, drop-down to an unrestricted sub, double-dip and pari-plus) rather than LMT counts collapsing.
- **Optimum is the bellwether.** It combines a 99% co-op, a drop-down to an unrestricted sub, a going-concern warning, a $4.12bn April 2027 maturity, more than $4bn of potential tax liability on deconsolidation, and an antitrust challenge to the co-op. How it resolves (consensual, deal-away, or Chapter 11) will set the template for co-op-vs.-sponsor bargaining.
- **Pending appellate events are regime-switch dates.** These are the Fifth Circuit decisions in Wesco (step-by-step vs. integrated) and ConvergeOne (exclusive backstops), any appeal of the Serta remand damages, and the first ruling on co-op antitrust. Each one changes the expected value of being excluded.
- **Private credit is next.** Moody's (Oct 2026) shows reported CE defaults understating stress by about 45% (4.0% vs. 5.8%). Fitch's private-credit default rate is at a record 6.1%, and the Fed and NY Fed launched a private-credit pilot survey (Aug 2026). Expect LMT-style tools (non-pro-rata extensions, PIK toggles, lender-led take-privates) to spread to direct lending. CreditSights reports that workouts increasingly end with lenders as owners.
- **Live leading indicators (as of this week).** Hertz has hired PJT to extend runway while revolver and secured groups lawyer up, and Cogent's bondholders have organized. Adviser hires and creditor-group formation are the earliest observable LMT signals.

## 4. Implications for a Systematic Credit Desk

### 4.1 What to build

1. **A multi-state default engine.** Replace the binary default label with performing → soft default (DE/LMT/PIK amendment/extension) → hard default (payment default/bankruptcy), plus an absorbing "cured" state. Fit transition intensities with competing-risks and recurrent-event hazard models (Andersen–Gill or PWP gap-time). The Moody's 1,173-borrower curve gives the prior: about 25% hard default and more than 33% any repeat event, with 70% of hard defaults inside 2 years. Roe–Rotaru gives the coercive-LMT conditional: 22% survive both bankruptcy and re-default at 2 years.
2. **A participation-conditional recovery model.** Recovery is a mixture: R = π·R_in + (1 − π)·R_out, where π is the probability your tranche ends up inside the deal. Calibrate R_in from the 71% (2024) / 55% (2023) 1L DE recoveries. Calibrate the R_out tail from Octus value-transfer percentages (100% in early deals, about 18% in recent ones), ConvergeOne (about 30% lower), and Serta (excluded loans at 25). This feeds directly into Nuveen's credit-loss term (default forecast × (1 − recovery)), which a static, seniority-only recovery table understates in the LMT era.
3. **A Document Vulnerability (Priming Risk) Score.** NLP over credit agreements and indentures (Ch 25) should flag:
   - "open market purchase" vs. bare "purchase" (the Serta/Mitel switch)
   - "directly and adversely affected" sacred-rights language
   - unanimous vs. majority consent for subordination and lien release (the Wesco 2/3 test)
   - J.Crew, Chewy and Serta blockers
   - unrestricted-sub and investment-basket capacity
   - pro-rata extension permissions
   - anti-co-op ("anti-cartel") clauses (WBD 2025)
4. **A holder-structure and co-op probability model.** Inputs are CLO trustee holdings, Rule 2019 disclosures and fund 13F/N-PORT data. Huang, Lewellen & Wang show coalitions form with large classes, dispersed or illiquid debt and prior interactions, and less often when banks hold large positions. The output is the probability that a co-op forms and the probability you are inside it.
5. **An event-study rig.** Measure price reactions around adviser hires, co-op announcements, unrestricted-sub designations, LMT announcements, rulings, and par-call expiries. Seaton reports a negative equity reaction to co-op announcements and tranche-dependent debt reactions, which is the template for these studies.

### 4.2 What to trade

- **Inside/outside spread.** Pair long likely-participant tranches with short likely-excluded tranches (or short equity) where the document score is high and holders are fragmented. Size it with a jump-to-default-aware Kelly (Ch 28).
- **Litigation optionality on excluded paper.** After the Serta remand, excluded claims carry dollar damages and interest. Price them as a contingent claim on appellate outcomes, with rulings as known event dates.
- **Re-default convexity.** Bonds and loans 6–24 months after an LMT sit in the peak re-default window, but ratings often stay flat after LMEs (Roe–Rotaru). That disconnect is a systematic short or underweight signal in defensive HY (Ch 11).
- **CLO forced selling.** DEs reclassify as defaults or CCCs inside CLO tests, pushing managers to sell into weakness. Supply the bid systematically (Ch 21) after modeling which managers are bound by OC tests.
- **CDS after drop-downs.** Moving debt or assets to an unrestricted sub can orphan reference obligations, and NA CDX typically trades without restructuring as a credit event. Model basis dislocations and successor events (Ch 24; Man 2022 p.11 on manufactured events).

### 4.3 What to worry about

- **Label leakage and regime breaks.** A PD model trained on pre-2020 "bankruptcy or missed payment" labels will under-predict by roughly the 3× ex-DE gap. Mixing vendor definitions (Moody's DE, S&P SD, Fitch RD, PitchBook with/without LMT) inside one training set produces silent label noise.
- **Survivorship in recovery data.** DE recoveries are measured at the event, while excluded-tranche losses show up in the *next* event.
- **MNPI and wall-crossing.** Co-op membership, cleansing schedules and RSA negotiations put desks over the wall. The systematic book needs restricted-list automation. Ch 3's detector for counterparties who know about an impending LMT is a compliance tool as much as an alpha tool (see the HLS Roundtable MNPI study, May 2024).
- **Legal-regime risk.** A single co-op antitrust ruling could flip creditor bargaining power overnight. Treat it as a scenario, not a parameter.

### 4.4 For the RSSG-to-quant reader specifically

Everything you did in the room is a feature. "Who's the company's adviser and when were they hired" is an event timestamp. "Who holds the 2L and do they talk" is a holder-concentration vector. "Does the doc have a Serta blocker" is a token in a parse tree. "What will the sponsor do with 14 months of runway" is a hazard covariate.

The cognitive shift is from asking "will this deal get done and what is my recovery" for one name to asking "what is the distribution of (event type, timing, participation, recovery) across 300 names given these features." The edge from having sat in the room is knowing which features matter: for example, that six-month par calls mark a deal-away as a negotiating tactic, and that Hertz hiring PJT means a runway extension, not a filing. The new discipline is measuring them, out of sample, with grouped cross-validation by issuer and by legal regime.

   - **Rationale:** Re-default is the base case. The evidence includes the Moody's 1,173-borrower curve, the Roe–Rotaru 89-LME sample, the 46% of the 2023 cohort, and the S&P CLO 33/56/10 split. The 2024 peak cohort is in the 0–24-month window now.
   - **Content:** Andersen–Gill and PWP models, multi-state Markov chains with soft and hard states, calibration, and a backtest of a "post-LMT short."
   - **Placement:** After 14-B.
3. **Chapter 17-B `17_ch17b_shadow_defaults_pik_and_private_credit_measurement`: "Shadow Defaults: PIK, Amend-and-Extend & the Private-Credit Default Gap (TREND)."**
   - **Rationale:** Default definitions now diverge sharply: Moody's CE 4.0% vs. 5.8%, PIMCO BDC about 19%, Fitch 6.1%, Proskauer 2.51%, S&P 0.85% vs. 3.9%. A quant desk arbitraging public vs. private credit needs a unified, definition-aware default measure.
   - **Placement:** After Ch 17.
4. **New section in Ch 13: "The Legal-Token Regime Map."** A table and decision tree mapping contract tokens to court outcomes: Serta/Mitel ("open market"), Wesco district court vs. STG (step-by-step vs. integrated), Robertshaw (damages only), ConvergeOne (in-court equal treatment), and the Serta remand damages methodology (breach-date valuation at 25, 9% interest). Placement: Ch 13, after the Priming Risk Score.
