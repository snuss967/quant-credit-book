# Papers dossier: The dealer quoting frontier (hit-ratio targeting, cover prices, client tiers, effective dealer count)

Slug `rfq_quoting_frontier_2025_2026` | kind: papers (annotated bibliography) | written 2026-10-18 | feeds Ch 18-B (`18_ch18b_the_rfq_response_engine`), Ch 3, Ch 18, Ch 19.

## Scope and method

This dossier covers the sell-side RFQ quoting literature behind the planned Ch 18-B: hit-ratio-targeted HJB quoting (arXiv 2604.20406, 2605.30643), cover-price likelihoods, client tiers, the effective dealer count, quote competition, fill-probability benchmarks and time-to-trade. Twenty-seven entries annotate 30 papers; four more are listed as see-also. Equations and tables were taken from the full texts; anything I computed myself is labelled as such. Three findings change the book:

1. The **five-term** quote decomposition belongs to Niang (2026); Barzykin-Ciceri have **three** terms. The Edition 2 TOC attributes both to Barzykin-Ciceri.
2. Fermanian-Guéant-Pu's "quotes become more conservative as $n$ rises" is their *full-participation* result; their preferred partial-participation model reverses it, and only the "about two answers" finding survives.
3. Hendershott-Madhavan's Table VI implies a response correlation of 0.06-0.30 and 3-11 effective independent invitees out of 25-28, which is the public anchor for $n_{\rm eff}=n/(1+(n-1)\phi)$.

## 1. Annotated bibliography

Reading key. *Verified link* means I fetched that URL this session. DOIs were checked against Crossref; I give them as strings, not links. "Read" means full text; "abstract" means abstract only. Replication status is stated per paper; none of [1]-[4] cites a code or data repository.

### 1A. The 2025-26 quoting frontier

**[1] Barzykin & Ciceri (2026), "Bond Market Making with a Hit-Ratio Target", arXiv:2604.20406** (HSBC; v1 22 Apr 2026, v2 27 Jul 2026; q-fin.RM; no journal reference). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.20406. *Read.* **Key result.** A quadratic penalty on the size-weighted hit ratio, $-\tfrac{\kappa}{2}(r-r^\star)^2=\inf_\xi[\xi(r-r^\star)+\xi^2/2\kappa]$, keeps the HJB separable by bond, tier, side and size. With $H(p)=\sup_\delta\Lambda(\delta)(\delta-p)$, the exact quote is $\delta^\star=f^{-1}(-H'(p)/\lambda)$, where $p=\Delta u-\tilde\xi$ for targeted tiers and $p=\Delta u$ otherwise. The quadratic (BEGV) ansatz gives the Riccati equation $A'=ADA-\phi\Sigma$ with $D=\mathrm{diag}(\sum z_kH''(0))$, so every tier loads the inventory curvature but only targeted tiers get a dual. Stationary $A=\sqrt{\phi}\,D^{-1/2}(D^{1/2}\Sigma D^{1/2})^{1/2}D^{-1/2}$. The linearised quote is $\delta_0+c^{-1}[e'A(\pm q+z/2)-\tilde\xi]$ with $c=2-ff''/f'^2$: three terms (riskless spread, inventory correction, hit-ratio correction). The dual is $\tilde\xi=\tilde\kappa\,y$, $y=r^\star+W^{-1}\sum zH'(0)+(2W)^{-1}\sum z^2A_{mm}H''(0)$, so it moves with inventory curvature. **Method/data.** Synthetic: ladder 1/5/20mm, intensities 500/200/50 per day, sigmoid fills, $r^\star=0.1$, $T=1$ day, JAX Euler scheme. The exact-closure BEGV version is "impressively accurate"; a constant dual fails at large inventory because $\tilde\xi(q)$ is roughly quadratic. A background tier, or a correlated background bond, cuts the skew and raises the achieved hit ratio. No RFQ data. **Desk use.** This is the control law: the target is a tier-specific shadow price, it must be re-solved when curvature changes, and untargeted flow is a free inventory hedge. *Not replicated; no code found.*

**[2] Niang (2026), "Quality-Adjusted Hit-Ratio Targeting in Corporate Bond Market Making", arXiv:2605.30643** (single author, independent; ex-HSBC/Citi; v1 28 May 2026; q-fin.TR). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30643. *Read.* **Key result.** Markouts are split into factor, carry/rolldown, issuer-RV, index/ETF and residual parts; only the residual defines toxicity $\chi_i$, and the target uses weights $w_i=e^{-\gamma\chi_i}$. The dual enters as a Hamiltonian shift $z_iH_i(p_i+\chi_i-w_i\xi)$, has a unique root (strictly increasing $F$), and for logistic fills has the Lambert-W form $\delta^\star=p+(1+x)/\beta$, $x=W_0(e^{-\alpha-\beta p-1})$. The five-term quote is: riskless spread + inventory skew − credit-alpha skew + residual-toxicity charge − quality-hit-ratio subsidy. **Numbers (20 synthetic EUR IG bonds, 300 paths).** At an 8% target, raw targeting earns −14.32 bp·MM/day against +5.25 for residual targeting. The saving is +19.57: spread capture −9.49, residual-toxicity cost +28.26, liquidation cost +1.67. Raw targeting wins 7.29% of carry/RV flow and 7.47% of toxic flow; residual targeting wins 7.05% and 0.87%. "Naive gross toxicity" over-widens (carry/RV hit ratio 1.08%). A sweep-warehousing extension raises PnL from 5.85 to 8.47. **Limits.** Synthetic data drawn from the same factors used to decompose; no perturbation analysis (an automated referee note at pith.science says so and the author reportedly agreed; promised, not delivered). **Desk use.** The five-term decomposition belongs to this paper, not to [1]. *Not replicated.*

**[3] Barzykin, Bergault, Guéant & Lemmel (2025-26), "Optimal Quoting under Adverse Selection and Price Reading", arXiv:2508.20225** (v1 27 Aug 2025, v6 7 Aug 2026; shortened version "to be published in Risk (Cutting Edge) in 2026"). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20225. *Read abstract, model and conclusion.* **Key result.** Two informational channels enter the reference price: adverse selection $\tilde\zeta^{n,k}(\delta)$ (tier- and size-specific impact of an execution) and price reading $\tilde J^n$(ladder skew), where "skew sniffers" infer inventory from the quotes streamed to them. A first-order perturbation splits the correction into a global component (changes the value function, applies to every client) and a tier-specific component. Price reading makes the dealer act more risk-averse franchise-wide but unwinds the skew shown to sniffer tiers; adverse selection from long-horizon signals (several dozen bp to a few percent) makes the dealer less risk-averse on risk-reducing trades, whereas sharp few-bp signals force defensive quotes. **Method.** Infinite horizon, Taylor expansion, simple functional forms; calibration inputs (intensity curves, markouts, skew sensitivity) are left to the implementer. **Desk use.** The second toxicity channel: [2] prices who trades against you, this prices what your ladder reveals. *Not replicated.*

**[4] Barzykin (2026), "Win-score promotion gates in aggregator-routed RFQ markets: A two-tier stochastic control model", arXiv:2603.10569** (HSBC; 11 Mar 2026; FX-framed). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.10569. *Read first half.* **Key result.** Tier A flow is multiplied by a gate $G(R)$ of the dealer's slow win score (top-list plus exploration); tier B flow is ungated. With fast inventory and slow score separated (adiabatic), steep logistic gates produce fold bifurcations, bistability and hysteresis: an endogenous "campaign versus harvest" pattern. Background flow keeps inventory mixing alive when the dealer is demoted. **Desk use.** If your platform ranks you by hit ratio, the hit ratio is a state variable with memory, not a flat KPI, which changes what the multiplier $\xi$ means. The bond analogue is an inference. *Not replicated; no data.*

**[5] Marín Martínez, Ardanza-Trevijano & Sabio (2026), "Causal interventions in bond multi-dealer-to-client platforms", PLOS ONE 21(1): e0341369** (BBVA CIB and Univ. Navarra; received 24 Jun 2025, accepted 6 Jan 2026, published 27 Jan 2026; arXiv:2506.18147). Verified links: https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0341369 and https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18147 (I read the arXiv v2 full text). **Key result.** A graphical model with latent client reservation spread, rival quotes, price-discovery intent and information asymmetry IA. The hit-probability model $P(\text{hit}\mid do(\delta))$ is identified by adjusting for volatility, RfQ, bond and client features. The revenue-on-hit model is *not* identified from observables alone: conditioning on a win is a collider on $\delta\to RS\leftarrow\delta_{res}\leftarrow IA\to\mu\to R$, so IA must be modelled. **Numbers.** 102,437 BBVA RfQs on European government bonds, 5,738 hits. ROC-AUC: generative (FGP-style) 0.742, LightGBM 0.743, logistic 0.684; balanced Brier skill 0.238, 0.406, 0.446. LightGBM violates monotonicity in the spread; the generative model does not. Client features carried no signal; cover-price features did not help. **Desk use.** The selection bias in winner-only markouts has a graph-theoretic fix, and a fill model must be monotone in the quote before it is inverted. Government bonds, not credit. *Not replicated.*

**[6] Ciceri, Cottrell, Freeland et al. (HSBC and IBM Quantum) (2025), "Enhanced fill probability estimates in institutional algorithmic bond trading using statistical learning algorithms with quantum computers", arXiv:2509.17715** (22 Sep 2025; quant-ph). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.17715. *Read.* **Key result.** On 1,073,926 European corporate-bond RFQ responses over 294 trading days (1 Sep 2023 to 29 Oct 2024; 5,166 bonds; about 37% accepted), 216 features, rolling time-blinded backtest: classical models (logistic, XGB, RF, NN) reach out-of-sample AUC about $0.63\pm0.04$, flat across 0-4 days of blinding. Quantum-hardware-transformed features lift this to about 0.75 and, with a longer circuit, 0.97 (the "up to 34%" headline). **Caveat.** The authors call the uplift "questionable" and unexplained; it is a backtest. **Desk use.** The credible benchmark is AUC of 0.6-0.65 for fill prediction at production scale (0.74 in [5], a different asset). An AUC of 0.97 that decays with a blinding gap is a leakage exhibit, not a signal. *Reproduced by the authors on three IBM devices; independent replication impossible (proprietary data).*

### 1B. Foundations: likelihoods, ladders, tiers, closed forms

**[7] Fermanian, Guéant & Pu (2016), "The behavior of dealers and clients on the European corporate bond market: the case of Multi-Dealer-to-Client platforms", Market Microstructure and Liquidity 2(3-4): 1750004** (issue dated Dec 2016, online Jun 2017; DOI 10.1142/S2382626617500046; arXiv:1511.07773, v4 22 Mar 2017). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.07773. *Read.* **Method/data.** BNP Paribas as "reference dealer", Bloomberg FIT, 2014-15, up to six dealers per RFQ: 209,069 buy and 272,189 sell RFQs after filtering. Outcomes are Done, Tied Traded Away, Covered, Other Traded Away and Not Traded (buy counts 44,745 / 2,892 / 44,054 / 69,716 / 47,662). Rivals' quotes are i.i.d. skew-exponential-power draws on the CBBT-normalised half-spread; the client reservation value is Gaussian. Closed-form likelihoods per outcome; for example Done with a cover price $C$ is $n f(C)(1-F(C))^{n-1}(1-G(Y))$, and Not Traded is $\int^{Y}(1-F)^n g$. **Key results.** (i) Only about 40% of requested dealers answer ($p=0.400$ buy, $0.424$ sell). (ii) Answer probability falls with panel size ($p(n)$ = 1.00, 0.655, 0.498, 0.417, 0.351 for $n$=1..5 rivals) so expected answers $(n+1)p(n)\approx2.0, 1.97, 1.99, 2.09, 2.11$: clients "often wait for only two answers". (iii) The full-participation model finds rivals' quotes *more conservative* as $n$ rises (buy-side SEP scale 1.41 to 5.72), which the authors attribute to non-answering; in the partial-participation model the mean answered quote *tightens* with $n$ (1.36 to 0.647), so competition among answerers is stronger. (iv) Clients' mean reservation value sits about 1.7 half-spreads from CBBT mid (about 90% of the full bid-ask spread, the paper's own gloss). **Desk use.** The cover price is observed only when you win, so $F$ is identified from one side; the likelihood is the estimator for winner's-curse shading. The book's Ch 3 sentence "quotes become more conservative as $n$ rises" is the full-participation result and should be corrected. *Replication: structure re-estimated by [5]; no code cited.*

**[8] Bergault & Guéant (2021), "Size matters for OTC market makers: general results and dimensionality reduction techniques", Mathematical Finance 31(1): 279-322** (DOI 10.1111/mafi.12286; arXiv:1907.01225, v5 Sep 2022). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.01225. *Abstract.* Generalises Avellaneda-Stoikov to several transaction sizes with a different quote per size (the "ladder"), plus a factor-model dimensionality reduction for large universes. **Desk use.** The ladder $z_k$ and the reduced Hamiltonian $H(p)=\sup\Lambda(\delta)(\delta-p)$ in [1]-[4] are this paper's operator. Open-source: none found.

**[9] Bergault, Evangelista, Guéant & Vieira (2021), "Closed-form approximations in multi-asset market making", Applied Mathematical Finance 28(2): 101-142** (arXiv:1810.04383, v5 Sep 2022). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04383. *Abstract.* Closed-form value-function proxies (quadratic in inventory, Riccati) for multi-asset Avellaneda-Stoikov models, finite-horizon and ergodic, usable as heuristics, RL initial values, or greedy quoting rules. **Desk use.** "BEGV" in [1]-[2]: the production approximation, because the exact inventory-grid HJB is infeasible beyond a handful of bonds.

**[10] Barzykin, Bergault & Guéant, two dealer-market papers.** (a) "Algorithmic market making in dealer markets with hedging and market impact", Mathematical Finance 33(1): 41-79 (2023), DOI 10.1111/mafi.12367, arXiv:2106.06974; (b) "Market making by an FX dealer: tiers, pricing ladders and hedging rates for optimal risk control", arXiv:2112.02269 (v3 Jun 2023). Verified links: https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06974 and https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02269. *Abstracts.* The dealer internalises within an inventory band (by skewing) and externalises outside it; "the larger the franchise, the wider the inventory range suitable for pure internalization"; (b) formalises client tiers and size ladders alongside hedging. **Desk use.** [1] has no hedge leg; my suggestion is that these papers supply the externalisation switch (hedge in CDS or index outside the band) for the stress-parameter toggle. FX-calibrated.

**[11] Bergault & Guéant (2023-24), "Liquidity Dynamics in RFQ Markets and Impact on Pricing", arXiv:2309.04216** (v3 19 Jun 2024). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.04216. *Abstract.* Bidimensional Markov-modulated Poisson RFQ flows; extends the micro-price to RFQ markets and defines a "Fair Transfer Price" for marking illiquid securities when trades are scarce. **Desk use.** The reference mid in every other paper is exogenous; this builds one from RFQ arrival imbalance. See also [24].

**[12] Guéant & Manziuk (2019), "Deep reinforcement learning for market making in corporate bonds: beating the curse of dimensionality", Applied Mathematical Finance 26(5): 387-452** (DOI 10.1080/1350486x.2020.1714455; arXiv:1910.13205). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.13205. *Abstract.* Model-based deep RL approximates optimal A-S type quotes over a large bond universe where grid methods fail. See also, not annotated: Atkins, Fathi & Assefa (2024), "Reinforcement Learning for Corporate Bond Trading: A Sell Side Perspective", arXiv:2406.12983 (working paper; learns a spread policy over the Fair Transfer Price). **Desk use.** The comparison class for the closed forms: RL has no equilibrium guarantee and, per [26], can learn to collude.

**[13] Avellaneda & Stoikov (2008), Quantitative Finance 8(3): 217-224 (DOI 10.1080/14697680701381228), and Guéant, Lehalle & Fernandez-Tapia (2013), Mathematics and Financial Economics 7(4): 477-507 (DOI 10.1007/s11579-012-0087-0).** Not fetched; DOIs checked in Crossref. Standard results, cited from general knowledge: reservation-price and exponential-intensity quotes; closed-form optimal quotes with inventory bounds. **Desk use.** Chapter 18's base case. In [1] the dual enters as $p=\Delta u-\tilde\xi$ (eq. 16), so a hit-ratio target is formally a shift of the A-S/GLFT reservation price by $\tilde\xi$, which is Niang's "subsidy".

### 1C. Empirical RFQ microstructure: panels, response, tiers

**[14] Hendershott & Madhavan (2015), "Click or Call? Auction versus Search in the Over-the-Counter Market", Journal of Finance 70(1): 419-447** (DOI 10.1111/jofi.12164). Verified links: https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/hender/Click_Call_OTC.pdf (published layout, read) and the 2012 working paper https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~jh4/SternMicroMtg/Old/SternMicroMtg2012/Accepted/ClickOrCall13.pdf. **Data.** All 4.6mm US corporate-bond trades, Jan 2010-Apr 2011, with MarketAxess auction records: 467,614 electronic trades in 5,528 bonds against 4,153,611 voice trades in 11,122 bonds. **Table VI (means).** All bonds, by size bucket Micro / Odd / Round / Max: dealers queried 27.7 / 27.2 / 26.6 / 25.1; response rate 15.2% / 20.3% / 25.5% / 28.9%; auctions with no response 8.2% / 8.6% / 9.7% / 14.7%; fill rate 73.4% / 68.7% / 66.1% / 51.0%. High yield is worse: response 8.7%, 13.1%, 15.8% and no-response 26.1%, 19.6%, 21.9%. On the ten worst volatility days response falls to 12.1%-25.8%. **Text.** Modal IG response is three; 7.3% of IG auctions get none; about 60% of HY auctions draw two or fewer bids; "dealers' decisions to bid... are not independent". Table VII: the coefficient on ln(dealers queried) is 0.54 (IG) and 0.45 (HY), so responses grow far slower than invitations. Cost falls from 24-45 bp with few responders to about zero beyond ten. **Desk use.** The empirical anchor for $n_{\rm eff}$. My arithmetic on Table VI (illustrative): an IG round-lot auction with 26.6 invitees and 26.1% response would see no response 0.03% of the time if independent, but sees 8.9%; a beta-binomial fit implies response correlation $\rho\approx0.06$ (micro) to $0.30$ (max), i.e. 3-11 effective independent invitees out of 25-28. *Consistent with [7], [16] and [17]; no independent replication of Table VI on post-2021 data that I found.*

**[15] Hendershott, Li, Livdan, Schürhoff & Venkataraman (2026), "Quote Competition in Corporate Bonds", Journal of Finance 81(4), from p. 2165** (August 2026; DOI 10.1111/jofi.70048; open access). Verified link: https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/hender/Quote_Competition.pdf (full text, read). **Data.** BondCliQ dealer "runs", 1 Oct 2019-1 May 2020: 35 dealers, 8.5mm quotes, about 24,000 bonds, dealers about 25% of TRACE dealer-to-client volume; matched to TRACE and insurer trades. **Results.** Quoting raises a dealer's chance of trading that bond-day by 4.8 pp and trade count by 4.4%; the presence of a quote improves client execution by 6-7 bp (coefficient 0.066; "4 to 7 bp" in one passage); 84%-110% of quote quality passes through to execution. Non-relationship dealers take 23% of insurer trades when they quote and 11% when they do not. Competition is imperfect: the best-quoting dealer gets only 15% of trades; trade-through rates (trade worse than the best quote under an hour old) are 17% for the best-quoting dealer (16% large, 23% small), about 31% for other CliQ dealers (25% large, 40% small) and about 33% for non-CliQ dealers. A shift-share IV shows quoting is not zero-sum: a one-SD rise in predicted quote supply raises CliQ share by 2.1 pp (IV; 2.9 OLS), total activity by 4.7% (3.9% OLS) and execution quality by 2.3 bp. Good quotes are firm, bad quotes get improved. **Desk use.** Quote visibility is the evidence behind the "franchise subsidy"; trade-throughs mean a hit ratio is not a ranking of quote quality. *Indicative runs, not RFQ data; mid-tier dealers only. "Replication Code" is listed as supporting information on the publisher's site (not accessed); the BondCliQ data are proprietary.*

**[16] Kargar, Lester, Plante & Weill (2025), "Sequential Search for Corporate Bonds", Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia WP 25-08** (DOI 10.21799/frbp.wp.2025.08; earlier NBER w31904; in journal review per its acknowledgements). Verified link: https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/FRBP/Assets/working-papers/2025/wp25-08.pdf (read). **Data.** All MarketAxess inquiries and replies, 3 Jan 2017-31 Mar 2021, matched to TRACE to catch voice completions. **Results.** About 30% of inquiries fail; a benchmark odd-lot IG purchase by a well-connected client takes about two days to complete after a failed inquiry ("2-3 days" in the abstract); blocks take about a day longer than micro lots; sells are about twice as fast as buys; the bottom 60% by "connectedness" take nearly twice as long as the top decile; first-inquiry failure is about 14% for the best-connected and above 50% for the worst. Clients who reject the first quotes trade later about 40% of the time at a price about 10 bp better than the best first offer; reply quality is stationary over the search; dealer inventory drives replies. **Desk use.** An RFQ is one draw in a repeated search, so per-RFQ hit ratio understates conversion of client intent; the 1/5/20-day markout horizons follow from the 2-3 day search time.

**[17] Riggs, Onur, Reiffen & Zhu (2020), "Swap trading after Dodd-Frank: Evidence from index CDS", Journal of Financial Economics 137(3): 857-886** (DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2020.03.008; working-paper version "Mechanism Selection and Trade Formation on Swap Execution Facilities", CFTC, 29 Sep 2017). Verified link (WP, read): https://www.cftc.gov/sites/default/files/idc/groups/public/@economicanalysis/documents/file/oce_mechanism_selection.pdf. **Data.** May 2016 message-level data, Bloomberg SEF and Tradeweb SEF, CDX and iTraxx. **Results.** When customers use RFQ they query 4.1 dealers on average and get 3.6 responses; panels are 3 dealers (about 45% of sessions), 5 (just under 30%), 4 (18%). Response rates exceed 90% for 3-5 dealers and fall to about 80% for 6-7; responders plateau near 7. A \$22mm larger order cuts the panel by 0.55 dealers. Theory: the winner infers it has the lowest inventory of the $k$, a winner's curse linear in $k$. **Desk use.** Index-CDS response (80-90%) is far above bond response (15-40%), so the bond panel's nominal size is a poor guide to competition.

**[18] Wang (2023), "The limits of multi-dealer platforms", Journal of Financial Economics 149(3): 434-450** (DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2023.05.005). *Abstract only; the author-page PDF link from search results returned 404, so no link.* In a model where dealers choose whether to respond, the client contacts only two dealers in equilibrium: more contacts lower each dealer's response probability, which makes each responder's price stochastically worse; no platform design improves the client's payoff. **Desk use.** The theory behind FGP's "about 2 answers" [7] and the slow growth of responses in HM's Table VII [14].

**[19] Baldauf & Mollner (2024), "Competition and Information Leakage", Journal of Political Economy 132(5): 1603-1641** (DOI 10.1086/727709). *Abstract only.* Clients limit contacts and disclose little (two-sided requests) because an extra contact raises competition but also leakage, which helps a *losing* dealer front-run; the client optimally reveals no direction. **Desk use.** A reason to treat the RFQ you lost as informative, and a warning that your Covered and Traded-Away log is a leakage-contaminated sample.

**[20] Oomen (2017; online 2016), "Execution in an aggregator", Quantitative Finance 17(3): 383-404** (DOI 10.1080/14697688.2016.1201589). *Abstract only.* In an FX aggregator the LP that receives the deal request suffers the winner's curse, "and this effect grows stronger when the trader increases the number of participants"; LPs defend with wider spreads or last-look rejection; "not only the number, but also the type of LP" matters. **Desk use.** The FX ancestor of [18] and of [4]'s rationale for limiting invitations.

**[21] Hendershott, Li, Livdan & Schürhoff (2020), "Relationship Trading in Over-the-Counter Markets", Journal of Finance 75(2): 683-734** (DOI 10.1111/jofi.12864). Verified link: https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/hender/OTCRelations.pdf (abstract confirmed in the PDF). One-third of insurers use a single dealer; execution cost is non-monotone in network size, falling at first and rising once a network exceeds 20 dealers. **Desk use.** The client side of panel size: relationship value competes with competition, so large panels are not monotone-better.

**[22] Jurkatis, Schrimpf, Todorov & Vause (2023), "Relationship discounts in corporate bond trading", BIS Working Papers No. 1140** (3 Nov 2023). Verified link: https://www.bis.org/publ/work1140.htm (page read, abstract; PDF not read). Regulatory dealer-customer transaction data with trader identities (amounts in GBP). The top 1% of relationship clients pay transaction costs 51% below the median client (about £1.3mm a year for the average such client); the discount more than doubles in stress; it is larger for clients who help dealers manage inventory and for asset managers that generate the bulk of dealer profit; no evidence dealers discount clients to learn their private information. **Desk use.** The empirical case for tiering on inventory help and franchise value, not on information.

**[23] Price discrimination in D2C markets, two papers.** Hau, Hoffmann, Langfield & Timmer (2021), "Discriminatory Pricing of Over-the-Counter Derivatives", Management Science 67(11): 6660-6677 (DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2020.3787): in FX derivatives the median non-financial client pays 10.9 pips more than blue-chip companies, against a 6.9-pip average effective spread, and discrimination vanishes on multi-dealer platforms. Collin-Dufresne, Junge & Trolle (2020), "Market Structure and Transaction Costs of Index CDSs", Journal of Finance 75(5): 2719-2763 (DOI 10.1111/jofi.12953): dealer-to-client cost exceeds interdealer cost because client trades have higher, mostly permanent impact; dealers "appear to price discriminate based on the perceived price impact of trades". *Abstracts only; no links.* **Desk use.** Tier pricing by perceived price impact is observed in CDS; platform competition erodes it in FX.

### 1D. Benchmarks, leakage, toxic flow and algorithmic collusion

**[24] Jacobsen, Venkataraman & Xu (2025), "Illiquidity Meets Intelligence: AI-Driven Price Discovery in Corporate Bonds", SMU working paper, September 2025.** Verified link: https://microstructure.exchange/papers/JVX_20250904.pdf (read first half and Table 7; MarketAxess supplied the CP+ and RFQ data). **Data.** 24.9mm non-retail trades and 11.8bn CP+ quotes, 20,335 issues, 2017-2023. **Results.** MarketAxess's CP+ predicts the next trade price better than the last trade; the typical bond has no non-retail print on 60% of bond-days; a regression of CP+ returns on Treasury returns has $R^2$ of 28.5% (36% with all public factors), and RFQ information adds to it: when a did-not-trade RFQ or delayed-spot RFQ falls between trades CP+'s outperformance rises 1.4x to 5.0x (mean deviation gap, trade gap over five days: \$0.35 to \$0.51 with a did-not-trade RFQ; \$0.40 to \$0.70 with a delayed-spot RFQ). **Desk use.** Untraded RFQs carry price information, so the dealer's full log (Covered, Traded Away, Not Traded) is an asset the hit ratio ignores, and every dealer's composite mid is partly built from this flow. *Not peer-reviewed; vendor-supplied data.*

**[25] Caretti, Mahajan & Husveth (Tradeweb) (2026), "Information Leakage in US Credit: Evidence from All-to-All RFQs", Tradeweb white paper, May 2026.** Verified link: https://www.tradeweb.com/49a042/globalassets/newsroom/market-insights/2026/5.13.26-credit-whitepaper/information-leakage-in-us-credit-5.13.26b-final-1.pdf (read). **Design.** IG bonds above \$1mm, 1 Jan 2025-30 Apr 2026; GLM with balanced weights on 5-minute cumulative spread changes (Tradeweb Ai-Price) between RFQ acceptance and TRACE publication, up to 100 minutes. **Result.** All-to-all RFQs show same-direction impact "in the order of 0.1 to 0.3 basis points" larger than voice, bigger for sells and for the less liquid half of bonds; the authors call it quasi-causal. **Caveat.** Vendor-authored, endorses SNAP+ (dealer selection by past performance); effect is small beside [15]'s 6-7 bp; protocol choice is not randomised. **Desk use.** Markouts are protocol-contaminated: tier toxicity must be estimated net of the all-to-all leakage channel, as [2]'s residualisation implies. *Not peer-reviewed.*

**[26] Algorithmic collusion in dealer markets: Cont & Xiong (2024), "Dynamics of market making algorithms in dealer markets: Learning and tacit collusion", Mathematical Finance 34(2): 467-521** (print 2024, online 2023; DOI 10.1111/mafi.12401). *Abstract only; no link.* Competition is a Nash equilibrium, collusion a Pareto optimum; decentralised multi-agent deep RL, with no information sharing, "may give rise to tacit collusion, with spread levels strictly above the competitive equilibrium level". **Desk use.** If every dealer runs a learning quoter, the equilibrium response curve $f$ that [1]-[2] take as given is endogenous; the abstract notes growing regulatory scrutiny. *Simulation-only; not tested on RFQ data.* See also, not annotated (existence and authors checked in Crossref; abstracts not retrieved): Cartea, Chang, Mroczka & Oomen (2022), "AI-driven liquidity provision in OTC financial markets", Quantitative Finance 22(12): 2171-2204 (DOI 10.1080/14697688.2022.2130087); Cont & Xiong (2025), "A Study of Algorithmic Collusion in Multi-dealer-to-client Platforms" (SSRN 5463594); Assayag, Barzykin, Cont & Xiong (2024), "Competition and learning in dealer markets" (SSRN 4838181).

**[27] Cartea & Sánchez-Betancourt (2025), "A Simple Strategy to Deal with Toxic Flow", arXiv:2503.18005** (23 Mar 2025). Verified link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18005. *Abstract.* Infinite-horizon broker versus informed and uninformed clients; closed-form dealing strategy and an algorithm that bypasses calibrating individual client parameters; the optimal discount in the price of liquidity balances order-flow capture against adverse-selection loss to informed traders. **Desk use.** A parameter-light alternative to [2]'s residual-markout pipeline, to be benchmarked against it. *Not replicated.*

## 2. Synthesis: open debates and replication status

**Debate 1: is the hit ratio a constraint, a state variable, or a KPI?** [1] penalises an instantaneous expected hit ratio, a Markovian surrogate; the literal windowed count ratio would need cumulative-count states and is declared out of scope. [4] makes it a slow score that gates future flow and finds hysteresis. [4] and [25] both say platforms rank dealers on historical performance, and Man's buy-side note filters RFQ participants on RFQ frequency and completion rate (shelf: `Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023` p.5), yet no academic paper tests the ranking on bond data. If a gate exists, the multiplier $\xi$ prices future access, and may be bistable. Open.

**Debate 2: whose flow is toxic?** [2] defines toxicity as the markout residual after factor, carry, RV and index effects; [3] adds a second leak, the dealer's own skew; [5] shows winner-only markouts are collider-biased unless information asymmetry is modelled; [19] and [25] show that shopping an RFQ leaks (0.1-0.3 bp in all-to-all, more in illiquid bonds). The only large-sample evidence on real tiering points elsewhere: discounts follow inventory help and franchise value, not information [22], although price discrimination by perceived impact appears in index CDS and FX [23]. No one has estimated residual toxicity on real RFQ logs with an out-of-sample test. The one critique on record is an automated referee note that [2]'s gains are built into its simulation.

**Debate 3: nominal versus effective competition.** Panels are 25-28 invitees on MarketAxess [14], at most six on Bloomberg FIT [7], about four in index-CDS RFQs [17]; responders are roughly 4-7, about 2, and 3.6. Theory says the client should call two [18] or ration contacts to limit leakage [19]; cost falls to about zero by ten responders [14]; relationship networks have a cost minimum below 20 dealers [21]. My beta-binomial fit to [14] gives response correlation 0.06-0.30. The dealer-side consequence is that winner's-curse shading should depend on the effective number of competitors and the answer rate, not on $n$. The formula $n_{\rm eff}=n/(1+(n-1)\phi)$ is the standard equicorrelation design effect; I found no paper that estimates it from a desk's log.

**Debate 4: does quote competition work, and does equilibrium survive learning quoters?** [15] finds quote presence worth 6-7 bp and not zero-sum, yet the best quote wins 15% of trades and 17-40% of trades are traded through. [26] shows deep-RL quoters reaching supracompetitive spreads without communication, in simulation. If many desks run [1]-[2] engines with the same fill curve estimated on the same platform, that curve is endogenous.

**Debate 5: how predictable is a fill?** AUC about 0.63 on 1.07mm corporate RFQs with 216 features [6]; 0.74 on 102k government RFQs with eight features [5]; an unexplained 0.97 under a quantum feature map. Structural models are monotone in the quote; boosted trees are better calibrated but not monotone [5].

**Replication status.** Of the nine papers in the table, eight cite no code repository; [15] lists "Replication Code" as supporting information on the Wiley site (not accessed; unusable without the proprietary BondCliQ data). [3] (theory and simulation, not tabulated) states that its code "is not publicly deposited" and offers it on enquiry. I could not search GitHub from this session, so "none cited" is the claim.
