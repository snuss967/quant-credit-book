# PERSPECTIVES: Dealer Desks, Market Makers, Systematic Credit Managers and Venues

Researcher dossier for the book "Systematic & Quantitative Credit: From PJT RSSG to the Automated Desk". Compiled 2026-10-18. Reader: a restructuring banker moving to a systematic credit desk.

Conventions: the paragraph "How X looks at our bond" in each section is the researcher's inference from the sourced seat facts, not a statement by the firm. Quotes are verbatim and 40 words or fewer, with speaker, date and URL; anything marked "(paraphrase)" is my summary of a source and not a quote. "UNVERIFIED" marks facts that came from memory or a secondary summary and were not confirmed against a fetched page this session. Facts carried over from existing dossiers in state/research/ are marked "(carried from <file>)".

## 0. How to use this dossier

**What is solid.** Section 1 to 4 statements marked with a date, speaker and source come from fetched primary or near-primary text: bank earnings-call transcripts (Fool machine transcripts, so wording may carry transcription errors), SEC exhibits (Jefferies, Virtu, ICE/MarketAxess, the SEC's Two Sigma order), JPMorgan and Barclays investor documents, firm web pages, the shelf PDFs with page numbers, and the FSB, Fed and NBER sources.

A Jane Street trader would say that is what the spread pays for.

## 3. Systematic and quantitative credit managers

### 3.1 D. E. Shaw

**Seat.** Multi-strategy, partly systematic, partly discretionary manager with more than $115 billion of "investment and committed capital" at 1 Sep 2026 (deshaw.com/what-we-do/investment-management, fetched 18 Oct 2026). Its credit exposure is mostly structured, asset-backed and private credit rather than flow corporate bonds: the firm lists "private credit, venture and growth equity, and capital solutions for financial institutions and corporates" among its privates, and its public credit papers are about mortgage credit-risk-transfer bonds (2020) and the cash-synthetic basis (2009). The seat buys dislocated paper from forced sellers, holds it for months to years, and is funded by investor capital that it allocated "across different pools of capital" by expected horizon.

**Roles in named events.** *March 2020:* buyer of more than $400 million of GSE CRT bonds from stressed holders (primary source above), the cleanest public example of a quant-fundamental shop acting as a liquidity provider of last resort for a fee. *2022 and 2025:* no public credit statement found. *LMTs:* no verified role.

**How D. E. Shaw looks at our bond.** The bond is a claim to a distribution of recoveries, and the firm asks whether a stressed price is below the loss-model value. It would buy the bond after an LMT-driven drop if the loan-level or document analysis says the market is over-extrapolating a bad analogue (as in 2020 with 2008). Its edge is a proprietary database plus patience; its constraint is that bespoke credit needs a team, not a screen.

**How AQR looks at our bond.** As a row in a cross-section: spread residual from a fair-value regression, momentum, quality and a defensive score. The bond is either in the cheap-and-improving quintile or not, and the document risk is a noise term. The LMT-prone B-rated name is the very bond a defensive or quality screen will exclude. AQR would never read the credit agreement; it would notice that the name's spread residual and equity volatility signals disagree.

## 5. The three stress events seen from each seat (March 2020, 2022, 2025)

Four public themes recur: dealers say they stayed in; non-banks say they stayed in; buyers say liquidity vanished; and the central bank or the volume record settles the argument. The evidence by event:

**March 2020 (the dash for cash).**
- *Authorities.* The FSB's 17 Nov 2020 review: "Dealers also faced difficulties absorbing large sales of assets," and ETFs "became one of the key mechanisms for price discovery during the dash for cash." Kargar, Lester, Lindsay, Liu, Weill and Zuniga (NBER 27355, Jun 2020) find "the cost of trading immediately via risky-principal trades increased dramatically," pushing customers to slower agency trades; the Fed's corporate credit facilities (SMCCF announced 23 Mar 2020; expanded on 9 Apr 2020 so the credit programs supported "up to $850 billion") "had a positive effect on market liquidity." Customers' willingness to pay for immediacy rose about 200 bp per dollar traded.
- *Dealer seat.* Dimon: banks "deployed over $500 billion in liquidity for clients" (self-reported). Morgan Stanley's CFO (16 Apr 2020): "liquidity dried up"; credit corporates "saw good velocity" but securitized and muni inventory was marked down.
- *Systematic and quant-fundamental seat.* D. E. Shaw bought more than $400 million of GSE credit-risk-transfer bonds in March and April 2020 from holders under "redemption requests and margin calls." Man Group (2022): dealer liquidity "can disappear when it is most needed." The data (Man 2023): electronic share jumped in this stress.
- *Market makers.* Public statements are firm-level and self-described (Jane Street: "a consistent fixed income liquidity provider for clients in all market environments"; Citadel Securities lists "Covid" among four volatility periods). No third-party credit no-quote data are public.

**2022 (rates shock, quantitative tightening).**
- Man Numeric (Jun 2023): "dealers inclined to commit less of their balance sheets to bond trading," portfolio-trading costs rose in H1 2022 and the loss of customised ETF baskets aggravated it, yet "One of the venues where pricing and liquidity held up best last year ... was electronic execution." BlackRock's eVestment data: the average high-yield manager beat its benchmark by 0.47% in 2022 and lagged by 0.68% in 2023. Dimon's letter of 4 Apr 2023 draws the policy conclusion: "many of the new 'shadow bank' market makers are fair-weather friends."

**April 2025 (tariff shock).**
- *Authorities.* Fed Financial Stability Report (25 Apr 2025): corporate bond liquidity was "in line with the average level observed in recent years but deteriorated with the higher volatility in early April." The Nov 2025 report says Treasury market liquidity "hit historically low levels."
- *Dealers.* JPMorgan's CFO was "a little bit surprised by the resilience of the Markets revenues" (15 Jul 2025); Citi reported "lower credit trading"; Goldman said "clients quickly re-levered"; Morgan Stanley's derivatives desk "actively supported clients." Trumid's Pender credits dealers with "resilient performance ... particularly in April."
- *Non-banks.* Citadel Securities cites "tariffs" among its four episodes; Flow Traders reports volatility that "drove elevated activity" in 2025 but cut fixed-income capital anyway. Tradeweb's CEO says March 2026 volatility was "nearly 40% below" April 2025.

**March 2026 (outside the brief, in the data).** JPMorgan's Barnum saw no "bad volatility" and fixed income revenue rose 21%; Morgan Stanley booked a "postcrisis record" $3.4B of fixed income; Virtu's Adjusted Net Trading Income rose 58.2%. Stress months now pay the market maker; the open question is who bears the tail when the market gaps.

**Read-across for the desk.** In each event the same pattern repeats: (1) price discovery moves to ETFs and electronic venues first; (2) dealers' balance-sheet statements are self-reported and favourable; (3) the buy side reports lost liquidity; (4) the ex-post proof is a regulator's or academic's dataset, which I found in depth for 2020 and only in outline for 2025. A systematic credit desk should log its own no-quote and markout statistics by regime rather than trust any seat's account.

### 5B.1 Citadel Securities: a market maker that left credit once and came back

In January 2017 Paul Hamill, then Citadel Securities' global head of fixed income, told Bloomberg Television that off-the-run and less liquid debt "is an area very heavily dominated by the banks" and "much more focused on relationship-driven trading" (Lisa Abramowicz, Bloomberg Gadfly column, 13 Jan 2017, republished at wealthmanagement.com). The same column records that in 2011 the firm had cut its distressed-debt team and shut a research group, so the 2017 push into off-the-run Treasuries was the second attempt at a less liquid product after a retreat. Credit proper came six years later. In July 2023 The DESK (Dan Barnes, 19 Jul 2023) reported that Citadel had made markets in US investment-grade credit since June 2023, streaming prices through MarketAxess and Tradeweb, and quoted its head of fixed income distribution Jordan Cila: "Credit was a natural expansion for us as it lives at the nexus of our fixed income and ETF franchises." Bob Cariste, head of fixed income ETF trading, added: "The credit market is less electronified than some of the other markets we're active in."

The sentence that matters for a desk comes from a buy-side doubter in the same article, Antoine Marmoiton of Alken Asset Management: "No one wants to hold much inventory that is not hedged very well." That is the whole Citadel model in a line. It enters credit through the ETF creation-and-redemption arbitrage and through Treasury and CDX hedges, which is why it began with investment grade and why this dossier infers it avoids a bond whose risk the hedge cannot carry (inference, not Citadel's words). The build-out followed in order: Erin Pomerantz moved from COO of rates to COO of credit trading in October 2023; Vishnu Murthy (HSBC), David Moe (Morgan Stanley) and Zack Siam (Deutsche Bank) were hired to cover investment grade by January 2024 (The DESK, 4 Jan 2024), a period the same article describes as one of "increased capital costs which may limit investment banks' market making capabilities." By 19 Nov 2025 Sam Berberian, global head of credit trading, was launching quoted baskets of Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and Meta bonds (10-year and 30-year) so that investors could "quickly" add or cut exposure to AI capital spending (Bloomberg News, Caleb Mutua, 19 Nov 2025; paywalled after the first lines, so only the paraphrase is verified). A market maker that packages a theme into a hedgeable basket is showing where it earns: it prices the basket against its own book of the underlying bonds and the ETFs.

### 5B.2 Jane Street: the argument it made before the test

In a paper dated 23 July (year not printed; the Bloomberg report carried on thewealthadvisor.com is dated 29 Jul 2019, so almost certainly 2019), Jane Street ETF strategist Andrew Upward wrote: "While credit ETFs will become more costly to trade in a stressed market, they don't pose a systemic risk." He argued market makers "have an incentive to stay in the game, rather than blindly feeding a slump," and that "If selling the underlying bonds is difficult or expensive, selling ETFs will become expensive, too." The paper answered European regulators who asked whether authorised participants would keep creating and redeeming in stress. Eight months later March 2020 was the test, and the FSB reported that ETFs "became one of the key mechanisms for price discovery during the dash for cash" (section 5), which is Upward's prediction in the regulator's words, with the premium that the cost to trade was indeed high. The accurate reading for the book: a market maker predicted its own role and was, on the public record, largely right about price discovery. It never claimed ETF prices would stay near NAV, and its paper is a firm's advocacy, not an independent test.

The funding story is the second half of the Jane Street seat. On 28 Apr 2025 The DESK (Etienne Mercuriali) reported a US$1.35 billion 6.75% senior secured note due 2033, launched 23 April and settled 25 April in the middle of the tariff shock, taking cumulative issuance to US$5.4 billion since January 2024. Pro forma long-term debt was about US$10.7 billion against members' equity near US$30 billion; the US$1 billion revolver was fully drawn as of mid-April; there was a US$6.4 billion liquidity buffer; net trading revenue almost doubled in 2024 to US$20.5 billion. The DESK's explanation for the borrowing: "Long-dated debt helps proprietary dealers hold trading capital above regulatory and internal risk requirements" (paraphrase) and lets them carry larger, more volatile positions. S&P called the deal "illustrative of the firm's comparatively higher risk appetite." Put next to the September 2026 US$14.6 billion issue in section 2.2, the arc is plain: the "internally funded" capital base now has a credit curve, and the market makers have begun to look like the dealers they replaced, with one difference that matters: their creditors can price it.

### 5B.4 Dealers in their own words: the bridge book in March 2020 and 2022

For a restructuring banker the cleanest dealer-seat fact is not trading revenue but the underwriting commitment a dealer carries between signing a leveraged deal and syndicating it, the bridge book. Two JPMorgan calls show the same seat at two stress points.

*14 Apr 2020 (Q1 2020 call, Jennifer Piepszak, CFO, and Jamie Dimon; Fool transcript, fetched 18 Oct 2026).* The trading desk made money from the panic: "Fixed income was up 34%, driven by strong client activity, most notably in rates and currencies and emerging markets", with total Markets revenue of $7.2 billion, up 32% (paraphrase of the call's figures). The underwriting side lost: "the investment grade debt market remained open" but "the high yield market was effectively closed, and high yield spreads widened significantly," and "our bridge book commitments were marked down by $820 million." Dimon added the explanation a banker would give a client: "we know that we have a bridge loan book that you're going to have quarters where things get bad." So the same franchise was a profitable liquidity provider in rates and a loss-taker on committed leveraged-finance paper in the same quarter. Goldman's Q2 2020 release (SEC Ex. 99.1) shows the recovery leg: FICC net revenues of $4.24 billion against $1.70 billion a year earlier, with intermediation up "particularly in interest rate products, credit products and commodities" (the filing's financing figure is internally inconsistent, $499 million in text and $449 million in the table, so it is not used here).

*14 Jul 2022 (Q2 2022 call, Barnum and Dimon; Fool transcript).* The pattern reversed. Fixed income rose on "elevated volatility drove both increased client flows and robust trading results in the macro franchise," partly offset by "credit unsecuritized products in a challenging spread environment" (Barnum). Investment banking revenue was $1.4 billion, down 61% (53% excluding bridge-book markdowns of about $250 million); debt underwriting fees fell 53%. Barnum on the strategy: "we made conscious choices here to dial back our risk appetite and accepted some share losses in leveraged finance." Dimon: "Our percent of that bridge book has come down substantially just in the last 12 months," adding (paraphrase) that the Street's bridge book was about $480 billion in 2007 and was then under $100 billion, of which JPMorgan held roughly 20% (Barnum). On capital, Barnum said buybacks were paused after a higher stress capital buffer, RWA fell about $44 billion in the quarter and CET1 was 12.2%; Dimon: "we dramatically reduced RWA this quarter." The Q3 2022 call (14 Oct 2022) adds Dimon on leveraged loans: "There are no real levels of loan write-down this quarter, and that market isn't yet clear," and on Basel: "we know we have to deal with Basel IV. We don't know when and how this is going to be." Fixed income rose 22% on "elevated volatility ... strong client activity in the macro franchise."

What this shows. The largest dealer describes credit as two businesses with opposite signs in stress: a flow business (days-long holds, paid by volatility) and a commitment business (months-long holds, hurt when issuers cannot refinance). By mid-2022 the dealer chose to shrink the second to protect capital, which is the mechanism behind the private-credit and LME-financing share gains elsewhere in this book (inference). A desk that trades LMT-affected paper should know which of those two businesses is across the table.

### 5B.5 Heads of credit trading and e-trading, in their own words

The first pass found no speaking heads of credit trading. Three public interviews fill part of the gap. None is a head of credit trading at Bank of America, Goldman or Morgan Stanley speaking about credit specifically; searches turned up appointment notices and webinars, not interviews, so those seats stay represented by earnings calls only.

*Jefferies, October 2023 (The DESK, Dan Barnes, 26 Oct 2023, EMCA 2023 winner profile).* Fred Orlan, global head of fixed income (and, per a university alumni page, Barclays' global head of credit trading 2011-2014), is the clearest published statement of a mid-sized dealer's economics: "It's hard for investment banks to run large balance sheets in this environment because it isn't making a lot of money"; "You're really making your money trading with and helping clients, which requires good ideas and good execution"; and "We might not provide as much overall liquidity as the top two or three banks at a given point in time." Emmanuel Parrenin, head of business and resource management for EMEA Fixed Income: "our business model does not rely on an extension of the balance sheet to generate revenues." Fred Jallot, head of EMEA Fixed Income, on the product: "Each time a client picks up the phone to discuss a block, they're taking a risk," and clients "would like to see us offering portfolio trading and blocks." The one credit-event anecdote is Jefferies's SVB claims-trading desk: Jallot says the desk "managed to source around US$15 billion of buying interest on the deposits" and built "a legal structure to transfer deposits both in Europe and in the US to trade these claims." That is a dealer acting as a distressed-claims intermediary in March 2023, which is the nearest public analogue of what an RSSG alumnus would do on a desk.

*Goldman Sachs, January 2024 (Coalition Greenwich webinar, Kevin McPartland, "The Present and Future State of Corporate Bond Portfolio Trading").* William Wolcott, partner in credit trading: "Portfolio trades are a way to interact with those custom baskets." The numbers attributed to Goldman: about 20% of its institutional corporate bond volume is portfolio trading, 80:20 investment grade to high yield; about 8,000 portfolio trades for more than 300 clients in 2023, averaging roughly $60 million across about 100 CUSIPs (paraphrase of the Greenwich page). On the same page Trumid's Paris Pender names the dealer's problem: "that risk ultimately needs to find a home," because "clients initiate portfolio trades, but dealers cannot send them back in the other direction," and "It's an imperfectly solved problem right now." Pender's view of Q4 2023 is that dealers "were very efficient in providing quotes and liquidity, and pricing was reliable." State Street Global Advisors' Edmund Griffin, a buy-side voice: "if the list is 60-70 items long, it's the first person to get to that bond wins."

*JPMorgan, 9 May 2025 (J.P. Morgan "Making Sense" podcast, Market Matters, "A new era of credit portfolio trading?", recorded 23 Apr 2025, host Gurps Kharaud).* This is the only dealer-desk voice on April 2025 found. Olivier Cajfinger, head of IG credit, public finance, short-term credit and alternative sales, on the sell-off: portfolio trading "has been crucial for risk transfer during these times," "there were days where portfolio trading made up to 25% of trades flow," IG portfolio trading volumes were "up by 30 to 35% year-to-date," and "PT now represents between 12 to 15% of the overall traded volume in U.S. IG." Peter Grant, co-head of credit systematic trading: "it's turned into a liquidity aggregation problem," "It's very important for dealers to be able to internalize the liquidity," and "dealers are really only as strong as their weakest link." The title of Grant's seat, co-head of credit systematic trading, is itself a data point: the largest dealer now has a desk whose name is the book's subject.

*The dealers as a group: the Fed's June 2025 Senior Credit Officer survey (SCOOS), fetched and read this session.* The supervisor asked dealers directly how they run portfolio trades, which is the closest thing to a collective dealer-seat disclosure. Nearly two-thirds of dealers were active in credit portfolio trading, and four-fifths of those had raised their provision since January 2023. When deciding whether to take a basket, four-fifths rated its composition "very important," two-thirds market conditions, and about half each the client relationship and balance-sheet availability. To manage what they bought, roughly four-fifths rated trading out of the individual constituents by other protocols "very important"; ETF creation and redemption and offsetting portfolio trades mattered to about three-fourths and one-half; three-fifths "frequently hedge" with credit ETFs when the bonds are not in the creation basket, and about half use credit default swaps at least in a few situations. High-grade bonds are frequent in baskets for all active dealers, high yield for three-fifths, leveraged loans for one-fifth. On the stress: "on net, one-fifth of the active dealers" said their willingness and ability to do portfolio trades, relative to single-name market making, "decreased somewhat" in April 2025, while the core questions for March to May 2025 found that "liquidity and market functioning remained basically unchanged" across asset classes (survey summary, prepared by Yuriy Kitsul, Fed Board; paraphrase of the summary, with short quotes). Two inferences for the book: the ETF is the dealers' main hedge for a basket, so a bond outside the creation basket (a stressed or LMT-affected credit) is the hardest line to warehouse; and in the one stress the survey covers, dealers' own account is mild, in contrast with the buy-side account in section 5. The March 2026 survey (same publisher, fetched) covers March 2026 at the core level: about one-sixth of dealers saw liquidity and market functioning in high-grade and high-yield corporate bonds "improved somewhat," and nearly all dealers who expected to expand secured financing for corporate bonds in 2026 cited increased availability of balance sheet capital as a very or somewhat important reason (about two-fifths "very important" for high grade and one-half for high yield).

Citi, December 2020 (The TRADE, Hayley McDowell, 15 Dec 2020, on an internal memo): Derek Hafer, previously head of US investment-grade credit trading, became global head of spread-products portfolio trading, electronic client connectivity and North America macro trading, "his main responsibility is risk pricing" (paraphrase). The memo says "portfolio trading has grown rapidly in the flow credit markets" and "We expect this trend to continue." The article cites an August 2020 JPMorgan client report: JPMorgan executed 77% of its prior-year portfolio-trading volume, portfolio trades up 141% on the year, and "huge uptake" from US buy-side clients in the 2020 volatility. The 2020 stress is therefore when the dealer-side portfolio-trading org charts were drawn.

### 5B.6 March 2020 as the market makers and venues told it, set against the ETF discounts

*Flow Traders (Q1 2020 trading update, 21 Apr 2020, fetched and read).* The ETP market maker reported net trading income of EUR 495.0m against EUR 46.1m in Q4 2019 and EUR 63.1m in Q1 2019: roughly eight to ten times a normal quarter, on an EBITDA margin of 65%. Its own ETP value traded rose 76% quarter on quarter; market ETP value traded rose 100% to EUR 10.9 trillion. Chief Trading Officer Folkert Joling: "we saw substantially increased volumes and heightened risk, as well as corresponding widening of spreads," and "the ETF mechanism performed as intended." The release adds "No loss days were recorded in the quarter," trading capital of EUR 771m, regulatory own-funds requirement of EUR 261m and excess capital of EUR 273m at 31 Mar 2020. CEO Dennis Dijkstra: "More liquidity leads to less volatility and contributes to market stability." Read with the Bank of England's statement (via Investment Executive, 21 Sep 2020) that "in mid-March, some of the largest ETFs in both the investment-grade and high-yield corporate bond segments recorded NAV discounts in excess of 5%, having been no larger than 0.1% in January" (paraphrase of the Bank's May 2020 report; the article gives the figures), the two accounts do not contradict each other. A market maker earns the width of a widened spread on a record volume, with no loss days, precisely because the ETF price moved to where it could clear. The cost was borne by those who needed to sell at a 5% discount. The BIS (April 2020), quoted in the same article: "ETF prices react to new information more quickly than NAVs do." Vanguard Canada's Scott Johnston in the same piece: "If you held individual fixed-income [securities] in March and you needed liquidity, you couldn't get it." The lesson for a credit desk is that a spread-widening regime pays the intermediary who is hedged and positioned to be a price-setter and pays nothing to the price-taker.

*MarketAxess (Q2 2020 release, filed with the SEC; quarter of April to June 2020).* CEO Rick McVey: "Record corporate bond new issuance and elevated credit spread volatility led to record corporate bond market volumes" and "Institutional investors and dealers both leaned into our Open Trading marketplace to meet their trading and liquidity needs." Credit volume was a record $741.8 billion (up 44%), Open Trading a record $243.8 billion (up 87%), estimated US high-grade share a record 21.5% (from 18.7%), operating margin 56.4%. The venue's share of US high grade later slid to 18.4% in 2025 (section 4.1), so 2020 is the high-water mark of the all-to-all pitch. A search summary of the Q1 2020 release (29 Apr 2020; not fetched) puts Q1 credit volume at $659.7 billion and says McVey described March as a record month for credit volumes (UNVERIFIED). Taken with Man Numeric's 2023 line that the biggest jumps in electronic trading came in 2020 and 2022, the venue and the buy-side agree on direction: stress drives the flow to screens.

*What the three do not say.* None gives credit-specific market-maker revenue. Flow Traders' release states that results reflect "the focus and investment in recent quarters on our US operation as well as in fixed income," but no fixed-income number is split out, so the claim that credit ETPs drove the quarter is not supportable from this source.

### 5B.7 BlackRock in March 2020: three seats in one firm

The earlier BlackRock section (3.4) flagged two items as unsourced: the firm's role as the Fed's corporate-facility manager and the ETF discount episode. Both are now primary-sourced, and together with the systematic arm they make BlackRock the one firm in this dossier that sat on three sides of the same bond market in the same month.

*The ETF sponsor (Q1 2020 call, 16 Apr 2020; Fool machine transcript, fetched).* Salim Ramji, global head of iShares: "the iShares investment grade bond ETF LQD traded almost 90,000 times" on 12 March "while its top five holdings traded an average of 37 times." He said the flagship fixed-income iShares "tracked performance at just 3 basis points in the quarter" while "competitors tracked in the double-digits," and predicted "fixed income ETFs will be a $2 trillion market over the next four years." Asked by Patrick Davitt (Autonomous Research) about grumbling over the Fed buying ETFs, Larry Fink replied "I object to your -- the way you framed it as a bailout." The sponsor's argument is the same as Jane Street's 2019 argument (section 5B.2): in a freeze the ETF is where trades happen. The same quarter the Bank of England put some of the largest IG and HY corporate bond ETFs at NAV discounts over 5% in mid-March (section 5B.6). Both facts are true; they describe the same event as liquidity (volume) and as price (discount).

*The Fed's agent (NY Fed "Terms of Assignment", 27 Mar 2020, fetched and read via text extraction).* BlackRock's Financial Markets Advisory unit was assigned to manage the Secondary Market Corporate Credit Facility on behalf of the New York Fed, "pending completion of Investment Management Agreement," and "No trades shall be executed by BlackRock until the Investment Management Agreement is completed and publicly disclosed." The conflict provisions are the interesting part for a book on seats: staff on the engagement were "segregated from other BlackRock engagements and activities"; BlackRock charges "no asset management fee on the value of any exchange traded funds" held by the Facility and credits back "all underlying fees and income that BlackRock earns (including for securities lending) on its own iShares ETFs" in the Facility; "BlackRock will treat BlackRock-sponsored ETFs on the same neutral footing as third-party ETFs"; and if the Facility's holding of BlackRock ETFs exceeds BlackRock's market share of corporate bond ETFs, the firm must notify the New York Fed, which "may direct portfolio adjustments at any time." The Fed (23 Mar 2020, section 5) announced the facility; the terms show that the dealer-of-last-resort was designed with an explicit conflict-of-interest architecture because the manager sells the product it was buying. On the call Gary Shedlin gave the CMBS-facility fee schedule as 2 basis points on the first $20 billion and 1.25 on the next $30 billion (paraphrase of the fetch tool's summary).

*The systematic arm.* BlackRock Systematic Fixed Income is a third, much smaller seat (section 3.4). Its paper on high yield (undated) argues that average managers sit defensive at the wrong time; the March 2020 sequence (sell-off, facility announcement on 23 March, recovery) is the case that supports the mean-reversion claim, though the paper's quoted figures are for 2022 and 2023 (eVestment), not 2020 (the 2020 link is mine, not the authors').

### 5B.8 AQR and D. E. Shaw: two ways a quant shop can live off the dealers' constraints

*AQR (corrects section 3.2, which said no credit title was on the research page).* AQR's own white paper "Systematic Credit Investing" (April Frieda and Scott Richardson; undated, but its hypothetical data run to December 2014 and the PDF metadata show November 2016; fetched and text-extracted this session) states the factory plan. Credit is "a unique asset class within the broader class of fixed income"; the spread is a function of default probability and loss given default ("Credit Spread proportional to PD x LGD," Equation 1); the active book it simulates is 10% market timing and 90% relative value and is implemented through CDS and CDS indices, not cash bonds. The authors say why: TRACE gave "transparency to the previously opaque over-the-counter corporate bond market," CDS had become "completely standardized, fungible contracts since 2009," and "Electronic trading has become the norm for indices, but it is still at the early stage for single names." Their conclusion: "We believe these developments in credit markets have created a unique opportunity to take a systematic approach to credit investing now." Market timing "is difficult and should only be used moderately" (paraphrase of a line on weights). So the AQR seat was designed around the dealers' constraint rather than against it: it avoided the bond inventory problem by trading the instruments that dealers had already standardized.

Two AQR papers by Diogo Palhares and Scott Richardson then make the seat's empirical case. (Il)liquidity Premium in Credit Markets: A Myth? (AQR working paper dated 31 Dec 2018; Journal of Fixed Income, Winter 2019, 28(3)) finds "only limited evidence of a liquidity premium in the cross section of corporate bonds" and that "portfolios that tilt toward (away from) less (more) liquid bonds exhibit considerably higher levels of volatility." Looking Under the Hood of Active Credit Managers (working paper 1 Jul 2019; Financial Analysts Journal 76(2), 2020, pp. 82-102): "credit long/short managers tend to have high passive exposure to the credit risk premium," while high-yield long-only managers have less exposure than their benchmarks, and neither group has "economically meaningful exposures to well-compensated systematic factors." In the search summary of the sample (UNVERIFIED beyond the abstract): 219 credit hedge funds and 96 mutual funds, 1997 to mid-2018, with about half of the median hedge-fund manager's variation explained by traditional premia. For the book, AQR's useful stance is a negative one on illiquidity: the firm argues that a desk should not be paid to hold the illiquid bond as such, which is the opposite of a distressed fund's thesis and sets up the cleanest "same bond, two seats" contrast with D. E. Shaw's below.

*D. E. Shaw: the other end of the dealer's balance sheet (press release, 1 May 2025, fetched and text-extracted).* The firm raised $1.3 billion for Diopter Fund II, "a private credit-focused fund" expected "to primarily pursue capital optimization investments, which are designed to help financial institutions enhance balance-sheet and business-model efficiency." The Diopter series (launched 2022) pursues "capital relief, capital optimization, risk transfer, risk sharing, or other similar transactions"; first private credit fund 2008; "more than $5 billion" raised for private credit including the Alkali series; the teams consist of "approximately 200 investment, technology, data analysis, and research professionals"; Managing Director Edwin Jager, who oversees the fundamental equities and credit work, said: "We believe that our extensive experience with capital optimization transactions, along with our culture of innovation and analytical rigor, make us an attractive partner for financial institutions seeking tailored solutions." Group investment capital was "more than $65 billion" at 1 Mar 2025 (the earlier dossier's "$115 billion of investment and committed capital" at 1 Sep 2026 is a different measure from a different page, so treat the two as unreconciled). The Alkali series is the older, less liquid sleeve: Alkali Fund VI raised $1 billion in commitments (Opalesque, 14 Nov 2024), targeted "less liquid credit and credit-related opportunities across private and public markets" and holds corporate debt, structured credit, synthetic securitizations and litigation-related investments (Alternatives Watch via StoneX, 12 Sep 2023; D. E. Shaw declined to comment on the fund).

The inference for the reader (mine, not D. E. Shaw's) is that the same regulatory pressure that made dealers shrink bridge books and balance sheets (section 5B.4) is D. E. Shaw's supply of risk: banks pay a quant-fundamental shop to take a slice of their portfolio's tail (synthetic securitizations and risk-sharing), and the dealer's constraint becomes the manager's edge. That would be a seat built on balance-sheet scarcity rather than on information, and its horizon is years, not days.
