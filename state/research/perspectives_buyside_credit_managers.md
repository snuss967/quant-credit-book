## 0. The one-paragraph read

Every buy-side seat in this set is defined by the rule that makes it patient or forced: Apollo, KKR and Blackstone's insurance books by a spread target and a capital charge; Ares, HPS and Sixth Street by a hold-and-amend lending model and, increasingly, by retail redemption gates; Oaktree, Silver Point and the distressed funds by price discipline and vote arithmetic; Elliott by a legal process; CLO managers by a 7.5% CCC bucket and OC tests; insurers by NAIC risk-based capital. The same houses appear on opposite sides of different deals (co-op defendants in Optimum, uptier participants elsewhere), so firm identity is a weaker predictor of behaviour than the position's seat and the rule that binds it.

## 1. Apollo (opportunistic credit, hybrid value, Athene)

Funding is Athene's annuity balance sheet, third-party insurers, wealth vehicles (ADS), the PRIV ETF with State Street (about 22.1% Apollo-sourced at 15 Oct 2026, carried) and institutional "fixed income replacement" mandates.

**How Apollo looks at our bond.** The desk sees a B-rated 2029 as spread per unit of DTS and a jump-to-default haircut over 20 days. The overlap is Apollo's dislocation seat (0-50% LTV), where it competes for the same bonds when spreads gap and is a natural block buyer of the desk's inventory. ## 4. Elliott (public letters, positions in credit events)

**Seat and the public-voice gap.** Elliott (Paul Singer) is a multi-strategy hedge fund and activist: it buys defaulted sovereign and corporate debt, term loans and bonds, joins or forms creditor groups, sues, and will buy the asset. Horizon runs to the legal catalyst (Argentina took 15 years); funding is permanent partner capital and long lock-ups. Elliott's investor letters are private, and no verifiable Elliott credit letter or quote was reachable this session (a Fa-Mag report of Singer's remarks on private credit returned 403 and is not quoted). Its public voice in credit is its litigation, filings and the vehicles it controls.

**Roles in named events.**
- *Argentina (default 2001; settled 29 Feb 2016):* NPR reports a hedge-fund group "led by Paul Singer's Elliott Management" refused the restructurings and sued; Judge Griesa barred paying other creditors without paying the holdouts; Argentina agreed to pay four holdouts $4.653B, "about 75 percent of what they claimed they were owed." A search summary names the four as Elliott, Aurelius, Davidson Kempner and Bracebridge.
- *Citgo/PDV Holding (Nov 2025): winning bidder through Amber Energy.* On 25 Nov 2025 Judge Leonard Stark (D. Del.) approved a $5.9B bid from an Elliott affiliate; it "offers a $2.1 billion payment to the holders of a defaulted Venezuelan bond collateralized with Citgo equity," and Stark wrote that "The Amber Bid offers the best overall combination of price and certainty of closing of any bid submitted" (Reuters via Pipeline & Gas Journal). Objector Gold Reserve alleged conflicts, including "$170 million in fees collected by the special master's advisors from affiliates of Elliott and the 2020 bondholders" (an allegation). OFAC approval and Third Circuit appeals were pending (search summary; check).
- *Veritas (2024):* Elliott and allies, holding more than half of $2.5B of loans and $1.8B of bonds, objected to Carlyle's plan that they said paid under 60 cents in cash and "have already formed their own 'co-operation agreement'" (Hedgeweek, 25 Jun 2024, citing the FT).
- *Thames Water (2025-26):* Class A consortium with Apollo and Silver Point (Hedgeweek, 3 Sep 2025). *Braskem (Jun 2026):* bought revolver pieces and international bonds, expected to resist the company's extension-only proposal, acting independently (Hedgeweek, citing Bloomberg).

**How Elliott looks at our bond.** For Elliott a bond is an option on a legal process. It reads the indenture for the clause that creates blocking power (one-third, two-thirds, majority), treats the court as the final counterparty, and buys the position that holds the veto. A thirty-cent bond the desk treats as a jump-to-default is, to Elliott, a claim whose value depends on a ruling, a sale order or a co-op. It is the buyer of last resort for paper whose short-horizon holders are forced sellers (CLOs at the CCC bucket, index funds), and the party that turns "excluded lender" into "winning bidder." The desk should model such holders as a state variable: when a known blocker accumulates, the hazard of a non-pro-rata deal falls and recovery variance rises.

**How the distressed seat looks at our bond.** To Silver Point or Monarch a 60-cent bond is a claim on a distribution of value; the questions are which tranche is the fulcrum and who holds the blocking position. They do the vote arithmetic first (Incora: 59% of the 2026s, then issue notes to cross two-thirds) and pay for documents and counsel. The desk's equivalent of their NDA is a price that begins to discount an LMT. They are natural buyers when CLOs and mutual funds are forced to sell and natural sellers when the claim becomes thin post-reorg equity; the desk can provide the bid on the way in and the exit on the way out, but a known distressed holder in a name raises the hazard of a coalition-conditional outcome.

## 7. CLO managers (CCC buckets, OC/IC tests, reinvestment)

**Seat.** A CLO manager buys broadly syndicated or middle-market senior loans with a locked-in stack (AAA to BB debt plus equity) that reinvests for 3-5 years; the 2025 market saw $232B of resets and $105B of refinancings (Eagle Point Income Co. letter, Thomas Majewski, 18 Feb 2026), and refi/reset reinvestment periods averaged 3.03 years in Sep 2026 (CRE Daily, carried). The manager earns fees on assets and often holds equity; it is paid for keeping the tests passing, not for beating a benchmark. The constraints, verbatim (Kundu, ESRB Working Paper 141, March 2023): "A CLO faces a limit on loans rated CCC/Caa1 or below, typically set to 7.5%. The loans in excess of this percentage are marked at the lowest market value of the loans in the CCC/Caa1 bucket"; a discount obligation stays at purchase price "until the loan trades above a specified threshold (typically 90 ¢/$) for more than 30 days"; and "the median CLO operates within 4% of the most stringent covenant threshold." Majewski's letter: market-wide CCC exposure averaged 4.3% (his fund 5.3%) and junior OC cushions 3.9% (his fund 4.7%).

**Buy and sell triggers.** Buys: par-ish B2/B3 loans that keep the equity arbitrage open, and discounted loans below 90 that build par. Sells: a downgrade that pushes the CCC bucket above 7.5%, a price drop that squeezes the OC numerator, an LME that triggers an SD/D then CCC rating, or a WARF or diversity breach. Kundu's finding is that the haircut-at-lowest-price rule makes managers sell their higher-priced CCCs first ("contractual arbitrage"), so the forced seller often sells the wrong bond.

**Manager voices.** Loomis Sayles' Full Discretion team (head Matt Eagan), Q4 2025 letter (Jan 2026), a large loan manager and Optimum co-op defendant (CLO-manager status unverified): "We believe we are firmly in late cycle and we see low risk of downturn in 2026," yet "we do believe the risk of liability management exercises will remain elevated as not all these maturities are refinanceable." Majewski, a CLO-equity investor, notes "captive CLO equity funds, which tend to be less sensitive to performance returns."

**Roles in named events.**
- *Serta (2020 uptier; Fifth Circuit, 31 Dec 2024):* the prevailing lenders were Barings, Boston Management and Research, Credit Suisse Asset Management, Eaton Vance and Invesco Senior Secured Management, providing "$200 million in new financing in exchange for $200 million in first-out, super-priority debt" and exchanging "$1.2 billion of their first-lien and second-lien loans for approximately $875 million in second-out, super-priority debt." The objectors were "Excluded Lenders" and "LCM Lenders" (LCM XXII Ltd., a CLO issuer, appears in a cited case caption; the CLO status is background knowledge). The court held: "the 2020 Uptier was not a permissible open market purchase within the meaning of the 2016 Agreement" (https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/opinions/pub/23/23-20181-CV0.pdf). The indemnity clause also names an "Apollo Proceeding." Loan managers sat on both sides of one deal.
- *Revlon (2020):* Symphony, Bardin Hill, Greywolf, ZAIS, Investcorp Credit Management and Highland Fund Advisors are among the "Loan Managers" sued in the wire case (CLO-manager status is background knowledge, not stated in the opinion).
- *Robertshaw (2023):* Invesco bought over 50% of first- and second-out loans to become Required Lender; the Bain/Canyon/Eaton Vance ad hoc group countered (carried). *Anchorage (Sep 2026):* took over 11 ORIX CLO vehicles (about $4.7B), reaching 60 vehicles and about $26.1B (CRE Daily, carried).

**How the CLO manager looks at our bond.** It asks whether the loan keeps the portfolio inside seven numbers (CCC share, WARF, diversity, spread, WAL, OC and IC cushions) and whether it can still reinvest. A single-B loan at 99 yielding SOFR+350 is ideal; the same name at 78 after an LME is a CCC whose mark hits the OC numerator. The desk's bid for a downgraded loan therefore competes with a seller who wants out at the cheapest approved moment, and the best entry is often just after month-end test dates. A CLO cannot easily hold equity from an exchange, which biases managers toward deals that deliver first-out loans and explains why they appear on both sides of the table.

## 8. Life insurers and private-credit allocations (Athene, NAIC)

**Seat.** Annuity and funding-agreement writers buy long-duration, high-quality spread assets against fixed-rate liabilities. Athene's net invested assets were $292B at year-end 2025 (up 18%), blended net spread ex-notables was 120 bps in Q4, and 2025 inflows were a record $83B (retail $34B, funding agreements $35B, reinsurance $12B) (Zelter, 9 Feb 2026). Horizon is maturity; the binding constraint is NAIC risk-based capital (RBC) and ratings. The Fed (Carlino, Foley-Fisher, Heinrich and Verani, 21 Mar 2025): life-insurer exposure to BSL and MM CLOs reached "a record $212 billion by the end of 2023"; insurers with affiliated asset managers controlled "$4.1 trillion, accounting for about 72 percent of the industry"; "insurers holding a portfolio of B-rated loans can cut their risk-based capital charges by two-thirds if they package those loans into a CLO and purchase the entire CLO capital stack," and by "a factor of 10" for middle-market loans.

**NAIC rules (Sidley, 9 Jul 2026).** In June 2026 the NAIC adopted new C-1 factors for CLOs effective for year-end 2026 reporting, keeping the 45% residual-tranche charge and adding an 11.77% pretax surcharge for below-investment-grade BSL CLO tranches 4% thick or thinner, measured on the latest trustee report; and, from year-end 2027, an overcollateralization-based framework for collateral loans replacing the uniform 6.8% charge (residual base 45%, falling to 22.5% at OC of 200% or more, with independent verification and a 50% haircut floor). A NAIC summary (carried, CHECK; page returned 403) says discretion over filing-exempt designations became operational in Aug 2026.

**Roles in named events.** Insurers appear through affiliated managers: Allstate Investment Management is a defendant-appellee in the Revlon wire case; PGIM (Prudential) is an Optimum co-op defendant; Barings (MassMutual's manager, background knowledge) was a Serta prevailing lender; Apollo, an Athene affiliate, joined the Carvana group. Athene itself has no verified named-event role in the sources fetched.

**How the insurer looks at our bond.** The question is capital charge per unit of spread: a BBB- private placement at T+250 with a clean NAIC designation beats a BB public bond at T+300. The insurer will not sell because a position moved five points; it sells when a designation, rating or downgrade-to-HY raises the charge. For the desk, insurer demand is slow, sticky and rule-driven, with discontinuities at NAIC calendar dates (year-end 2026 CLO factors, Aug 2026 SVO discretion), and the regulatory shock case is a forced flow from private placements and MM-CLO mezzanine into public BBB/BB.
