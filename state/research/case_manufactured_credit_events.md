A Windstream price of 29.5 paid 70.5. ## 5. Legal and structural mechanics

**Failure to Pay.** A missed payment of at least the Payment Requirement ($1mm, North American), after the Grace Period, under any Obligation. If the instrument has no grace period for principal, a three-Grace-Period-Business-Day period is deemed, and without Grace Period Extension it ends no later than the scheduled termination date (iHeart: event date 20 Dec 2016) [S26]. The DC looks only at public information and applies the text: in iHeart the trustee's notice said the affiliate holder "did not waive its underlying right to be repaid" and no supplemental indenture moved the maturity, so the due date stood; the DC did not ask why the issuer withheld [S26, S22]. There is no cross-default or acceleration requirement, which is why a $1.04mm payment can be a credit event.

**The clause that made it legal.** The Statement of Information to the DC (13 Mar 2018) cites s.11.1(b)(iii) of the 2014 Definitions (s.9.1(b)(iii) of the 2003) and *Good Hill Master Fund v. Deutsche Bank*, 146 A.D.3d 632 (1st Dep't 2017), which held a CDS party could act "without regard to the existence of the swap or any adverse effect it might have on [the counterparty]'s position". Swain's opinion never reached the merits; Solus's claims ended in a no-payment, no-admission dismissal [S3, S7].

**Deliverables.** Payout is 1 minus the price of the cheapest deliverable. For North American corporates the 2014 Definitions' characteristics are: Bond or Loan; Not Subordinated; Specified Currency; Assignable Loan; Consent Required Loan; Transferable; Maximum Maturity 30 years; Not Bearer. The Updated 2003 Definitions add Not Contingent, which the 2014 text replaced with the Outstanding Principal Balance machinery [S14]. Three clauses decided these cases. *Consent Required Loan* (s.3.14(b)(ii), "capable of being assigned or novated with the consent of the Reference Entity ... or any agent"): the Sears panel held an Eligible-Assignee clause that narrowed "but not eliminat[ed]" the buyer pool did not defeat it [S10]. *Borrowed Money* (s.3.13(a)(ii)) and *Transferable*: the Sears challenge argued intercompany paper might not be money borrowed, could not be delivered out of a bankruptcy estate, and might be recharacterised as equity; the DC excluded undocumented programs and admitted the documented series [S9, S12]. *Outstanding Principal Balance* (s.3.8): Windstream's challengers argued the New Notes had no determinable balance and the Aurelius notes might be equitably subordinated to a zero balance; the DC rejected the challenge 15 to 0 and found "insufficient basis" for less than par [S13].

**Auction rules.** The DC holds an auction if at least 300 transactions on auction settlement, confirmed through DTCC and with at least five dealer-members as parties, are outstanding (the Relevant Transaction 300/5 Criteria); otherwise the DC decides by a majority. Net physical interest on one side is matched against opposite limit orders, and the Cap Amount is one-sided as described in §3 [S19, S16]. Where the DC calls "no auction" under s.6.11 of the 2014 Definitions, buyers fall back to physical settlement with a notice cut-off (1 Aug 2025 for the Altice Holding) [S18].

**NTCE (15 Jul 2019).** Where applied (by Confirmation, by the Protocol, or by default on cleared trades from 27 Jan 2020 [S25]), (a) a Failure to Pay does not count unless it "directly or indirectly either result[s] from, or result[s] in, a deterioration in the creditworthiness or financial condition of the Reference Entity"; (b) Outstanding Principal Balance is cut to a straight-line interpolation if a bond is issued below 95% of redemption value, and in an exchange the issue price is deemed to be the tendered bonds' balance, allocated across the new bonds by the Calculation Agent. Exhibit F lists indicators that the requirement is not met: an arrangement with "an essential purpose" of creating a CDS benefit; new debt "likely to be the cheapest-to-deliver" or material extra deliverables; no acceleration; ample liquidity and no technical cause; prompt cure; debt held by affiliates. And indicators that it is met: prior distress, restructuring advisers, a court-supervised process, widely held debt, inability to refinance, other debt accelerating or bankruptcy. A bona fide standstill, or a payment skipped to win hedged creditors into a restructuring that avoids bankruptcy, "rarely" fails the test [S20]. Because it works through Failure to Pay, it does not reach orphaning, non-defaults, or squeezes by deliverable holders.

## 7. Outcome and later rulings

- **Hovnanian:** no credit event; interest cured 30 May 2018 (the company's 8-K says only that the obligation was to pay Sunrise Trail what was due); GSO's financing stood "unchanged"; Solus recovered nothing and was paid nothing [S6, S7]. No court ever ruled that inducing a default is or is not manipulation. As of Dec 2019 no regulator had brought a case (Sidley, secondary) [S27].
- **Sears:** the DC admitted the intercompany notes (12 to 3), the External Review Panel (21 Dec 2018) admitted the loans, expressly "not [a] situation such as might arise through a 'manufactured' set of facts", and reserved the point for restrictions "so commercially unreasonable" as to block assignment. The anonymous request to delay the auction was not granted; it ran on 17 Jan and cleared 24 points above the midpoint [S9, S12, S17].
- **Windstream:** protection that expired between the Dec 2017 acceleration and the Feb 2019 ruling never paid; a request to backdate was rejected; contracts alive on 25 Feb 2019 paid 70.5 [S13, S17].
- **Altice France:** Holding contracts settled only physically; OpCo contracts auctioned at 87.75 after the DC wrote a "Lock-up Settlement Block" fix: where a chain of auction-settled trades could not deliver before Altice's 9 Sep 2025 transfer cut-off because a party was not bound by the lock-up, delivery became a composite package of cash, new notes and equity after the 1 Oct effective date, with the seller compensating the buyer's funding cost in between [S17, S18, S29].
- **Hovnanian settlement terms (secondary):** K&L Gates reports that GSO also agreed "not to support future failure-to-pay events affecting Hovnanian" [S30].
- **The rule fix** (NTCE, 2019-2020) is contractual and elective. Governance changes since: S&P Global Market Intelligence named DC Administrator in June 2026 [S19].

## 8. What a systematic desk could have observed beforehand, and how it would have flagged and traded it

*A reconstruction from documents that were public at the time, not a claim about what any desk did.*

**Hovnanian: the deal explained its own purpose.** In December 2017 an ordinary credit model sees a homebuilder with $463.7mm of cash at 31 October, an 8% note valued by management near 107, and one slot in a 100-name high-yield index [S2, S7, S8]. Nothing says default. Then the documents arrive. Counsel for an ad hoc group writes to the company on 13 November and Bloomberg publishes the plan on the 15th [S2]. The 28 December 8-K shows a lender offering nine-year 5% money to a high-yield builder, a $17.5mm break fee, and a new indenture that bars interest on notes held by one subsidiary [S4]. A screen that asks "is anyone about to default?" has nothing to say. A screen that asks "does any term here make non-payment cheaper than payment?" lights up, and its inputs are countable: the withheld coupon is 0.37% of cash; the holder is a related party with an interest date inside the quarter; a covenant bars paying it; and the two new bonds priced 65 points apart on day one (13.5% 2026s near 113.7, 5% 2040s near 48.8) [S7]. The fifth input, that the lender also held the protection, was visible only to the court [S2], which is why the Committee, limited to public information, could not weigh it [S3, S22].

What the flag buys is a different question rather than a trade. For a protection seller it says the short-dated hazard now contains a jump unrelated to solvency, with a payoff set by the cheapest deliverable: price a binary paying about 51 points whose probability reflects the engineers' appetite for legal risk, and size it so that Solus's arithmetic (about $133mm on $260.5mm) is survivable. For an index holder the event is 0.51% of notional. For a buyer, the front-end spike is someone else's trade, with a trade body (11 April) and a regulator (24 April) leaning against it; under that pressure and counterparty pushback the engineers stood down on 30 May (sec.), and the event never came [S22, S23, S27].

**Sears: the scoreboard was public.** The 8 January Final List already carried $11.6bn of intercompany notes beside seven bonds and a $525mm loan whose agent was c/o ESL Investments [S9, S11, S12]. A challenger had warned on 13 November that such paper "can be created merely at the stroke of a pen" [S9]; the Committee split 7 to 8 on the loan and 12 to 3 on the notes [S9]. By 10:30 on auction day two more numbers arrive: stage-one midpoints spanning 45/50 to 59/64, and physical requests of $70.1mm to buy against $25.1mm to sell [S17]. A squeeze model needs only the sign of net open interest and the ratio of physical demand to free float; with net buying the upside is uncapped (§3), and the anonymous submission put free trading under $1mm a day against $44.9mm of net interest [S12, S17]. The seats mirror each other: a protection seller wants the scarce paper before stage two; a holder of deliverables sells into the stage-two book. A fund that bought $10mm face at the 55.625 midpoint and delivered at 79.875 would have made $2.4mm (arithmetic), if it could find the paper.

**Windstream: the clock was public.** A docket-driven state machine (Chapter 25) would have carried three variables from September 2017: the notice of default and the cure period tolled to 7 December, the trustee's declaratory-judgment motion, and the Committee's two 15 to 0 refusals [S13]. The contract calendar adds a fourth. Standard swaps mature on 20 March, June, September and December, so five maturities fell between the December 2017 acceleration and the February 2019 ruling (my arithmetic); protection bought for those dates could not pay, and the Committee refused to backdate [S13]. The tradeable idea is a calendar spread: own tenors beyond the expected ruling, avoid those that expire inside the gap. The company filed ten days after the ruling [S15].

**Altice France Holding: the clock, and the headcount.** The grace period was arithmetic (coupon due 15 May, grace ending 14 June) and the Committee voted 11 to 0 [S18]. The unusual feature was visible months earlier: a 25 February 2025 framework agreement that, by the Paris court's account, bound over 95% of the OpCo's bonds [S29]. A desk that counts deliverables and locked-up share before the event can predict that the contract will not auction cleanly, that sellers outside the agreement cannot take delivery, and that the Committee will need a bespoke fallback. For the Holding the 300/5 test failed and the contracts moved to physical settlement [S18].

## 9. Which model assumptions broke

Each assumption below is a sentence a pricing model quietly contains; each case broke one.

1. *Default arrives at a rate set by the issuer's finances, independent of who holds the swaps.* At Hovnanian the holder of the largest position became the lender and wrote the missed payment into the indenture [S2].
2. *A credit event measures distress.* The 2014 Definitions measure a missed payment of at least $1mm after grace; $1.04mm against $278.2mm of cash qualified [S2, S7, S26].
3. *Recovery is a property of the issuer.* The payout is one minus the cheapest deliverable, so recovery is a property of the deliverable set, which issuers can enlarge: 5% 2040s, $11.6bn of intercompany notes, $553.7mm of disputed New Notes [S5, S11, S13].
4. *The auction price estimates recovery.* Sears cleared 24.25 points above the midpoint, $24.25mm per $100mm of notional in an afternoon; the three net-buy auctions in the record all cleared above the midpoint (+2.75 to +24.25), while net-sell auctions ran from 29.4 below (Avon, 2024) to 1.5 above (Altice France, 2025), the asymmetry a one-sided cap predicts [S16, S17]. Recent auctions drew 4 to 8 dealers [S17].
5. *The Committee settles ambiguity promptly.* Windstream took 14 months from acceleration to a court answer, and protection that expired in the gap never paid [S13].
6. *Deliverables are fungible and available.* Free trading in Sears was alleged to be under $1mm a day; over 95% of Altice France S.A.'s were locked up [S12, S29].
7. *The bond you hold is the bond that sets the payout.* The old 8s stood near 107 while the payout would have been set by a 22-year bond issued near 48.8, so a hedge built on the old bonds hedged the wrong instrument [S5, S7, S8].
8. *The contract is a constant.* The 2003 Definitions found no Windstream successor where the 2014 text found two; cleared trades carried the NTCE terms from 27 January 2020 while bilateral ones did not unless elected; the Altice decision is silent on which applied [S13, S18, S20, S25].

What to model instead: event probability, deliverable set, cheapest-deliverable price and settlement route as four separate random objects, each with its own dependence on the people holding the position.

## 10. What the RSSG banker saw that the quant missed

The quant who met Hovnanian as a hazard-rate problem asked how likely a builder with $278.2mm of cash was to default, and the honest answer was almost never. The banker asked a different question: why does this indenture say that? The seat trains three habits that turn up the answer.

The first is reading the exhibits as a design brief. A subsidiary that buys $26.0mm of its parent's 8% notes for $26.52mm, does not guarantee the new bonds, and may not be paid interest before maturity [S5] is not a transaction a restructuring adviser would build for the company's benefit. It exists to create one defaulted instrument small enough to dodge cross-default, and the banker knows the thresholds, the springing liens (iHeart's stated reason was a $500mm lien trigger [S26]) and the secured-notes covenant that stopped the company repaying the 8s with cash [S2]. The covenant stack is the design constraint. The quant sees a $1.04mm line item.

The second is knowing who plays which part. A sponsor that appears in an 8-K as a lender normally accepts a below-market return only when something else pays it. A nine-year 5% loan discounted at an assumed 11.5% is worth about 65, a subsidy of some 35 points (illustrative), and the court said it plainly: the CDS profit "was factored into the below-market pricing" [S2]. The banker asks who is paying for the discount; the quant sees an input to a spread model.

The third is following the process rather than the price. At Windstream the quant's input was two 15 to 0 refusals. The banker reading the docket knows what tolling a cure period does, what a trustee's declaratory-judgment motion is for, and that a Committee reads public text while a federal judge decides what the text means. He expects the Committee's answer to be re-read when the court rules, and a filing within days of an acceleration, because accelerated notes cross-accelerate the rest; it came ten days later [S15]. At Sears he knows an intercompany note in bankruptcy is a claim that can be set off, recharacterised, or traded only with the estate's blessing, that the estate itself held deliverables [S27], and that the credit agreement's "Permitted Holder" meant ESL Investments and its affiliates, the same group as the agent [S12]. At Altice he has signed support agreements: once 90% of a tranche is in, the bonds stop being freely transferable, so "can this swap physically settle?" answers itself months before the Committee wrote "Lock-up Settlement Block" [S29].

The quant's counterweights are real. The banker's prior, that nobody defaults on purpose, is exactly what the Hovnanian designers exploited and what the Committee's rules cannot see: it reads public text and does not ask why a payment was withheld [S22, S26]. The banker also finds motive where the contract sees none, and Codere is the control case, an ordinary-looking missed coupon that no primary source supports calling manufactured [S16]. Only the quant is likely to have counted the dealers, the open interest and the one-sided cap. The two views fit together as a pair of questions: is this default a statement about the borrower or about a position, and how many bonds will actually be there to deliver?
