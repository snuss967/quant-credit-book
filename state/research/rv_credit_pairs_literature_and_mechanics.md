## 3. Annotated bibliography

**R1. Duarte, Longstaff, Yu (2007), "Risk and Return in Fixed-Income Arbitrage: Nickels in Front of a Steamroller?", Review of Financial Studies 20(3), 769-811 (advance access 6 July 2006; doi 10.1093/rfs/hhl026). [FT]** Link: https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/Duarte_Longstaff_Yu_nickelsRFS.pdf (author-hosted full text, read in full this session via the squarespace mirror it redirects to). Key result: five strategies (swap spread, yield curve, mortgage, volatility, capital structure arbitrage) built as rule-based return indexes, each scaled to 10% annualized volatility so leverage is held fixed. All five earn positive average excess returns; most are positively skewed. After controlling for equity and bond factors, swap-spread and volatility alphas are insignificant, while yield-curve, mortgage and capital-structure alphas are significant, and the authors tie that to "intellectual capital": the strategies that require complex models and hedge ratios keep the alpha. Fees of 2/20 erode but do not erase the alpha of the equal-weighted "intellectual capital" portfolio. The credit-relevant piece is Section 5 (capital structure arbitrage; see R2 for Yu's own paper): CreditGrades model CDS spread versus Markit 5-year CDS for 261 North American industrial obligors, 135,759 daily spreads, Jan 2001 to Dec 2004 (48 monthly returns); if the market spread exceeds the model spread by a trigger, short CDS ($100 notional) and short stock by the model delta; unwind on convergence or after 180 days; a 5% CDS bid-ask assumed. Table 5: monthly excess returns of 0.61% to 0.77% on six sub-strategies at a 10% volatility target; the equal-weighted portfolio has mean 0.705% a month (t = 1.70), Sharpe ratio 1.203 (the sub-strategy Sharpe ratios are 0.74 to 0.92). The capital required per $100 CDS notional for 10% vol is $44.9 to $90.5 (equal-weighted $66.25), versus $3.7 to $15.8 for swap-spread arbitrage: that is the "risk" in the strategy, because "convergence occurs for only a small fraction of the individual trades". Alpha t-statistic of the equal-weighted CS portfolio 2.11; net of 2/20 fees about 0.68% a month, significant at 10%. R-squared of CS strategies on bond and equity factors is 15 to 35% with positive loadings on the industrial-bond factor and negative on SMB, read by the authors as a business-cycle (default-risk) component. At small triggers (0.5) the mean return becomes zero or negative: small model-vs-market gaps carry little information. The paper's GM vignette is a pair-trade horror story: the 2002 convergence trade and the May 2005 trade where hedge funds shorted GM CDS and shorted GM equity, then Kerkorian's $31 offer sent the stock up while the debt was downgraded, "a painful one-two punch" (quoting the WSJ via Zuckerman 2005). Desk use: the template for a rules-based credit RV backtest (fixed vol target, capital-per-notional as the honest leverage figure, alpha after factors and after fees); the GM episode is the canonical hedge-leg failure for Ch 6, Ch 8-B and Ch 23. Caveat: 48 months only; a CDS sample of 2001-2004 is pre-Big-Bang, pre-standard-coupon; fees assumption is theirs.

**R2. Yu, Fan (2006), "How Profitable Is Capital Structure Arbitrage?", Financial Analysts Journal 62(5), 47-62 (September 2006; DOI 10.2469/faj.v62.n5.4282; page range confirmed in the Crossref record). [ABS]** Link: https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2006/how-profitable-is-capital-structure-arbitrage (CFA Institute record; full text is member-only and was not read; I verified title, author, volume and issue, DOI, date and abstract, but not the page range). Key result per the abstract: capital structure arbitrage exploits mispricing between a company's CDS spread and its equity price; the study uses 135,759 daily CDS spreads on 261 North American obligors and the CreditGrades model as the benchmark; "substantial losses can occur" on individual trades because CDS spreads and equity prices are weakly correlated, but an equally weighted portfolio of all trades earns Sharpe ratios similar to other fixed-income arbitrage strategies and to hedge fund benchmarks. The sample is the same 261-obligor, 135,759-spread panel described in section 5 of R1 (Yu is the third author of R1); the R1 full text is therefore the full-text evidence for how the strategy is built. An earlier FDIC Center for Financial Research working version (April 2005, smaller sample) is at https://www.fdic.gov/analysis/cfr/2005/apr/fyu.pdf (surfaced in search, not opened). Desk use: the cap-structure pair and the proof that the hedge ratio, not the signal, is the weak link; for Ch 6 and Ch 23.

**R3. Asquith, Au, Covert, Pathak (2013), "The market for borrowing corporate bonds", Journal of Financial Economics 107(1), 155-182 (Jan 2013; DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2012.08.007); NBER Working Paper 16282 (Aug 2010). [ABS]** Links: https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/73466 (MIT repository record) and https://nber.org/papers/w16282 (NBER). Both abstracts read this session. Key results: from a comprehensive dataset from "a major lender" (the NBER acknowledgments carry a State Street disclaimer; the abstract does not name the lender or the sample window), borrowing costs for corporate bonds are comparable to those for stock, roughly 10 to 20 basis points, and both have fallen over time; cost depends on loan size, percentage of inventory lent, rating, and the identity of the borrower; "no evidence that bond short sellers have private information" (the working paper adds that strategies based on borrowing cost or amount do not yield excess returns; the published abstract omits this); bonds with CDS contracts are lent more actively; the 2007 credit crunch did not move average cost or loan volume but raised the variance of borrowing cost. Desk use: the empirical anchor for "shorting a bond is not that expensive on average, but averages hide the specials"; the 10-20 bp mean is a fee on a lent bond, not the cost of finding one; note the lender is a single agent lender with institutional inventory (so it describes borrow availability from long-only lenders, a different market from a prime-broker locate for a hedge fund). Do not assert a 2020s fee level from this paper.

**R4. Ben Dor, Dynkin, Hyman, Houweling, van Leeuwen, Penninga (2007), "DTS (Duration Times Spread)", Journal of Portfolio Management 33(2), 77-100 (Winter 2007). [SEC + local text]** Citation verified from Robeco's explainer (https://www.robeco.com/en-sg/insights/2024/08/duration-times-spread-a-measure-of-spread-exposure-in-credit-portfolios, fetched this session); the JPM/CFA Digest PDFs came back as unreadable binary and the original paper was not read. Robeco's account: spread volatility rises roughly in proportion to the spread level, so credit spreads move in relative rather than parallel terms and excess-return volatility is roughly proportional to duration times spread; its worked example is bond A (spread duration 1, spread 500 bp) and bond B (spread duration 10, spread 50 bp), both with DTS = 500; if A's spread rises 10% (to 550) B's rises 10% (to 55 bp), not to 100. Robeco says the measure was originally developed by its researchers in 2003, a joint Lehman-Robeco project led to the 2007 paper, and the measure has been implemented in MSCI RiskMetrics and Bloomberg POINT (Barclays) risk systems (company claim). The local shelf book Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management carries the full treatment; chapter 8 section 8.4 of this book already derives the DTS-neutral pair. Desk use: the hedge ratio for a pair is the DTS ratio, so a long 10-year 60 bp bond is hedged with a short 2-year 300 bp bond at the same DTS, not the same spread duration; DTS ignores default risk and rates, so JTD and curve risk have to be handled separately.

**R5. Schaefer, Strebulaev (2008), "Structural models of credit risk are useful: Evidence from hedge ratios on corporate bonds", Journal of Financial Economics 90(1), 1-19 (DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2007.10.006). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v90y2008i1p1-19.html (abstract read this session; full text not). Key result per the abstract: structural models "provide poor predictions of bond prices" but "quite accurate predictions of the sensitivity of corporate bond returns to changes in the value of equity"; even the Merton model, with or without stochastic rates, "does not capture the interest rate sensitivity of corporate debt", which is substantially lower than conventional duration measures imply; bond prices also load on market-wide factors such as the Fama-French SMB factor in ways the model does not predict. Reading for a desk: the equity leg's hedge ratio (the model delta) is the part of a structural model that works; the rate hedge from analytical duration is the part that over-hedges, which is exactly the empirical-duration story of Ch 8; and the leftover (SMB-like) loading is a non-credit factor that becomes fake "idio" if the factor model omits it. A 2012 paper reported by a search summary (Del Viva, EFMA, "Corporate bond hedging"; not opened) is more skeptical of the equity hedge ratios at the individual-bond level; treat as an open lead (see Section 8).

**R6. Kapadia, Pu (2012), "Limited arbitrage between equity and credit markets", Journal of Financial Economics 105(3), 542-564 (DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2011.10.014). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v105y2012i3p542-564.html (abstract read). Key result: short-horizon pricing gaps between a firm's equity and credit markets are common, and an economically significant share of them are anomalous; a statistical integration measure moves with firm-specific impediments to arbitrage, chiefly liquidity in both markets and idiosyncratic risk; the paper offers an explanation for why Merton-model hedge ratios fit observed stock-bond sensitivities while the same model fails to explain how integrated the two markets are. Desk use: the empirical case that a cap-structure pair (equity vs CDS or bond) converges less reliably in exactly the names where the idio is large; companion to R1/R2; Ch 6, Ch 23.

**R7. Nashikkar, Pedersen (2007), "Corporate Bond Specialness", NBER/AQR working paper, 1 February 2007. [ABS]** Link: https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Working-Paper/Corporate-Bond-Specialness (AQR's page, read; I could not retrieve the PDF, and I did not find a journal publication). Key results per the page: using a dataset of corporate bond loans arranged by one of the world's largest custodians (not named), about 3% of the bonds in the sample are on loan, which the authors say makes bond lending and shorting nearly as common as equity shorting; lending fees are highest for risky, low-rated bonds, especially around downgrades, and higher for small issues, older bonds, and bonds where the custodian holds a small share (harder to locate); bonds with high shorting costs are overpriced relative to the issuer's CDS (a more positive bond-CDS basis); equity specialness is also linked to bond overvaluation relative to CDS, even after controlling for bond specialness. A search-engine summary of the author's CV adds that about 11% of loaned bonds carry shorting costs above 50 bp; I did not verify that figure at the source, so it is excluded from the evidence table. Desk use: the clearest statement that the same names you most want to short (junk, downgrade-prone, rich to CDS) are where borrow is costly, so the pair's carry is worst where the idio signal is best.

**R8. Bai, Collin-Dufresne (2019), "The CDS-bond basis", Financial Management 48(2), 417-439 (DOI 10.1111/fima.12252); earlier SSRN versions on the 2007-2009 basis (2011, 2012). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/finmgt/v48y2019i2p417-439.html (abstract read). Key results: the cross-sectional deviation of the CDS-bond basis from zero is larger for bonds with higher frictions (trading illiquidity, funding cost, counterparty risk, collateral quality), consistent with limits-to-arbitrage; and, "surprisingly", the basis becomes more negative when the bond-lending fee is higher, which the authors read as arbitrageurs avoiding negative-basis trades when short interest in the bond is high. Desk use: the basis is a funding-and-borrow variable as much as a credit variable; pairs that use a bond as one leg inherit it; Ch 24.

**R9. Garleanu, Pedersen (2011), "Margin-Based Asset Pricing and Deviations from the Law of One Price", Review of Financial Studies 24(6), 1980-2022 (DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhr027); NBER WP 16777 (Feb 2011). [ABS]** Link: https://www.nber.org/papers/w16777. Key result: with margin-constrained agents, required returns depend on both beta and margin, so identical cash flows with different margins trade at different prices ("bases"); the time series of bases depends on the shadow cost of capital (measured by the spread between collateralized and uncollateralized loan rates); the authors test the model on CDS-bond bases and other law-of-one-price violations. Desk use: the theory of why a negative basis is wide exactly when funding is scarce; supplies the "margin is the real risk" framing for Ch 8-B and Ch 24.

**R10. Mitchell, Pulvino (2012), "Arbitrage crashes and the speed of capital", Journal of Financial Economics 104(3), 469-490 (DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2011.09.002). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v104y2012i3p469-490.html (abstract read). Key result: the imminent failure of prime brokers in 2008 cut hedge-fund leverage suddenly; the ultimate financiers (rehypothecation lenders) faced an asymmetric payoff, so "seemingly long-term debt capital became short-term capital", and arbitrageurs became unable to keep prices of similar assets similar; the paper also estimates the discounts at which assets transact during crises. Desk use: the mechanism behind pod-level de-grossing of long/short credit books; Ch 8-B, Ch 28 (crowding and capacity).

**R11. Bank for International Settlements (2005), "Stress testing of credit markets: the downgrade of General Motors and Ford", box in the BIS Quarterly Review, June 2005, p. 6. [FT of the box]** Link: https://www.bis.org/publications/qr-200506.pdf (fetched; text extracted locally with pdftotext). This is the best primary account of a pod-style RV unwind. Content: Ford and GM together were about 3% of the investment-grade bond market and could eventually be up to 15% of the global high-yield market; cash bond markets "appeared to adjust in an orderly way" (the downgrade had been long anticipated, GM's spreads had been trading nearer to speculative grade since mid-2003, and index providers using the average of three ratings kept Ford in some IG indices), while credit derivatives were hit harder, CDS spreads "gapping" on several days. The BIS notes "it is easier to take positions, especially short positions, in credit derivatives markets than in corporate bond markets", so leveraged investors are more active there, and that "some 'relative value arbitrage' trades ... suffered large mark to market losses". Two trades are described: (1) GM convertibles versus a replicating portfolio: investors "lost money on both legs": on 4 May GM stock rose 18% on Kerkorian's offer and on 5 May bond and CDS spreads widened after S&P's downgrade; (2) equity-tranche versus index (long correlation): in early 2005, selling protection on the iTraxx Europe equity tranche and buying index protection for the market-wide delta earned 300 to 400 bp; default correlation then fell sharply as investors worried about idiosyncratic risk; unwinds pushed down mezzanine tranche spreads, so that "at times in early May" mezzanine and index moved in opposite directions. "Many leveraged investors had similar positions, and this concentration of activity magnified the deleveraging process", and the circle was "similar in nature, albeit certainly not in magnitude" to 1998; hedge funds were "significantly less leveraged than in 1998". Desk use: a true pod-shop story in which the hedge is right in expectation and wrong in the tail; sits in Ch 8-B and Ch 28 as the crowding case and in Ch 23 for correlation.

**R12. Kato, Nakamura (2023), "Cointegration analysis of hazard rates and CDSs: Applications to pairs trading strategy", Physica A 612, article 128489 (DOI 10.1016/j.physa.2023.128489). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/phsmap/v612y2023ics0378437123000444.html (abstract read). Builds a cointegrated hazard-rate model where hazard-rate drifts follow a vector error correction model, derives an arbitrage-free CDS spread formula, estimates it by Bayesian methods with a numerical ODE solver, and studies a CDS pairs trading strategy on Japanese corporate CDS; the abstract gives no performance numbers, sample period or issuer count. Included because it is one of very few peer-reviewed pairs-trading papers that use CDS spreads directly, and to document that the pairs literature in credit is thin (Section 2).

**R13. Haesen, Houweling, van Zundert (2017), "Momentum spillover from stocks to corporate bonds", Journal of Banking & Finance 79, 28-41 (DOI 10.1016/j.jbankfin.2017.03.003; SSRN 2131032, 2012, earlier titled "Residual Equity Momentum for Corporate Bonds"). [ABS via Robeco summary + Crossref record]** Link: https://robeco.com/en-uk/insights/2012/08/residual-equity-momentum-for-corporate-bonds (Robeco summary of the 2012 working paper, fetched; authors there are Haesen and Houweling). Per Robeco: equity momentum predicts corporate bond returns, but raw momentum carries time-varying factor exposures, and ranking on the firm-specific (residual) component of the stock return lowers factor exposures and "roughly halves volatility", raising the Sharpe ratio; robust to formation and holding periods, estimation windows, factor models, liquidity and rating subsamples. A search-engine summary of the paper gives the long-short design (quintile sort on 36-month residual stock returns against Fama-French factors; bond excess returns over duration-matched Treasuries; Jan 1994 to Sep 2011; annualized volatility 6.16% total versus 3.25% residual; gross excess return 2.16% versus 2.69%); I did not verify those numbers at the source, so they are flagged in the evidence table as secondary. This is the cleanest published case of "idio = residual after factors" beating "total" in credit. Desk use: Ch 10 (residual momentum), Ch 8 (definition of idio).

**R16. Ben Dor, Dynkin, Hyman, Houweling, van Leeuwen, Penninga: "Hedging the Market Risk in Pairs Trades" (book chapter 8) and its 2007-2008 crisis re-run (chapter 10, pp. 241-244), in Ben Dor, Dynkin, Hyman, Phelps, Polbennikov and others, Quantitative Credit Portfolio Management (Wiley), shelf id `Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management`. [FT of the pages, local PDF pp. 228-237 and 270-274]** The chapter-level analysis is the same hedge-ratio experiment published with the DTS paper (R4); the page citations below are PDF-page numbers of the shelf file, printed pages in parentheses. This is the best single public experiment on "hedge ratios disagree". Design (PDF p.229, printed 199): weekly 5-year CDX.NA.IG constituent CDS spreads, Nov 2003 to Dec 2006 (161 weekly samples; statistics reported for April 2004 to Dec 2006); 84 issuers present throughout; 63 issuers in five industries (consumer cyclical, consumer stable, industrials, financials, utilities); 392 same-industry pairs; each pair is long issuer L, short issuer S, each leg sized to "one unit of market risk" (market = equal-weighted industry portfolio); three hedges: matching risky PV01 (spread-duration analogue, effectively equal notionals because all are 5-year CDS), matching DTS, and empirical beta from a trailing window (24 weeks, then 52); rebalanced monthly; P&L measured on weekly spread changes (carry ignored). Results, Table 8.1 (PDF p.231, 24-week beta): average P&L volatility 13.3 bp/week (notional/RPV), 11.2 (DTS), 18.0 (empirical beta); dispersion of that volatility across pairs 5.6, 2.7, 6.9; average absolute market correlation 0.37, 0.23, 0.11 (beta looks best); standard deviation of the hedge ratio over time 0.0, 0.3, 1.3; systematic share of P&L variance 18.6%, 12.6%, 18.9%. The authors' reading (PDF p.232-233): the beta hedge's low market correlation is spurious, because trailing betas that fall near zero blow the hedge up in random directions ("a leveraged short position in the market" or an under-hedge), so correlation averages to zero while the systematic variance share stays high; the analogy is a coin-flip strategy that goes long or short one unit of the market each week and shows zero correlation with the market while taking nothing but market risk. With a 52-week window (Table 8.2, PDF p.234) the beta hedge improves (volatility 15.2 bp/week, systematic share 13.9%, hedge-ratio standard deviation 0.7) while DTS improves further (10.4 bp/week, 8.9%, 0.3). Predicting next-period beta (Table 8.3, PDF p.236): the DTS ratio gives R-squared 0.36 (26-week) and 0.50 (52-week), intercept insignificant and slope 1.09 and 1.05, versus 0.19 and 0.23 for the prior-period empirical beta, whose intercept (0.56, 0.49) is as large as its slope (0.44, 0.51), i.e. betas mean-revert halfway to 1. "Adjusted beta" (average of 1.0 and the prior beta) cuts hedge-ratio standard deviation to 0.2 (PDF p.237). The minimum-variance hedge (half the DTS short) lowers volatility but leaves a trade P&L beta of about 0.5 to the market: lower variance is not what the pair is for ("the goal is not to minimize all risks"). A sanity target (PDF p.232): if each leg has systematic volatility of about 7 bp/week and similar idiosyncratic volatility, a perfect hedge leaves roughly sqrt(2) times a single leg's idiosyncratic volatility, about 10 bp/week; the DTS and notional hedges come close, the beta hedge does not. Crisis re-run (chapter 10, PDF pp.272-274, 2007-2008, 26-week betas, same 63 issuers, 392 pairs): average P&L volatility 159.1 (RPV), 117.5 (DTS), 137.3 (beta) bp/week; systematic share of variance 21.6% (RPV), 25.6% (DTS), 41.1% (beta) in the full set, where DTS is not the lowest; excluding financials (272 of the 392 pairs remain) the systematic shares are 19.2% (DTS), 28.6% (beta) and 23.3% (RPV), and average P&L volatility falls to 73.0 (RPV), 57.7 (DTS) and 66.0 (beta) bp/week, i.e. by more than half; the book concludes that the DTS hedge is "far superior" to duration matching and that beta's low correlation with the market comes from unstable ratios, and that all hedges were "less effective during 2007-2008 than in the pre-crisis period". Desk use: this is the pod's "idio share" calculation done on 392 real pairs. Note that this dataset is CDS, not cash bonds, 2003-2008, and "views" were randomly assigned, so it measures hedge quality, not alpha.

**R17. Ben Dor et al., "DTS-neutral steepeners" and issuer matching (same book, chapter 9, PDF pp. 237, 246-258). [FT of p.246, 250]** A DTS-neutral curve trade (long short-duration bonds, short long-duration bonds of the same issuer) removes market spread beta and, by construction, most carry (printed p.228, already used in Ch 8 section 8.4). Matching positions issuer by issuer lowers volatility, VaR and maximum loss, with information ratios from 0.2 (utilities) to 1.8 (banking), but the book states the limit that matters for a pair: "When an issuer is near default, its bonds tend to trade on price rather than spread. To protect against default, any long-short positions should be matched on a market-value basis rather than DTS-hedged." The utility-sector steepener lost 4.73% in July 2002 on defaults and extreme widening at El Paso Energy, PSEG Energy Holdings and Williams Holdings of Delaware; excluding that month lifts the information ratio from 0.2 to 0.8. Desk use: the clean demonstration that a DTS-neutral pair is not jump-to-default neutral, that "the hedge ratio flips from DTS to market value as price replaces spread as the quoting convention" (Ch 8, Ch 5), and that one month can set the Sharpe of a pair book.

**R18. Man Group / Man Institute (2022), "Quantitative Credit: Alpha Opportunities for Early Movers", pp. 10-11 (shelf id `Man_Quantitative_Credit_Alpha_Early_Mover_2022`). [FT of pp. 10-11]** The only industry document on the shelf that speaks to shorting cash bonds: quant credit "isolates the credit risk component of a portfolio of bonds", neutralizing rate risk either through CDS or by matching benchmark duration and key-rate exposures; Man generally uses cash bonds because the CDS universe is narrow (it counts "just over 100 US CDS names that trade with great regularity" and perhaps 200 more sporadically, against "north of 10,000" bonds from "close to 2,000 issuers") and because CDS "has run into trouble with the legal definition of default, such that several hedge funds have manufactured credit events"; "typical leverage for long-short strategies currently active in the market starts at 4-5x and extends to levels comparable to the most leveraged quant equity managers"; on shorting: "There has been some scepticism expressed about the ease with which corporate bonds can be shorted; our experience in executing live trades ... has been positive so far", with prime broker securities-lending desks now providing "daily feeds with firm borrow availability and fees across the entire investable cash bond universe", which was "largely unheard of even a few years ago when all cash bond security lending was essentially manual and ad hoc"; leverage comes from a prime broker that "keeps the bond portfolio on balance sheet and requires a commitment fee and margin posting". Treat as the firm's own statement (a marketing document, undated within 2022), not independent evidence of borrow cost levels. Desk use: Ch 4, Ch 8, Ch 22.

**R19. Dynkin et al., Systematic Investing in Credit, chapter 21, "Equity Short Interest as a Signal for Credit Investing" (shelf id `Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit`, PDF pp. 793-797; Barclays Research data from FIS Astec Analytics). [FT of the introduction and data section]** Equity short interest (shares on loan over shares outstanding) predicts bond underperformance and downgrades; issuers with the highest equity short interest within their rating category are the likeliest downgrade candidates over the next 3 to 12 months; bond short interest is "subsumed by information in ESI" and is not useful in IG because IG bonds "are likely shorted as part of market-making activity"; equity short interest is complementary to relative value measured as excess spread to peers (ESP), with a combined portfolio earning "almost twice the risk-adjusted return" of either alone. Data caveat the authors give: the lending dataset is a lower bound because some lenders and borrowers do not report. The chapter also lists Hendershott-Kozhan-Raman (R20). Desk use: two linked points for the pair book: (a) the short leg's borrow data are themselves a signal, (b) a short leg in an IG name may be crowded with dealer hedges rather than informed shorts.

**R20. Hendershott, Kozhan, Raman (2020), "Short Selling and Price Discovery in Corporate Bonds", Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis 55(1), 77-115 (Feb 2020; DOI 10.1017/S0022109018001539). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v55y2020i1p77-115_3.html (abstract read). Key results: short selling in corporate bonds predicts future bond returns; the effect is concentrated in high-yield bonds ("where private information is more likely") and is stronger after Lehman's 2008 collapse; short selling rises after past buying-order imbalances (shorts also trade against price pressure); it predicts returns in the shorted bond and in other bonds of the same issuer; past stock returns and stock short selling predict bond returns but do not subsume bond short selling; bond short selling does not predict the issuer's stock returns, so information flows from stocks to bonds, not the reverse. The abstract on the IDEAS page does not state the sample period or the short-selling data source. Tension with R3: Asquith et al. (2013) find no evidence of private information in bond short sellers in their agent-lender data; Hendershott et al. find predictability in later data concentrated in high yield. Desk use: Ch 9 and Ch 7 (short interest as a feature), with the R3 caveat noted.

**R21. Collin-Dufresne, Goldstein, Martin (2001), "The Determinants of Credit Spread Changes", Journal of Finance 56(6), 2177-2207 (DOI 10.1111/0022-1082.00402). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v56y2001i6p2177-2207.html (abstract read). Key results, per the abstract: variables that theory says should matter explain little of monthly credit spread changes in straight industrial bonds; the regression residuals "are highly cross-correlated" and principal-components analysis shows them to be driven mostly by "a single common factor" that the authors cannot proxy with macro or financial variables; the authors conclude that monthly changes are "principally driven by local supply/demand shocks" unrelated to credit-risk factors or standard liquidity proxies. A secondary citing source puts the unexplained share near 75%, which I did not verify, so the 75% is not used. Reading for the pair book: this is the foundational warning that the "idio" you get by regressing on observable factors is not idiosyncratic; a large share of the residual is a common latent factor that sums across every long/short pair in the same direction, and it is what a crowded unwind looks like from the inside.

**R22. D'Amico, Kurakula, Lee (2020), "Impacts of the Fed Corporate Credit Facilities through the Lenses of ETFs and CDX", Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago Working Paper 2020-14 (17 May 2020). [REC via search]** Surfaced by search (https://chicagofed.org/publications/working-papers/2020/2020-14), not opened. Uses bond-ETF prices and CDX spreads to measure the effect of the 23 March and 9 April 2020 Fed facility announcements; reports large positive jumps in prices of directly eligible ETFs and a fall in perceived credit risk for eligible bonds, especially after 9 April. Listed because ETF-versus-CDX is the headline cross-vehicle pair of Ch 20 and Ch 12; content beyond the abstract-level summary is unread.

**R23. Choi, Shachar (2013), "Did Liquidity Providers Become Liquidity Seekers?", Federal Reserve Bank of New York Staff Report No. 650 (Oct 2013). [FT of abstract and introduction]** Link: https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr650.pdf (fetched, text extracted locally). Result: after Lehman, dealers (including bank prop desks) absorbed client selling and their corporate-bond inventories rose; the large negative CDS-bond basis was driven mainly by hedge funds unwinding pre-crisis basis trades; price drops concentrated in bonds with CDS and high basis-trade activity; "limits to arbitrage, possibly in the form of limited capital" kept the basis from converging. Desk use: the single best public account of a crowded two-leg book unwinding into its own market (Ch 24, Ch 28).

**R24. Oehmke, Zawadowski (2017), "The Anatomy of the CDS Market", Review of Financial Studies 30(1), 80-119. [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v30y2017i1p80-119..html. Hedging motives drive volume in both bond and CDS, "speculative trading concentrates in the CDS"; basis-trade arbitrage compresses the negative basis and lowers price impact in the bond market. Use: the CDS is where the shorts live; it is also where a pod's short view meets the manufactured-event and auction rules of Ch 24.

**R25. Nashikkar, Subrahmanyam, Mahanti (2011), "Liquidity and Arbitrage in the Market for Credit Risk", JFQA 46(3), 627-656. [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v46y2011i03p627-656_00.html. Bonds with higher "latent liquidity" (turnover of the funds that hold them) are expensive relative to CDS; highly illiquid bonds of uncertain firms are also expensive, which the authors attribute to limits to arbitrage because shorting illiquid bonds is costly; CDS does "not fully capture the credit risk of the bond". Use: the cash bond and its CDS are not the same risk, even before borrow.

**R26. Hilscher, Pollet, Wilson (2015), "Are Credit Default Swaps a Sideshow? Evidence That Information Flows from Equity to CDS Markets", JFQA 50(3), 543-567. [ABS via search summary only]** Equity returns predict CDS changes at daily and weekly horizons, not the reverse. Surfaced by search (Cambridge listing https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/abs/are-credit-default-swaps-a-sideshow-evidence-that-information-flows-from-equity-to-cds-markets/F7CCD2FFB20AAEACE1E093D12EEF7A31); not opened. Use: with R20, the equity-leads-credit direction that makes "short the equity, long the bond" a view on timing, not only on level.

**R27. Funding and crowding theory, records only: Brunnermeier, Pedersen (2009), "Market Liquidity and Funding Liquidity", Review of Financial Studies 22(6) (NBER WP 12939 verified via Crossref); Stein (2009), "Presidential Address: Sophisticated Investors and Market Efficiency", Journal of Finance 64(4), 1517-1548 (verified via Crossref); Khandani, Lo (2007/2008), "What Happened to the Quants in August 2007?" (SSRN 1015987; NBER WP 14465, verified via Crossref). [REC]** Abstracts not read. They are the standard citations for margin spirals, for the point that arbitrageurs cannot see each other's crowding, and for the equity-quant analogue of a pod unwind; the credit-specific versions are R10, R11 and R23.

**R28. Hu, Pan, Wang (2021, JFQA 56(1); NBER WP 21502, Aug 2015), "Tri-Party Repo Pricing". [ABS]** Link: https://www.nber.org/papers/w21502. In money-market-fund tri-party repo, haircut dispersion is largest on equity- and corporate-bond-backed repos; most fund families apply a single haircut per asset class regardless of collateral quality or counterparty. Use: the repo haircut on a long bond is set by the lender's policy sheet, not by the bond's DTS; relevant to the funding leg of Section 5.4.

**R29. Industry data: EquiLend Data & Analytics, full-year 2025 securities lending summary (published January 2026). [secondary trade release]** Links: https://mondovisione.com/media-and-resources/news/equilend-securities-lending-revenue-soars-to-all-time-highs-of-153-billion-202616/ (corporate bond lender-to-broker revenue $463 million, +8%; balances +22%; fees -12%) and https://www.hedgeweek.com/global-securities-lending-revenue-hits-record-15-3bn-in-2025/ (global revenue $15.3bn, +26%; balances above $4tn). Balances up and average fees down is the opposite of "shorting bonds is getting harder"; averages hide named credits. The pages say nothing about high yield versus IG or recalls.

**R30. Regulation: SEC Rule 10c-1a and Rule 13f-2.** Reg SHO's locate duty (Rule 203(b)) is written for "any equity security" (https://www.sec.gov/rules/2004/07/short-sales, read to its first 100,000 characters). Rule 10c-1a requires reporting of securities loans to FINRA; the SEC's July 2025 order moved initial reporting to 28 Sep 2026 and public dissemination to 29 Mar 2027 (https://www.dechert.com/knowledge/onpoint/2025/8/sec-extends-compliance-deadlines-for-securities-lending-transpar.html), and the SEC's 3 Dec 2025 order moved Rule 10c-1a reporting to 28 Sep 2028 and Rule 13f-2 to 2 Jan 2028 after the Fifth Circuit (Aug 2025) remanded both rules without vacatur (https://www.proskauer.com/blog/sec-again-extends-short-sale-and-securities-lending-rules-compliance-deadlines-to-2028-while-it-considers-response-to-court-ruling). I could not confirm from a primary page that corporate bonds are in the 10c-1a scope (the rule is defined by reference to reportable securities); do not state it in the book until the rule text is read. Net: as of 18 Oct 2026 there is no public, loan-level bond-borrow tape, so borrow data are vendor-only (EquiLend, S&P Global, FIS Astec in Dynkin chapter 21).
