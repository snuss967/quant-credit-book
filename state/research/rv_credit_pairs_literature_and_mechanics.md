## 3. Annotated bibliography

**R1. Duarte, Longstaff, Yu (2007), "Risk and Return in Fixed-Income Arbitrage: Nickels in Front of a Steamroller?", Review of Financial Studies 20(3), 769-811 (advance access 6 July 2006; doi 10.1093/rfs/hhl026). [FT]** Link: https://www.johnhcochrane.com/s/Duarte_Longstaff_Yu_nickelsRFS.pdf (author-hosted full text, read in full this session via the squarespace mirror it redirects to). Key result: five strategies (swap spread, yield curve, mortgage, volatility, capital structure arbitrage) built as rule-based return indexes, each scaled to 10% annualized volatility so leverage is held fixed. All five earn positive average excess returns; most are positively skewed. After controlling for equity and bond factors, swap-spread and volatility alphas are insignificant, while yield-curve, mortgage and capital-structure alphas are significant, and the authors tie that to "intellectual capital": the strategies that require complex models and hedge ratios keep the alpha. Fees of 2/20 erode but do not erase the alpha of the equal-weighted "intellectual capital" portfolio. The credit-relevant piece is Section 5 (capital structure arbitrage; see R2 for Yu's own paper): CreditGrades model CDS spread versus Markit 5-year CDS for 261 North American industrial obligors, 135,759 daily spreads, Jan 2001 to Dec 2004 (48 monthly returns); if the market spread exceeds the model spread by a trigger, short CDS ($100 notional) and short stock by the model delta; unwind on convergence or after 180 days; a 5% CDS bid-ask assumed. Table 5: monthly excess returns of 0.61% to 0.77% on six sub-strategies at a 10% volatility target; the equal-weighted portfolio has mean 0.705% a month (t = 1.70), Sharpe ratio 1.203 (the sub-strategy Sharpe ratios are 0.74 to 0.92). The capital required per $100 CDS notional for 10% vol is $44.9 to $90.5 (equal-weighted $66.25), versus $3.7 to $15.8 for swap-spread arbitrage: that is the "risk" in the strategy, because "convergence occurs for only a small fraction of the individual trades". Alpha t-statistic of the equal-weighted CS portfolio 2.11; net of 2/20 fees about 0.68% a month, significant at 10%. R-squared of CS strategies on bond and equity factors is 15 to 35% with positive loadings on the industrial-bond factor and negative on SMB, read by the authors as a business-cycle (default-risk) component. At small triggers (0.5) the mean return becomes zero or negative: small model-vs-market gaps carry little information. The paper's GM vignette is a pair-trade horror story: the 2002 convergence trade and the May 2005 trade where hedge funds shorted GM CDS and shorted GM equity, then Kerkorian's $31 offer sent the stock up while the debt was downgraded, "a painful one-two punch" (quoting the WSJ via Zuckerman 2005). Desk use: the template for a rules-based credit RV backtest (fixed vol target, capital-per-notional as the honest leverage figure, alpha after factors and after fees); the GM episode is the canonical hedge-leg failure for Ch 6, Ch 8-B and Ch 23. Caveat: 48 months only; a CDS sample of 2001-2004 is pre-Big-Bang, pre-standard-coupon; fees assumption is theirs.

**R2. Yu, Fan (2006), "How Profitable Is Capital Structure Arbitrage?", Financial Analysts Journal 62(5), 47-62 (Sep/Oct 2006; DOI 10.2469/faj.v62.n5.4282). [ABS]** Link: https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/financial-analysts-journal/2006/how-profitable-is-capital-structure-arbitrage (CFA Institute record; full text is member-only and was not read; I verified title, author, volume and issue, DOI, date and abstract, but not the page range). Key result per the abstract: capital structure arbitrage exploits mispricing between a company's CDS spread and its equity price; the study uses 135,759 daily CDS spreads on 261 North American obligors and the CreditGrades model as the benchmark; "substantial losses can occur" on individual trades because CDS spreads and equity prices are weakly correlated, but an equally weighted portfolio of all trades earns Sharpe ratios similar to other fixed-income arbitrage strategies and to hedge fund benchmarks. The sample is the same 261-obligor, 135,759-spread panel used in R1 section 5, which is the same author; the pages of the DLY paper are the full-text evidence for Yu's design. An earlier FDIC Center for Financial Research working version (April 2005, smaller sample) is at https://www.fdic.gov/analysis/cfr/2005/apr/fyu.pdf (surfaced in search, not opened). Desk use: the cap-structure pair and the proof that the hedge ratio, not the signal, is the weak link; for Ch 6 and Ch 23.

**R3. Asquith, Au, Covert, Pathak (2013), "The market for borrowing corporate bonds", Journal of Financial Economics 107(1), 155-182 (Jan 2013; DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2012.08.007); NBER Working Paper 16282 (Aug 2010). [ABS]** Links: https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/73466 (MIT repository record) and https://nber.org/papers/w16282 (NBER). Both abstracts read this session. Key results: from a comprehensive dataset from "a major lender" (the NBER acknowledgments carry a State Street disclaimer; the abstract does not name the lender or the sample window), borrowing costs for corporate bonds are comparable to those for stock, roughly 10 to 20 basis points, and both have fallen over time; cost depends on loan size, percentage of inventory lent, rating, and the identity of the borrower; "no evidence that bond short sellers have private information" (the working paper adds that strategies based on borrowing cost or amount do not yield excess returns; the published abstract omits this); bonds with CDS contracts are lent more actively; the 2007 credit crunch did not move average cost or loan volume but raised the variance of borrowing cost. Desk use: the empirical anchor for "shorting a bond is not that expensive on average, but averages hide the specials"; the 10-20 bp mean is a fee on a lent bond, not the cost of finding one; note the lender is a single agent lender with institutional inventory (so it describes borrow availability from long-only lenders, a different market from a prime-broker locate for a hedge fund). Do not assert a 2020s fee level from this paper.

**R4. Ben Dor, Dynkin, Hyman, Houweling, van Leeuwen, Penninga (2007), "DTS (Duration Times Spread)", Journal of Portfolio Management 33(2), 77-100 (Winter 2007). [SEC + local text]** Citation verified from Robeco's explainer (https://www.robeco.com/en-sg/insights/2024/08/duration-times-spread-a-measure-of-spread-exposure-in-credit-portfolios, fetched this session); the JPM/CFA Digest PDFs came back as unreadable binary and the original paper was not read. Robeco's account: spread volatility rises roughly in proportion to the spread level, so credit spreads move in relative rather than parallel terms and excess-return volatility is roughly proportional to duration times spread; its worked example is bond A (spread duration 1, spread 500 bp) and bond B (spread duration 10, spread 50 bp), both with DTS = 500; if A's spread rises 10% (to 550) B's rises 10% (to 55 bp), not to 100. Robeco says the measure was originally developed by its researchers in 2003, a joint Lehman-Robeco project led to the 2007 paper, and the measure has been implemented in MSCI RiskMetrics and Bloomberg POINT (Barclays) risk systems (company claim). The local shelf book Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management carries the full treatment; chapter 8 section 8.4 of this book already derives the DTS-neutral pair. Desk use: the hedge ratio for a pair is the DTS ratio, so a long 10-year 60 bp bond is hedged with a short 2-year 300 bp bond at the same DTS, not the same spread duration; DTS ignores default risk and rates, so JTD and curve risk have to be handled separately.

**R5. Schaefer, Strebulaev (2008), "Structural models of credit risk are useful: Evidence from hedge ratios on corporate bonds", Journal of Financial Economics 90(1), 1-19 (DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2007.10.006). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v90y2008i1p1-19.html (abstract read this session; full text not). Key result per the abstract: structural models "provide poor predictions of bond prices" but "quite accurate predictions of the sensitivity of corporate bond returns to changes in the value of equity"; even the Merton model, with or without stochastic rates, "does not capture the interest rate sensitivity of corporate debt", which is substantially lower than conventional duration measures imply; bond prices also load on market-wide factors such as the Fama-French SMB factor in ways the model does not predict. Reading for a desk: the equity leg's hedge ratio (the model delta) is the part of a structural model that works; the rate hedge from analytical duration is the part that over-hedges, which is exactly the empirical-duration story of Ch 8; and the leftover (SMB-like) loading is a non-credit factor that becomes fake "idio" if the factor model omits it. A 2012 paper reported by a search summary (Del Viva, EFMA, "Corporate bond hedging"; not opened) is more skeptical of the equity hedge ratios at the individual-bond level; treat as an open lead (see Section 8).

**R6. Kapadia, Pu (2012), "Limited arbitrage between equity and credit markets", Journal of Financial Economics 105(3), 542-564 (DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2011.10.014). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v105y2012i3p542-564.html (abstract read). Key result: short-horizon pricing gaps between a firm's equity and credit markets are common, and an economically significant share of them are anomalous; a statistical integration measure moves with firm-specific impediments to arbitrage, chiefly liquidity in both markets and idiosyncratic risk; the paper offers an explanation for why Merton-model hedge ratios fit observed stock-bond sensitivities while the same model fails to explain how integrated the two markets are. Desk use: the empirical case that a cap-structure pair (equity vs CDS or bond) converges less reliably in exactly the names where the idio is large; companion to R1/R2; Ch 6, Ch 23.

**R7. Nashikkar, Pedersen (2007), "Corporate Bond Specialness", NBER/AQR working paper, 1 February 2007. [ABS]** Link: https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Working-Paper/Corporate-Bond-Specialness (AQR's page, read; I could not retrieve the PDF, and I did not find a journal publication). Key results per the page: using a dataset of corporate bond loans arranged by one of the world's largest custodians (not named), about 3% of the bonds in the sample are on loan, which the authors say makes bond lending and shorting nearly as common as equity shorting; lending fees are highest for risky, low-rated bonds, especially around downgrades, and higher for small issues, older bonds, and bonds where the custodian holds a small share (harder to locate); bonds with high shorting costs are overpriced relative to the issuer's CDS (a more positive bond-CDS basis); equity specialness is also linked to bond overvaluation relative to CDS, even after controlling for bond specialness. A search-engine summary of the author's CV adds that about 11% of loaned bonds carry shorting costs above 50 bp; I did not verify that figure at the source, so it is excluded from the evidence table. Desk use: the clearest statement that the same names you most want to short (junk, downgrade-prone, rich to CDS) are where borrow is costly, so the pair's carry is worst where the idio signal is best.

**R8. Bai, Collin-Dufresne (2019), "The CDS-bond basis", Financial Management 48(2), 417-439 (DOI 10.1111/fima.12252); earlier SSRN versions on the 2007-2009 basis (2011, 2012). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/finmgt/v48y2019i2p417-439.html (abstract read). Key results: the cross-sectional deviation of the CDS-bond basis from zero is larger for bonds with higher frictions (trading illiquidity, funding cost, counterparty risk, collateral quality), consistent with limits-to-arbitrage; and, "surprisingly", the basis becomes more negative when the bond-lending fee is higher, which the authors read as arbitrageurs avoiding negative-basis trades when short interest in the bond is high. Desk use: the basis is a funding-and-borrow variable as much as a credit variable; pairs that use a bond as one leg inherit it; Ch 24.

**R9. Garleanu, Pedersen (2011), "Margin-Based Asset Pricing and Deviations from the Law of One Price", Review of Financial Studies 24(6), 1980-2022 (DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhr027); NBER WP 16777 (Feb 2011). [ABS]** Link: https://www.nber.org/papers/w16777. Key result: with margin-constrained agents, required returns depend on both beta and margin, so identical cash flows with different margins trade at different prices ("bases"); the time series of bases depends on the shadow cost of capital (measured by the spread between collateralized and uncollateralized loan rates); the authors test the model on CDS-bond bases and other law-of-one-price violations. Desk use: the theory of why a negative basis is wide exactly when funding is scarce; supplies the "margin is the real risk" framing for Ch 8-B and Ch 24.

**R10. Mitchell, Pulvino (2012), "Arbitrage crashes and the speed of capital", Journal of Financial Economics 104(3), 469-490 (DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2011.09.002). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v104y2012i3p469-490.html (abstract read). Key result: the imminent failure of prime brokers in 2008 cut hedge-fund leverage suddenly; the ultimate financiers (rehypothecation lenders) faced an asymmetric payoff, so "seemingly long-term debt capital became short-term capital", and arbitrageurs became unable to keep prices of similar assets similar; the paper also estimates the discounts at which assets transact during crises. Desk use: the mechanism behind pod-level de-grossing of long/short credit books; Ch 8-B, Ch 28 (crowding and capacity).

**R11. Bank for International Settlements (2005), "Stress testing of credit markets: the downgrade of General Motors and Ford", box in the BIS Quarterly Review, June 2005, p. 6. [FT of the box]** Link: https://www.bis.org/publications/qr-200506.pdf (fetched; text extracted locally with pdftotext). This is the best primary account of a pod-style RV unwind. Content: Ford and GM together were about 3% of the investment-grade bond market and could eventually be up to 15% of the global high-yield market; cash bond markets "appeared to adjust in an orderly way" (the downgrade had been long anticipated, GM's spreads had been trading nearer to speculative grade since mid-2003, and index providers using the average of three ratings kept Ford in some IG indices), while credit derivatives were hit harder, CDS spreads "gapping" on several days. The BIS notes "it is easier to take positions, especially short positions, in credit derivatives markets than in corporate bond markets", so leveraged investors are more active there, and that "some 'relative value arbitrage' trades ... suffered large mark to market losses". Two trades are described: (1) GM convertibles versus a replicating portfolio: investors "lost money on both legs": on 4 May GM stock rose 18% on Kerkorian's offer and on 5 May bond and CDS spreads widened after S&P's downgrade; (2) equity-tranche versus index (long correlation): in early 2005, selling protection on the iTraxx Europe equity tranche and buying index protection for the market-wide delta earned 300 to 400 bp; default correlation then fell sharply as investors worried about idiosyncratic risk; unwinds pushed down mezzanine tranche spreads, so that "at times in early May" mezzanine and index moved in opposite directions. "Many leveraged investors had similar positions, and this concentration of activity magnified the deleveraging process", and the circle was "similar in nature, albeit certainly not in magnitude" to 1998; hedge funds were "significantly less leveraged than in 1998". Desk use: a true pod-shop story in which the hedge is right in expectation and wrong in the tail; sits in Ch 8-B and Ch 28 as the crowding case and in Ch 23 for correlation.

**R12. Kato, Nakamura (2023), "Cointegration analysis of hazard rates and CDSs: Applications to pairs trading strategy", Physica A 612, article 128489 (DOI 10.1016/j.physa.2023.128489). [ABS]** Link: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/phsmap/v612y2023ics0378437123000444.html (abstract read). Builds a cointegrated hazard-rate model where hazard-rate drifts follow a vector error correction model, derives an arbitrage-free CDS spread formula, estimates it by Bayesian methods with a numerical ODE solver, and studies a CDS pairs trading strategy on Japanese corporate CDS; the abstract gives no performance numbers, sample period or issuer count. Included because it is one of very few peer-reviewed pairs-trading papers that use CDS spreads directly, and to document that the pairs literature in credit is thin (Section 2).

**R13. Haesen, Houweling, van Zundert (2017), "Momentum spillover from stocks to corporate bonds", Journal of Banking & Finance 79, 28-41 (DOI 10.1016/j.jbankfin.2017.03.003; SSRN 2131032, 2012, earlier titled "Residual Equity Momentum for Corporate Bonds"). [ABS via Robeco summary + Crossref record]** Link: https://robeco.com/en-uk/insights/2012/08/residual-equity-momentum-for-corporate-bonds (Robeco summary of the 2012 working paper, fetched; authors there are Haesen and Houweling). Per Robeco: equity momentum predicts corporate bond returns, but raw momentum carries time-varying factor exposures, and ranking on the firm-specific (residual) component of the stock return lowers factor exposures and "roughly halves volatility", raising the Sharpe ratio; robust to formation and holding periods, estimation windows, factor models, liquidity and rating subsamples. A search-engine summary of the paper gives the long-short design (quintile sort on 36-month residual stock returns against Fama-French factors; bond excess returns over duration-matched Treasuries; Jan 1994 to Sep 2011; annualized volatility 6.16% total versus 3.25% residual; gross excess return 2.16% versus 2.69%); I did not verify those numbers at the source, so they are flagged in the evidence table as secondary. This is the cleanest published case of "idio = residual after factors" beating "total" in credit. Desk use: Ch 10 (residual momentum), Ch 8 (definition of idio).

**R14. Gebhardt, Hvidkjaer, Swaminathan (2005), "Stock and bond market interaction: Does momentum spill over?", Journal of Financial Economics 75(3), 651-690 (DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2004.03.005). [REC]** Bibliographic record verified via Crossref; abstract not read this session. The book's own Ch 8 (section 8.5) already cites this paper for stock-to-bond momentum spillover, with Dynkin p.606 numbers for the equity-momentum decile spread in bonds (bottom decile about -4.7% a year versus about +4.0% for the top, 1993-2017); I rely on that existing treatment and do not repeat the numbers as new findings.

**R15. Ben Dor, Dynkin, Hyman, Polbennikov (2026), "Twenty Years of Duration Times Spread (DTS)", Journal of Fixed Income 36(2), 89-115 (online 5 Aug 2026; DOI 10.3905/jfi.2026.018). [REC]** Bibliographic record verified via Crossref (title, authors, volume, pages, date); abstract not available through Crossref and the publisher page was not read, so the content is unknown to me. Worth one line in Ch 8 section 8.4 as a 2026 anniversary reassessment by the original team (the first author list differs from the 2007 paper by the addition of Polbennikov and the absence of the Robeco authors); to be read before it is quoted.
