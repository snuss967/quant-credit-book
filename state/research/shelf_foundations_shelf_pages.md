## 1. Moyer: classes, voting, cramdown, plan process (Ch 12-B)

Source: `Moyer_Distressed_Debt_Analysis` (Stephen G. Moyer, *Distressed Debt Analysis: Strategies for Speculative Investors*, J. Ross Publishing, 2005; 453 PDF pages, no embedded TOC). PDF page = printed page + 17 (p.357 PDF = printed 340). Every citation in this dossier uses the PDF page. The worked example throughout is "Boxco": an operating company with a secured bank claim A and trade claim B, under a holding company with unguaranteed senior notes C, plus equity (Figures 3-6, 12-1 to 12-3).

### 1.1 The process as Moyer tells it (p.94-108, p.327-362)

**Phases and timing (p.94, p.97).** A chapter 11 has "four main objectives": stabilise operations and liquidity; develop a going-forward business plan; determine the "legitimate" liabilities and their priority; and "create and allocate a new capital structure" (p.97). The filing types are a ladder of preparation: *free-fall* (no consensus, "a warning sign that the reorganization process may be unusually lengthy (e.g., 1-3 years) and expensive", p.94); *prenegotiated* (plan agreed at filing, vote after); *prepackaged* (vote solicited before filing under BRC section 1126(b), confirmation "in less than 45 days", p.94, p.335-336). Moyer's reality check on prepacks: "Full prepacks are not common"; they were "less than 7% of cases" in 1998-2002 (p.336, Table 12-1), because a prepack needs very few voting classes, so the consenting bond class must tolerate pari passu trade creditors being left whole (p.336).

**Automatic stay, DIP, priming, roll-up (p.97-99).** The stay "freezes all creditors in their prepetition position" (p.97). Postpetition borrowings are administrative claims; the code lets the court grant the DIP lender "a super-priority interest in previously encumbered assets", "sometimes called priming", conditional on adequate protection; prepetition lenders offer to become DIP lenders to avoid being primed and "effectively convert their prepetition claim into a postpetition claim in a process commonly referred to as a rollup" (p.98). His priming arithmetic is a ready test case: a lender owed $65 with $150 of receivables as collateral can be adequately protected after a $75 DIP is given super-priority over $75 of the collateral (p.98). Critical-vendor motions, KERPs and the CRO appear at p.99.

**Exclusivity (p.94, p.346-347).** "Under BRC section 1121(b), the debtor has the exclusive right to propose a plan of reorganization for 120 days following the filing of the petition and then an additional 60 days to solicit acceptances" and courts extend it "virtually indefinitely" (p.94, p.346). Moyer's view is that this is the management-versus-creditor axis: exclusivity "tends to work against creditor interests" (p.346). **Dated:** BAPCPA (effective 17 Oct 2005) capped extensions at 18 months (file) and 20 months (solicit); see section 9.

**Assets and liabilities (p.101, p.304).** Voidable preferences: payments on antecedent debt in the 90 days before filing, one year for insiders (p.101, p.304). Fraudulent conveyance: no fair value received, "frequently ... considered in leveraged buyout situations that fail" (p.101). Substantive consolidation "effectively eliminates the notion of structural priority" (p.304) and so converts a structurally-senior operating-company trade claim and a holdco bond into pari passu claims (Figure 12-3, p.360); Moyer flags it as a tradeable hedge: buy the lower-priced claim, short the higher-priced one (p.305).

### 1.2 Classes, impairment and voting (p.106-107, p.334, p.356-358)

- **Classification.** The plan "can propose the segregation of claims and interests into various classes" (p.356). All claims in a class get identical treatment, so "the main purpose of creating separate classes of claims with similar priority is to give them different treatment" (p.360). Secured claims "are required to be segregated into classes in accordance with the underlying collateral" (p.358). Gerrymandering is possible and "bankruptcy courts are mindful of this risk" (p.107).
- **Impairment.** "Effectively, a claim must be either paid off in full or reinstated under identical terms to be unimpaired. Any other treatment (even improvements) likely will be judged as an impairment" (p.356-357). Unimpaired classes are deemed to accept and are not solicited; classes receiving nothing are deemed to reject (p.106). So the voting set is the classes that "are receiving some type of recovery but are impaired" (p.106).
- **Voting test.** A class accepts if more than 50% in number and at least 66 2/3% in amount of *those voting* approve (p.107; p.357 states it as "66.7% of those voting in amount and more than 50.0% in number"). "These thresholds are based on those participating in the voting process, not the full class size" (p.107). Accepting class binds dissenters and non-voters (p.334).
- **Blocking position.** The two-prong test "was designed to prevent one large holder from imposing its will on a class where the majority (in number) objects" (p.358). The consequence: 33.4% of a class's claims blocks (more precisely, one-third plus of the *voting* amount), and a plan proponent's counter is to merge classes to dilute the blocker below one-third. Moyer's Block Partners example (45% of claim C, bonds merged with trade claims, p.358) is a ready worked example; the limit he notes is that secured claims cannot be merged across collateral.
- **Tipping.** The consensus-building mechanism: "horse trading" such as warrants struck at an equity-friendly value of 60 given to the equity (p.357). "This process, which in particular gives value to claims that would appear to be out-of-the-money, is called tipping" (p.357). Arithmetic: a $250 senior class giving up 2 points hands a $50 preferred class a 10-point improvement (p.361). Investor lessons (p.361): count votes accurately because tip-laden plans need *all* impaired classes; senior holders should "budget in potential 'tip giving'"; out-of-the-money juniors are "a bet on how big a tip can be extracted".

### 1.3 Confirmation and cramdown (p.107-108, p.359-362)

- **Section 1129 as a "bill of rights".** Confirmation is "limited democracy" (p.359): the court checks procedural items (fees, post-confirmation officers disclosed, regulatory approvals, US Trustee fees, retiree benefits; p.107) plus four substantive tests: *best interests* (each dissenting creditor gets at least chapter 7 liquidation value; "generally not much protection for unsecured creditors" because the liquidation analysis is a "fire-sale", p.107-108), *good faith* (undefined, hard to use, p.108), *feasibility* (no subsequent liquidation or reorganisation; "the most contentious part of the confirmation hearing because any single creditor can raise a challenge", p.108) and *consent or cram-down*.
- **Cram-down gate (p.359).** Three requirements: (1) at least one impaired accepting class excluding insiders; (2) no unfair discrimination; (3) fair and equitable. Unfair discrimination is the pari passu fairness test between *different* classes of the same priority (p.360: trade claims B offered 70% in cash while notes C get equity, B demanding 75%). Fair and equitable for unsecured classes is "strict enforcement of the absolute priority rule" (p.360): no junior recovery unless the objecting class is paid in full. The practical effect (p.108): any value, "regardless of how minimal", to equity means the debtor must obtain consent of, or fully pay, all impaired creditors.
- **Secured cramdown (p.362).** Absent a section 1111(b) election, the lien attaches to sale proceeds, or the creditor retains its lien to the extent of the allowed claim and gets deferred payments with present value equal to the lesser of collateral value or allowed claim. With the 1111(b) election the creditor waives its deficiency claim in exchange for a lien for the full face amount and payments whose gross equals face but whose present value equals the lesser of collateral and claim. Moyer's essence: "the secured claim must, at a minimum, receive a new secured piece of paper with a present value equal to the secured portion of the allowed claim", and a plan that partially equitises a secured claim "likely will need to be acceptable to the secured class" (p.362). Administrative claims must be paid in cash or consent at confirmation; hence rights offerings to fund exit (p.362).
- **Valuation contest (p.356).** If the committee thinks the disclosure-statement valuation is wrong, "the bankruptcy court ... will hear the conflicting testimony of the debtor's and committee's ... financial advisors", and "often ... the parties will come to a compromise" (p.356). The incentives are symmetrical and are why plan value is a random variable: seniors want a low valuation (they keep a "windfall" if the sale price exceeds it, p.106), juniors a high one.

### 1.4 Priority, subordination and the recovery waterfall (p.68, p.158-161)

- **Four priority techniques (p.158-159):** grants of collateral, term structure (maturity), contractual provisions, and corporate structure. A security agreement is a separate document referencing the main lending agreement, and "perfection" failures give unsecured lenders leverage (p.159).
- **Contractual subordination is purely contractual.** "Legal subordination can only occur through an express provision in the bond being subordinated", and the book's "Super-Duper Senior Notes" example shows a note cannot assert seniority over others (p.159-160). Scope matters: "Senior Debt" is a defined term and normally narrower than "all other obligations", with subordination usually limited to debt rather than trade claims (p.160).
- **Partial subordination and subrogation (Dead Co., Figure 7-2 and Table 7-1, p.160-161).** Assets $75; bank debt $50, trade claims $50, sub notes $100. Pro rata recovery is 37.5% (75/200), i.e. bank 18.75, trade 18.75, sub 37.50. Because the sub notes are subordinated only to the bank, the sub's $31.25 is turned over to the bank. Adjusted recoveries: bank 50.00 (100%), trade 18.75 (37.5%), sub 6.25 (6.3%). Sub subordinated to both bank and trade would have given both a 75% recovery (75/100). Table 7-1 is the minimum regression test for any waterfall engine; it is the "subordination as a transfer, not a rank" idea that App E law 12-style reasoning needs.
- **The pricing paradox (p.68).** Boxco senior debt at 50, sub at 20, equity positive: "the price of one security appears to imply that the price of another security is wrong". Implied asset value $290 from market prices (p.68). Useful for the cramdown chapter as the market-implied waterfall that no cut-off ever satisfies.

### 1.5 Out of court as a prisoner's dilemma (p.85-87)

The holdout arithmetic is a complete tiny model. Boxco's $700 face of bonds exchanges into 14 cents of new debt and 10 shares per dollar; full participation implies 41% (287/700); participants' securities are worth 36.5 per 100 whereas a holdout of 50 bonds is "materially better off" at 100; if too many hold out, chapter 11 costs cut value from 287 to 215 and recovery from 41.0% to 30.4% (p.85). Solutions: repeated-game "moral" coercion among a small community of regulars (the "bluff" must be credible, p.86); and "coercive structural devices", the most common being to "strip the covenant protections of nontendering bonds" (exit consents) and to couple the exchange with a prepack solicitation (p.86). Moyer p.334 draws the contrast the book needs: "a class minority can effectively defeat the wishes of the majority to effect an out-of-court restructuring. In bankruptcy, minority rights are significantly reduced." That sentence is the economic reason the whole LMT era exists (Ch 13): the out-of-court unanimity right (sacred rights) is the friction that uptiers and drop-downs route around.

### 1.6 Claims and trade-claim purchase (p.310-312)

Trade claims "are general unsecured claims and thus are equal in status with unsecured debt" and trade at a discount to the bonds (p.310); the risk is that the purchased claim will not be "an allowed claim" (p.311) and that the transfer is not valid (Rule 3001(e), p.312). There is no 503(b)(9) discussion in the PDF (the 20-day goods administrative priority came with BAPCPA, after the book); Ch 12-B must source it elsewhere.

## 2. Bohn-Stein and Schmid: seniority/lien subordination and the P-to-Q change of measure (Ch 6-B, 12-B)

Sources: `Bohn_Stein_Active_Credit_Portfolio_Management` (Jeff Bohn and Roger Stein, *Active Credit Portfolio Management in Practice*, Wiley, 2009; 643 PDF pages; printed page = PDF page - 32); `Schmid_Credit_Risk_Pricing_Models` (Bernd Schmid, *Credit Risk Pricing Models: Theory and Practice*, 2nd ed., Springer, 2004; 387 PDF pages, no TOC; printed page about PDF - 6 to - 8). Tip for the extractor: the text layer drops Greek letters, so pull equations with `pdftotext -f N -l N -layout source_pdfs/<file>.pdf -` (the Bohn-Stein h^Q multiplier is psi, the market price of risk is lambda).

### 2.1 Structural P-to-Q: pd^Q = Phi(Phi^-1(pd) + R*lambda*sqrt(T)) (Bohn-Stein p.131, p.66, p.209-211)

**The definitions (p.131).** Bohn-Stein separate four objects the book must keep apart: physical PD (a transformation of DD, used "in risk assessment, some valuation applications, and the estimation of loss distributions used in portfolio and capital analyses"), risk-neutral PD^Q, cumulative versions (cpd_t = 1 - (1 - pd)^t for a constant term structure), and LGD. "If we estimate a physical PD from equity prices, we need to adjust it with an estimate of the market price of risk" (p.131), and the adjustment is used "only in the context of valuation" because it lets one "discount probability-adjusted cash flows at the default risk-free rate" (p.131). Backing physical PDs out of CDS-implied PD^Q "proceeds in the opposite direction" (p.131). On p.66 they describe the same step in words: investors "require an extra premium beyond payment for expected loss to compensate them for the risk associated with purchasing a security that may lose value in excess of everyone's expectations".

**The derivation (Appendix 3B, p.209-211).** Under BSM, pd = P(A_T <= X) = N(-DD) with DD = [ln A_0 + (mu_A - sigma_A^2/2)T - ln X]/(sigma_A sqrt(T)); the risk-neutral version replaces mu_A with r. Assume CAPM, mu_A - r = beta (mu_M - r); let R be the correlation of the firm's assets with the market for firm assets and lambda = (mu_M - r)/sigma_M the market price of risk. Then (mu_A - r)/sigma_A = R lambda, so r = mu_A - sigma_A R lambda and

DD^Q = DD - R lambda sqrt(T), and therefore pd^Q = Phi(Phi^-1(pd) + R lambda sqrt(T)) (p.210-211).

This is the Wang-transform form (a shift of the normal quantile by a Sharpe-like number scaled by sqrt(T)); the shelf gives it as a four-line corollary of Merton, which is exactly the "single P-to-Q bridge" Ch 6-B needs. Three reading notes the page itself supports: (i) "the risk-neutral DD is smaller than the physical DD" (p.210) so pd^Q > pd always for R, lambda > 0; (ii) the shift grows with sqrt(T), so the premium ratio pd^Q/pd explodes at low pd and short horizon in relative terms but is largest in absolute terms at long horizons; (iii) R is a *correlation* in the derivation although the page prints "R^2" in the definition, a typo to avoid copying. Related Chapter 3 landmarks: Black-Cox first passage p.150-156, Vasicek-Kealhofer p.156, empirical DD-to-PD mapping p.161-177, and DD for endogenous barriers p.183.

### 2.2 Reduced-form P-to-Q: the hazard multiplier (Bohn-Stein p.342-344)

The "brief interlude to discuss valuation" (p.342-344) is the shelf's only explicit treatment of the intensity change of measure. Pricing relationship: D(T) = E^Q_0[exp(-integral (r(t) + h^Q(t) L) dt)], with spread s(t) = h^Q(t) L (p.342-343; the "no recovery" version has exponent r + h^Q). Since a reduced-form model has no CAPM-style link, the authors say bluntly: "In the reduced-form framework, we do not have the same kind of direct connection between the physical and risk-neutral worlds" and rely on "mathematical characterizations that are reasonable and simple to integrate ... but do not have any particular theoretical justification" (p.343). Two parametrisations: **(a) linear multiplier h^Q(t) = psi * h(t), psi >= 1**, and **(b) affine: h^Q(t) = psi_0 h(t) + psi_1 X_c(t)** with a common state variable (p.343-344). The estimation advice (p.344) is the desk-level recipe: back out risk-neutral intensities from the CDS market, estimate actual default probability from equity, convert to a physical intensity, "then the functional form relating risk-neutral to physical default intensities can be used to back out the implied market risk premium parameters. The objective is to triangulate across at least two markets." For valuation alone "we do not need to worry about the transformation" (p.342); for portfolio risk we need physical PDs, and for that the premium ratio (psi, or CDS spread over EDF-implied expected loss) is the traded quantity.

**Schmid's version (p.71, p.129, p.189-195).** The Cox (doubly stochastic) construction is Remark 2.3.4 (p.71): T^d = inf{t: integral_0^t lambda_s ds = E} with E ~ Exp(1) independent; constant intensity gives survival e^{-lambda (T-t)}, mean time to default 1/lambda (p.71). Proposition 3.3.1 (p.129, after Lando 1998): with zero recovery, the defaultable zero price is E^Q[exp(-integral (r + lambda~) dl) | F_t], where "under the risk-neutral probabilities default is also a Cox process" with intensity lambda~ (p.129). Schmid's three-factor model deliberately "start[s] our considerations in the 'real world' instead of the 'risk-adjusted world' and use[s] Girsanov's theorem for the change of measure" (p.189); under Q only the mean-reversion constants change (alpha -> alpha~) and the diffusion structure is unchanged (p.195, Remark 6.2.1). Schmid's calibration (p.263) makes the identification point concretely: "Because we use real world transition probabilities we can only estimate" the real-world uncertainty-index parameter and not its risk-adjusted twin (the tilde is lost in the text layer), so the Q-parameter is estimated "together with the parameters for the short rate spread from market prices of observed credit spreads". P-data identify the P-parameters only.

**Recovery conventions (Schmid p.95-96; Bohn-Stein p.131-132).** Schmid lists the three conventions the book already uses (fractional recovery of par RFV-type, of market value RMV, and equivalent default-free bond RT), noting that "different recovery models can be transformed into each other" because they are different numeraires, that RFV and RT "don't assume recovery of the coupons", and that "a proportional error in either the probability of default or recovery rate affects potential credit losses identically" (p.95). He also lays out the four-stage distress timeline (last cash paid date; default about six months later; bankruptcy between default and a year later; emergence) and that "the time spent in bankruptcy ... on average ... is approximately two years" (p.96). Bohn-Stein p.131-132 give the trader-versus-banker split: traders look at the post-default price, bankers at the ultimate recovery, and "determining how best to represent the discounted value of the ultimate recovery is nontrivial".

### 2.3 Seniority, liens and the absolute priority rule as data (Bohn-Stein p.289-317; Schmid p.95-99)

- **Bohn-Stein p.289 (LGD scale).** Most LGD estimates "fall between 30 and 70 percent, so that an extreme misestimation will be off by a factor of 2 to 3" whereas PDs span 1 bp to 3,500 bp. Their "spoonful of raw sewage" example: in 2000 senior subordinated bonds had a median post-default price of about 32% of par and the median deviation from that average was about 22 points, a "typical error ... on the order of 70 percent" (p.289).
- **U-shape of recovery (p.298-299).** The distribution has large masses at full recovery (LGD = 0) and zero (LGD = 100%); the paired pie chart separates the zero, one and fractional cases, which is the case for zero-and-one-inflated Beta in Ch 5 (p.299).
- **Which recovery? (p.299).** If the portfolio is liquid and must be sold within 30 days of default, "the post-default price is de facto the appropriate measure"; for illiquid or hold-through-resolution portfolios, ultimate recovery matters; "distressed-debt fund managers may find knowing both measures particularly useful for spotting potential investment opportunities", discounting nominal recovery to get an IRR. They cite Varma-Cantor (2005): the single-B spread is a reasonable discount rate that "on average, equated post-default prices with prices at emergence" (p.299).
- **Look-up tables (p.303, p.305).** "Debt type, seniority, industry, collateral" tables are the industry standard but historical-average tables "account for only about 40 percent of the power" of the LossCalc model (p.305).
- **Seniority class and relative debt cushion (p.309).** Debt type and seniority class are "the bases of most rating agency recovery studies"; controlling for seniority "addresses the additional effects of the Absolute Priority Rule" in North America; and a better measure than the label is the **relative debt cushion** "below the instrument of interest" or the share of debt standing senior, because preferred stock can be the most senior claim in a firm with no loans or bonds, and a senior class maturing reveals a lower class as most senior (p.309). Footnote 7 (p.309) defines APR as "satisfaction of the obligations of a defaulted firm in accordance with the seniority of the lien" and notes that outside the US and for small firms the lender consolidates debt into one class. This is the cleanest shelf statement that lien rank, not instrument label, is the regressor.
- **Schmid p.97 (APR is a prior, not a law of nature).** Figure 2.19 draws the seniority ladder (loans, senior secured, senior unsecured, senior subordinated, subordinated, junior subordinated, preferred, common). "This rule is more a theoretical than a practical one": Eberhart-Weiss (1998) find APR violated in 65-80% of bankruptcies; Moody's (Gupton et al.) average syndicated-loan recovery is 70% for senior secured against 52% for senior unsecured; Altman-Kishore sample of over 750 bonds averages 40.11% (p.97). **Dated:** see section 9.
- **Beta recovery (Bohn-Stein p.313-315, p.503, p.526).** A beta distribution for LGD with mean L is the working assumption; parameters estimated from transformed factors (p.315), reused in the portfolio model's variance-in-default-state calculation (p.526).

### 3.1 Price-yield and the yield-to-maturity (Ch 2, PDF p.65-101)

- **Discount-factor first (p.78-87).** A coupon bond is a portfolio of zeros: P_c(0,T_n) = sum_{i<n}(c/2) Z(0,T_i) + (1 + c/2) Z(0,T_n) (eq. 3.11, p.115). Bootstrapping discount factors from bonds is iterative: Z(t,T_i) = [P_c(t,T_i) - (c_i/2)100 sum_{j<i} Z(t,T_j)] / [100(1 + c_i/2)] (eq. 2.27, p.83); the procedure needs "all of the bonds, because otherwise the iterative procedure stops" (p.83).
- **YTM as an internal rate (p.83-86).** Definition 2.6 (eq. 2.30): the semi-annually compounded yield to maturity is "the constant rate y that makes the discounted present value of the bond future cash flows equal to its price" (p.83). The key distinction: "Unless the term structure of interest rates is exactly flat, the yields at various maturities are different, and will not coincide with the yield to maturity y" (p.84), and Fact 2.10: two bonds with the same maturity but different coupons have different yields. Worked check (p.84): on 15 Feb 2008, a 4.750% and an 8.875% Treasury, both maturing in 9.5 years, have fair prices 107.8906 and 141.5267 from one discount curve, hence yields 3.7548% and 3.6603%; the high-coupon bond trades at 140.0781, about 1% below fair value, which Veronesi attributes to illiquidity of old issues (p.84, footnote 9). That is a clean toy for "the same curve, two yields, one liquidity discount".
- **Reinvestment assumption (p.86).** "The price of the bond is (by definition) equal to the present value of the total payoff at T, discounted at the yield to maturity y, under the assumption that all of the coupons can be reinvested at same rate y over the life of the bond", which is "practically impossible", so YTM "is in fact a poor measure of expected return" (p.86).
- **Quoting (p.87).** Invoice = quoted + accrued; quoted is "clean", invoice is "dirty"; accrued = coupon x days since last coupon / days between coupons; day counts: Actual/Actual (Treasury notes and bonds), 30/360, Actual/360 (bills); T-bill discount-yield quoting d = (100 - P)/100 x 360/n, worked example 97.477 for a 182-day bill giving 4.99% (p.87).

### 3.2 Duration (Ch 3, PDF p.109-126; printed p.73-90)

- **Definition (p.112, eq. 3.1-3.2).** Duration is "the (negative of the) percent sensitivity of the price P to a small parallel shift in the level of interest rates": D_P = -(1/P) dP/dr, so dP = -D_P x P x dr. Example 3.1: a $100m portfolio with D = 10 loses $100,000 per basis point. Footnote 3 (p.112): this definition is "modified duration"; Veronesi reserves the word *duration* for it and "rarely" uses Macaulay.
- **Zero and coupon bonds (p.113-116).** D_zero = T - t (eq. 3.6). A portfolio's duration is the value-weighted average of component durations (Fact 3.3, eq. 3.10; Example 3.3: $100m of 5y and $200m of 10y STRIPS gives 8.3 and a $249,000 loss per bp). A coupon bond's duration is the weighted average of cash-flow times, the weights being discounted cash flow over price (eq. 3.12-3.13). Example 3.4: a 10-year 6% bond priced 107.795 has D = 7.762.
- **Duration is not average time in general (p.117-118).** Equivalent only for fixed cash flows. Floating-rate bond: D_FR = T_{i+1} - t, the time to the next reset (eq. 3.16); "securities for which the duration is actually longer than their maturity, or ... negative" exist (p.118). Ch 4-B should use this as the bridge to spread duration of FRNs and loans.
- **Properties (p.118-119, Table 3.2).** Duration falls as coupon rises (0% coupon: 10.00; 6%: 7.76; 12%: 6.88) and as the yield level rises (6% coupon, flat semi-annual yield 1%: 8.13; 5%: 7.76; 13%: 6.90) for the same two reasons: lower average payment time and lower sensitivity.
- **Traditional definitions (p.119-120, eq. 3.19-3.20).** Against the semi-annual yield, MD = (1/(1 + y/2)) x sum w_j T_j; "the weighted average of cash flow maturities ... is called the Macaulay duration". Veronesi's reasons for preferring spot-curve duration: YTM "is bond specific, it depends on the coupon rate", "is not well defined" for floaters and optioned bonds, and a parallel spot shift "is always well defined" so effective duration can be done numerically (p.120).
- **Dollar duration (p.120, eq. 3.21-3.22).** D$ = -dP/dr = P x D; for zero-value positions (swaps, long/short) only dollar duration is defined. This is the object under DV01 and CS01.
- **Hedging (p.126-133)** is a 2-bond duration-matching problem (section 3.3); asset-liability management p.133.

### 3.3 Convexity (Ch 4, PDF p.149-163)

- **Definition 4.2 (p.151, eq. 4.1):** C = (1/P) d^2P/dr^2, and **Fact 4.1 (eq. 4.2):** dP/P = -D dr + (1/2) C dr^2, "the convexity term C augments the precision of the approximation made by the duration term, but it does not substitute for it" (p.152). Footnote 1 reminds the reader to multiply C by 1/2 (Taylor).
- **Zero coupon (p.153, eq. 4.3-4.5):** C_z = (T - t)^2.
- **Worked test (Figure 4.2, Example 4.1, p.150, p.153).** A 20-year zero moves from r = 5% to 2.5%: price $36.79 to $60.65; duration-only gives $55.18; duration plus convexity (C = 400) gives $59.78 (the example prints the starting price once as 36.76, a typo; use 36.79). This is the cleanest regression case for any `bondmath.py` price-change decomposition: exact 60.65 versus 55.18 versus 59.78.
- **Finite-difference convexity (Appendix 8.8, PDF p.366):** C ~ (P(+x bp) + P(-x bp) - 2P)/(P (x bp)^2), the effective convexity that applies when no closed form exists; same for effective duration (p.120, p.366).
- **Slope and curvature, PCA (4.2, 4.6, PDF p.163-189)**: factor decomposition of curve moves, the gateway to key-rate and PCA-based hedges; and Ch 10.5 introduces *spot-rate duration* on a tree: D = -(1/V) (V_u - V_d)/(r_u - r_d), eq. 10.14-10.15 (PDF p.408).
- **Meaning conflation to avoid (book's "three meanings of convexity"):** Veronesi's convexity is a derivative with respect to a continuously compounded parallel shift. It is neither the callable bond's *negative* convexity (p.464) nor the positive convexity bias of volatility. Ch 4-B should state which is which with page-specific sources.

### 3.4 Callable bonds, negative convexity and OAS (Ch 12.1, PDF p.459-470; printed p.423-434)

- **Mechanics (p.460-463).** Callable bond value = non-callable bond minus an American call at the call price, valued on a recombining tree (Example 12.1 with a three-period toy; Example 12.2 prices a real callable Treasury). Optimal exercise is *not* simply "in the money": at node (15,5) the call is worth $8.37 while immediate exercise pays $7.404, so the issuer waits because "there is a value to waiting" (p.463, printed 427).
- **Example 12.2 (p.463-466).** Tree fitted by the simple BDT model to 8 Jan 2002 zeros; the 7.5% callable Treasury maturing 15 Aug 2014 with first call 15 Aug 2009; non-callable value $169.732 minus call option $22.33 gives a model price $147.40 against a quote of $146.5 (average bid-ask).
- **Negative convexity (p.464, Figure 12.1).** "While lower interest rates still increase the price of the bond, they do so at an increasingly lower rate, as opposed to an increasing rate as for any non callable bond." The bond price converges to the call price ($100) as rates fall; convexity turns negative below about 14% for this bond.
- **OAS (12.1.3, p.467).** "Securities with embedded American options are often traded on an option-adjusted-spread (OAS) basis." Definition: "The OAS represents the increase in the spot curve that is necessary for the model price to equal the traded price"; a positive OAS means the model had to raise yields to match, i.e. the bond is cheap after accounting for the option. In the example a 11 bp parallel shift moves the model price to $146.55 (equal to the ask), hence OAS = 11 bp. Caveats on the page: low liquidity can create a positive OAS (a liquidity discount); "the size of the OAS depends on the model used to compute it" (p.467). Footnote: a parallel spot shift is the same as a parallel forward shift so OAS can be computed either way.
- **Replication and relative value (12.1.4, p.467-470).** The "Buy Cheap and Sell Dear" trade: buy the underpriced callable, short the dynamic replicating portfolio of non-callables; equations 12.3-12.5 give the delta (N^L) and bond position (N^S) at each node with coupons; the replication must handle the issuer not exercising optimally (p.468). Worked numbers: three-period tree, callable model price 100.3564 versus quoted 100.1, N0^L = 0.5852, N0^S = 0.4188 (p.468-469).
- **MBS and effective duration (Ch 8, 12.3).** Prepayment as a short option; effective duration/convexity by shifting the curve (p.366 appendix; p.474-486); same toolkit used for loan prepayment, par calls and CLO refis.
- **Pricing versus risk measure (Section 10.5, PDF p.408).** "Pricing (task 1) is performed by using risk neutral probabilities, as shown earlier. Risk assessment (task 2) is performed by using risk natural, i.e., true, probabilities." This is the shelf's cleanest one-sentence statement of the "say which measure you are in" law (App E), in the same book as the OAS derivation. The structured-note Example 10.3 (p.408-409) shows the two numbers side by side: risk-neutral value $79.88 against $79.77 for a plain zero, but spot-rate duration 2.08 against 4.62.

### 3.5 Spread vocabulary on the shelf (Bohn-Stein p.68-69, Schmid p.159-161, Ben Dor)

- **Bohn-Stein p.68 (printed 36):** "The word spread is perhaps the most overloaded (and sometimes abused) word in finance." Their spread taxonomy: *bid-offer spread*; *total spread* (to Treasuries, or better to a corporate-risk-free "0-EDF" curve about 10-20 bp below swaps); *expected spread* = total spread minus expected loss; *credit spread* (EL plus the premium for credit risk only); *liquidity spread* (residual); *zero-coupon spread*; *par spread* (the floater-spread that prices at par). They say the swap curve is the best simple benchmark and define OAS ("some kind of lattice") and Z-spread (the same fitting when "the debt security does not have optionality attached", p.68). Footnote 17 p.69 explains why swaps are a proxy for a roughly AA borrower.
- **Schmid p.159-161 (5.3.4 "Par and Market Asset Swaps"):** the asset swap market "was born at the beginning of the 1990s"; figures 5.5-5.7 show the first and second trades of par and market asset swaps (pay Libor + asset-swap spread against the bond's coupon). It is the only on-shelf source for the asset-swap spread definition (and the origin of the par/market distinction).
- **Ben Dor (spread duration, DTS):** p.51 (average spread and spread duration of 30 buckets), p.267-270 (replicating portfolios by spread duration and DTS quadrants); Dynkin p.333 (key-rate durations, KRD), p.214 and p.226 (CDS DV01 in event studies). These carry the *credit* sensitivity measures (spread duration, DTS, KRD) that Veronesi lacks.

## 4. Greeks and option measures (Ch 6-B)

**Gap.** Natenberg and Hull are not on the shelf (Hull appears only as a reference: Bohn-Stein p.131 fn.10, p.613; Veronesi p.829, p.772). The searches "vega", "the Greeks" and "gamma/theta/delta" return only Veronesi p.611-612 and the Paleologo word "vegan". Greeks must therefore be built from four on-shelf anchors plus the web.

- **Black-Scholes-Merton as the credit Greek machine (Bohn-Stein p.135-137, printed 103-105).** The BSM PDE for equity as a function of assets, (1/2) sigma_A^2 A^2 E_AA + r A E_A + E_t - rE = 0; the **equity-delta identity** sigma_E = (dE/dA)(A/E) sigma_A ("a convenient, though sometimes misinterpreted, relationship", p.136); "moving from zero to 50 percent leverage, all else equal, will approximately double equity volatility" (p.136-137); "asset volatility is much more important to default prediction than is asset drift" and drift matters little under two to three years (p.137). The two-step estimation (solve for implied A and sigma_A from E and sigma_E, then DD) is the credit version of implied volatility. Appendix 3A (p.203-209) derives DD.
- **Hedge ratio = credit delta (Dynkin p.87; Ben Dor p.193).** Dynkin p.87: "a corporate bond (D) is short a put on the underlying asset (V) plus a risk-free asset, while the equity (E) is equivalent to a call option on V"; the analytical equity hedge ratio from Merton is the bond-return-to-equity-return delta, cited to Schaefer and Strebulaev (2008); the average ratio rises as ratings worsen and analytical ratios came in about 5% below empirical ones in both IG and HY (p.87, Table 1.6). Ben Dor p.193 is the rates-side delta (section 8 below).
- **Theta-gamma (Veronesi PDF p.611-612, eq. 16.17-16.18).** For a delta-hedged portfolio the fundamental pricing equation reduces to (1/Pi) dPi/dt + (1/2)(1/Pi) d2Pi/dr2 sigma^2 = r, so "High Theta <=> Low (or even negative) Gamma": "a large convexity (Gamma) is always counterbalanced by a large, and opposite, portfolio sensitivity to time, Theta" (p.611). The page uses a short call on a zero hedged with the zero: positive carry paid for by negative convexity. This is the exact intuition for "HY is a short put" and for carry-versus-convexity in callables.
- **Delta and replication (Veronesi Ch 9-10 and 16.1, PDF p.371-412, p.599-601; appendix 16.9 p.626).** Replicating portfolio N^L = (V_u - V_d)/(P_u - P_d) in the long instrument and N^S in the short bond (eq. 9.9-9.10; with coupons eq. 12.5, p.468); relative-value trade on the Vasicek tree: sell the traded option at $3.00 against a model value of $2.2921, hold Delta_0 = 0.4731 of the zero, "profit $0.7079" if the model is right (p.611).
- **Black's formula for caps and swaptions and the vol surface (Veronesi Ch 20, PDF p.721-740).** Flat versus spot volatilities and the cap-market analogue of YTM (p.726); the shelf has no equity-style skew/smile discussion or vega definition, so the "Greeks as risk-report lines" section of Ch 6-B needs a web or textbook source for vega, rho, and vanna/volga.
- **Where P and Q enter the Greeks (Veronesi p.408).** Delta hedging uses risk-neutral pricing, risk assessment the physical measure; the same sentence is quoted in section 3.4.
- **Importance sampling as P-to-Q mechanics (Bolder p.477-478).** Bolder defines the change of measure through the likelihood ratio: "The ratio ... often referred to as either the Radon-Nikodym derivative or the likelihood ratio" and notes it is "closely related to the Girsanov theorem", usable "to handle risk preferences, such as moving from P to Q" (p.478). That is the best on-shelf bridge between Greeks, Girsanov and variance reduction (Ch 7-B).

## 5. Fundamental law of active management (Ch 2, 4, 28, App E)

**Gap.** Grinold and Kahn are cited but not on the shelf (Paleologo p.205 references list; Ben Dor p.239 and p.398). The law itself, IR = IC x sqrt(BR) (Grinold 1989; Grinold-Kahn 1999/2000) must be derived by the book and sourced to the original (web-verified in section 6/Sources). The shelf gives four usable partial anchors, so Chapters 2, 4 and 28 can all cite one derivation plus these credit-specific illustrations.

- **Paleologo p.149-150 (printed 137-138; section 8.2.2 "The Relationship Between Performance and Diversification").** With hit probability p and N_e effective names, IR = (2p - 1) sqrt(252 N_e). Worked numbers: p = 51%, 70 names gives IR 2.6; p = 50.5% gives 1.3; with N_e = 3,000 an IR near 8 needs only p = 50.5%. "The power of diversification is great. It is not a skill: it is a skill multiplier." Counterweight on the same page: hit probability is not constant in breadth ("Beyond a certain threshold, coverage and breadth increase at the expense of accuracy"), so there is an optimum; the standard error of an annual hit rate with 90 names is 0.33%, comparable to the 0.5% edge, so a PM can see a *lower* measured IR after raising breadth almost half the time. "Increase diversification as much as you can, but not more." Procedure 8.1 defines the effective number of stocks as 1/H, H = sum of squared GMV weights (p.150). That is the breadth the law needs for correlated credit positions.
- **Ben Dor p.239-240 (printed 209-210; Chapter 8 Appendix).** "As in Grinold and Kahn (2000), we define skill as the correlation between the investor's predictions and the actual realizations"; with skill rho the conditional idiosyncratic variance is (1 - rho^2) sigma^2, so the hedge ratio for a pairs trade is H_min-var = (beta_l/beta_s)/(1 + (1 - rho^2)(sigma_s^2/(beta_s^2 sigma_m^2))) and converges to half the beta ratio as rho -> 0 (eq. 8.8). IC enters credit pair-trade hedging as a shrinkage factor on the hedge.
- **Dynkin p.345 (Chapter 7, concessions).** Desclee, Dynkin, Hyman, Maitra and Polbennikov (2014) report alpha, volatility and IR for market timing, sector rotation and issuer selection "assuming typical levels of skill"; "issuer selection generated much higher alpha due to the fact that the 'breadth' of the strategy is much larger" and the concession alpha (average 14 bp/yr since 2008, above 20 bp in 2008-09) "does not require skill" (p.345). This is the shelf's only credit-specific breadth illustration.
- **Man 2022 p.28 (IR ladder)**: single-factor IRs of 2.15 (value), 1.69 (momentum), 1.55 (quality), 0.79 (informed investor), and 2.35 combined; Ch 28 can use the combination as the empirical counterpart of breadth across (partly correlated) signals, with the caveat that these are model back-test IRs, not realized.
- **Dated/limits:** the law assumes independent bets, constant IC, unconstrained long-short implementation; the transfer-coefficient extension (Clarke, de Silva, Thorley 2002), the problem of serial dependence in credit signals and the overlap/clustering of many bonds per issuer (Ch 7-B) are web-sourced (section 9).

## 7. Bolder and Bohn-Stein: threshold models, copulas, tail dependence (Ch 7-B, Ch 5, Ch 6)

Source: `Bolder_Credit_Risk_Modelling` (David Jamieson Bolder, *Credit-Risk Modelling: Theoretical Foundations, Diagnostic Tools, Practical Examples, and Numerical Recipes in Python*, Springer, 2018; 704 PDF pages; printed page roughly PDF - 25 to - 29). Python-code boxes sit in the text (e.g. Algorithm 4.8).

- **Gaussian one-factor threshold (4.1, PDF p.179-185).** Latent y_n = sqrt(rho) G + sqrt(1 - rho) eps_n (eq. 4.8) with y_n ~ N(0,1) and pairwise correlation rho (eq. 4.9-4.10); default when y_n falls below Phi^-1(p_n). Obligor-specific loadings rho_n give correlation sqrt(rho_n rho_m) (eq. 4.11-4.12). "Conditioning on the same global variable through the parameter, rho, creates default dependence" (PDF p.182).
- **Merton equals a threshold model (5.4, PDF p.283).** "If one uses default probabilities to infer the default barrier ... the Merton (1974) approach reduces to a one-factor threshold model" (p.283); the default event is {tau <= T} = {A_T <= K} (eq. 5.4, p.260), where tau is the first-passage time, "a special case of a more general probabilistic concept termed a stopping time" (p.260 fn.4).
- **Tail dependence coefficient (4.3, PDF p.204-212).** Upper: Lambda = lim_{q->1} P(Y > F_Y^-1(q) | X > F_X^-1(q)) (eq. 4.56-4.57); lower: lim_{q->0} C(q,q)/q (eq. 4.58-4.60), and for exchangeable copulas Lambda = 2 lim P(U1 <= q | U2 = q) (eq. 4.63, using dC/du_2 = P(U1 <= u1 | U2 = u2), eq. 4.61, "neither precise, nor probably even exactly true, but ... useful motivation" for the proof, fn.11). **Gaussian: Lambda = 0 for rho < 1** (eq. 4.66), "an important result ... independent of the value of the correlation coefficient. Since credit-risk computations routinely examine outcomes at the 99.97th quantile ... this is not merely a theoretical exercise" (PDF p.208-209). **Student-t: Lambda = 2 F_{T_{nu+1}}(-sqrt((nu+1)(1 - rho)/(1 + rho)))** (eq. 4.68; Algorithm 4.8), "an increasing function of the correlation coefficient, but a decreasing function of the degrees-of-freedom parameter", non-zero for all rho in (-1,1) (PDF p.210-212). t-threshold construction: y_n = sqrt(nu/W)(sqrt(rho) G + sqrt(1-rho) eps_n), W ~ chi^2(nu), var(y_n) = nu/(nu - 2) (eq. 4.69-4.72, PDF p.213-214).
- **Why it matters numerically (Table 1.2, PDF p.61).** On Bolder's sample portfolio, at the 99.97th percentile VaR is $84.7 for independent defaults, $229.1 for the threshold model and $197.3 for the mixture model; expected shortfall $92.3, $270.0 and $222.9. Dependence matters 2.5-3x in the far tail.
- **Normal-variance mixtures (4.5, PDF p.222-239), canonical multi-factor setting (4.6-4.7, PDF p.240-254)**: the generalisation that embeds Gaussian, t, VG and others in one latent-variable frame; Chapter 10 estimates default and asset correlation by method of moments, likelihood and transition likelihood (PDF p.597-655), which feeds the Ch 7-B calibration section.
- **Bohn-Stein Chapter 8 (PDF p.487-580).** Structural default correlation via a bivariate normal (p.501), credit migration and DD transition densities (p.502-507), and a section on copulas and frailty for reduced-form portfolio models (p.566-571); also lattice models of DD (p.544). Use as the structural-model counterpart to Bolder's threshold chapter.
- **Monte Carlo and the change of measure (Bolder Ch 8, PDF p.454-511; 8.5.1 importance sampling p.477-478).** Standard error, variance reduction (importance sampling with likelihood ratio f_X/h; Glasserman-Li 2005 for credit portfolios, p.478-480), interval estimation (8.4, p.470). Schmid p.65-66 notes that where "explicit solutions for first passage times are not known ... the conditional default probability can be determined, e.g., by Monte Carlo simulation" (p.66), which is where the Brownian-bridge correction (section 6) enters.
- **Recovery-default dependence (Bohn-Stein Ch 5, Ch 8; Schmid Ch 2).** Beta LGD with portfolio variance in the default state (Bohn-Stein p.526); Schmid p.97 on APR violations; both feed Ch 5's mixture-of-moments.

## 8. Dynkin and Ben Dor: empirical duration regimes (Ch 4-B, Ch 8)

Sources: Ben Dor, Dynkin, Hyman, Houweling et al., *Quantitative Credit Portfolio Management* (Wiley, 2012; `Ben_Dor_Quantitative_Credit_Portfolio_Management`, Chapter 7 "Empirical versus Nominal Durations of Corporate Bonds", PDF p.188-200; printed 158-170) and `Dynkin_Systematic_Investing_in_Credit` (Dynkin, Hyman, Polbennikov et al., Wiley, 2018; Chapter 10 "Coupon Effects in Corporate Bonds", PDF p.429-453; Chapter 1 p.56-125).

- **Derivation (Ben Dor Ch 7, PDF p.190).** Two-factor return model R_i ~ -D_i dy - D_i s_i ds_rel (eq. 7.1); relation of relative spread change to yield change, d s_rel/dy ~ rho_{y,s} sigma_s/sigma_y (eq. 7.2); hence **empirical duration = -D_i(1 + s_i rho_{y,s} sigma_s/sigma_y)** (eq. 7.3). "If this correlation is negative, the empirical duration would be lower than the analytical one and may even end up being negative" (PDF p.191). The regression (eq. 7.4): R_i = beta_i D_i dy + gamma_i D_i s_i dy + eps, so the **empirical hedge ratio H(s) = beta + gamma s** (eq. 7.5): beta is the zero-spread limit, gamma <= 0.
- **Estimates (Table 7.1, PDF p.192).** Barclays daily data, 1 Aug 1998 to 30 Nov 2009, six rating buckets, OAS-lagged one day. Beta (hedge-ratio limit) Aaa-Aa 0.94, A 0.92, Baa 0.85, Ba 0.41, B 0.15, Caa 0.07; spread slope gamma -0.05 (Aaa-Aa, A, Ba), -0.02 (Baa, Caa), -0.03 (B); hedge ratio at average OAS 0.88, 0.85, 0.80, 0.22, -0.03, -0.15; at maximum OAS Ba -0.26, B -0.42, Caa -0.52. Model-based (eq. 7.3) at average spread: 0.95, 0.93, 0.88, 0.24, -0.04, -0.23; rho_{y,s} -0.14 (Aaa-Aa), -0.17, -0.23, -0.50 (Ba), -0.56 (B), -0.40 (Caa); relative-spread volatility 1.6-2.5% across buckets ("consistent with the DTS paradigm"); Treasury-yield volatility 6.4 bp/day. R^2 collapses from 0.79 (Aaa-Aa) to 0.06 (Ba) and 0.01 (B, Caa): the HY regression says rates explain almost nothing.
- **Regime reading (PDF p.193).** IG hedge ratios decline with spread "but ... maintain strong sensitivity to interest rates at all relevant spread levels. In contrast, high-yield bonds seem almost completely disconnected from interest rates, even at spread levels that overlap with" IG (p.193). Baa example: beta 0.85 and gamma -0.02 give 0.83 at a 1% spread and 0.75 at 5% (p.193). Daily data before 2006 were only available as rating-bucket aggregates (p.191); the sample includes the 2008 crisis, so the regime dependence is partly a crisis effect (Dated, section 9).
- **Dynkin Chapter 10 (PDF p.435-440).** Pooled monthly regression, all noncallable bonds in the Bloomberg Barclays US IG Corporate Index, January 1992 to April 2014, dependent variable carry-adjusted return (total return minus one-twelfth of beginning yield): Model 1 base hedge ratio 0.97 (t = 405) with spread sensitivity -0.13 per point of spread; Model 2 adds coupon/price-distance terms. Findings: empirical duration "is slightly lower than analytical duration for low spreads and continues to decrease as spreads rise"; premium bonds' rate sensitivity is higher when yields rise and lower when they fall, discount bonds the reverse, effect proportional to price distance from par (p.438); a coupon-spread effect of about 10 bp of spread per 1% of coupon since 2008 (p.435). Explanation (p.437): negative rate-spread correlation plus proportional (DTS) spread changes, so the price effect of the correlation is larger for wide-spread bonds.
- **Dynkin Chapter 1 (PDF p.70-103)**: whether a bond can be replicated by Treasuries plus same-issuer equity; analytical (Merton) versus empirical (OLS) hedge ratios (p.87, p.117); empirical-duration alternative is also discussed at p.127 ("estimate empirical durations as a function of analytical durations, as suggested in prior QPS studies").
- **Original paper:** Ambastha, Ben Dor, Dynkin, Hyman and Konstantinovsky (2010), "Empirical Duration of Corporate Bonds and Credit Market Segmentation", *Journal of Fixed Income* 20(1):5-27 (Dynkin p.449 reference list).
- **Mapping to Ch 4-B and Ch 8.** Ch 4-B should present analytical duration (Veronesi) then the empirical-duration identity (Ben Dor eq. 7.3) as the bridge to Ch 8, using Table 7.1 as the empirical regime table; Ch 8's sign discipline ("negative for CCC") rests on the B and Caa rows (-0.03, -0.15 at average OAS; -0.42, -0.52 at maximum OAS).
