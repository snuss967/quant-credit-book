# Advisor Brief: Chapter 1, The Anthology of the Desk & the Modern Quant Credit Landscape
## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for an ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** Credit is not one market; it is a plumbing diagram of holders who each carry a *contractual or regulatory trigger that converts a rating, a test, or a redemption into a sale at a known time and a knowable size*. Insurers sell on downgrade because capital charges jump at the IG/HY line; IG index funds and ETFs sell at the month-end rebalance after a fallen-angel event; CLOs sell (or stop reinvesting) when CCC buckets breach ~7.5% and OC cushions thin; open-end HY funds sell whatever is liquid when redemptions arrive; banks and CDOs dump post-reorg equity because they are "not natural holders"; trade-claim holders sell because they cannot wait for a plan. Every one of those sales is *information-free about the bond and information-rich about the seller*. The whole systematic-credit industry, from Blackstone's "contrarian liquidity provider" optimizer to Man Numeric's fallen-angel program to the SIG-style RFQ engine, is a set of machines for standing on the other side of those flows at the right price. The shelf, read this way, is one book: Ben Dor tells you how to size the risk you take when you catch the flow (DTS); Bohn–Stein and Schmid tell you how to price the default you might be catching (DD, intensity); Bolder tells you what happens when the flows correlate (threshold models); Dynkin and Man tell you which flows are systematically harvestable (fallen angels, equity momentum); Moyer and Houlihan Lokey tell you where the flows end up when the company breaks (fulcrum, 363); Paleologo tells you how to tell whether you actually made money doing it (IDO).
**The aha.** An RSSG banker already knows the *triggers* better than any quant: which test, which clause, which date. What they have never done is write the triggers down as a **forced-supply function** $Q^{\text{sell}}(\text{event})$ with a size and a timestamp, and then price the *other* leg: how much of that supply the natural buyers absorb before the price has to move. The quant desk's entire edge is that function. Man Group says the quiet part out loud on the shelf: quant credit "is yet to move into distressed debt – this is a high-touch asset class that requires a significant degree of human intervention" (Man 2022 p.42). The reader is the human intervention, pre-packaged. Chapter 1's job is to make them see their old job as a feature pipeline, and the ecosystem as a graph whose edges they can already label.
**Second aha (the bid equation).** The seed's §1.1 bid formula is not decoration; it is the reservation price of a CARA dealer plus a Glosten–Milgrom adverse-selection term, and each of its three pieces maps to one part of the book: fair value (Parts II, VI), inventory skew (Parts V, VIII), toxicity (Parts III, IV, VII). Derive it in Chapter 1 so the rest of the book is a commentary on three terms.

## 3. Derivation skeletons
### 3.1 The dealer's reservation bid (measure: $\mathbb{P}$, the desk's own beliefs)
Setup: inventory $q$ (notional, long positive) in a bond with current mid $S_t$; residual (post-hedge) P&L variance per unit time $\sigma^2_{\text{IDO}}$; holding horizon $h$ years; CARA risk aversion $\gamma$ with Gaussian P&L, so certainty equivalent $= \mathbb{E} - \tfrac12\gamma\,\mathrm{Var}$. Let $m = \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[S_{t+h}\mid \mathcal{F}_t, \mathcal{O}_t]$ be the conditional fair value *given the order*.
Indifference between not trading and buying $\Delta q$ at price $B$:
$$
(q+\Delta q)\,m - B\,\Delta q - \tfrac12\gamma\sigma^2_{\text{IDO}}h\,(q+\Delta q)^2 \;=\; q\,m - \tfrac12\gamma\sigma^2_{\text{IDO}}h\,q^2
$$
$$
\Rightarrow\quad B^{\text{res}} \;=\; m \;-\; \tfrac12\,\gamma\,\sigma^2_{\text{IDO}}\,h\,(2q+\Delta q). \tag{1.1}
$$
Glosten–Milgrom conditioning: with probability $\pi$ the seller is informed and expects $S_{t+h} = S_t - J$ (the post-event mark, e.g. after an LMT or an 8-K); with probability $1-\pi$ the seller is a liquidity/forced seller and $\mathbb{E}[S_{t+h}] = S_t$. Then
$$
m = S_t - \pi J, \qquad
B^* = S_t - \underbrace{\pi(\mathbf{x})\,J}_{\Lambda_{\text{tox}}} - \tfrac12\gamma\sigma^2_{\text{IDO}}h\,(2q+\Delta q) - c(\Delta q), \tag{1.2}
$$
with $\pi(\mathbf{x}) = \big(1+e^{-\theta^\top \mathbf{x}}\big)^{-1}$ on features $\mathbf{x}$ = (size, minutes since the lender call, days to earnings, counterparty type, dealers in competition, counterparty's past markouts), and $c(\Delta q)$ the hedge half-spread plus expected liquidation cost (the LCS of Ben Dor Ch 5 is one estimate). This is exactly the seed's formula with $\sigma^2 h$ written as per-horizon variance.
Error-prone spots: (i) units: $S$ per 100 of par and $q$ in millions gives $B$ in nonsense units unless $\gamma$ is per \$ of P&L; (ii) $h$ in days with $\sigma$ annualized; (iii) using total bond variance instead of residual variance after the CDX/UST/ETF hedge (that is Ch 8's job); (iv) conditioning twice (if $m$ already conditions on the RFQ, do not subtract $\pi J$ again); (v) the $\Delta q^2$ term is routinely dropped, which under-skews large RFQs; (vi) Gaussian CE with jump-to-default is wrong; small-$\gamma$ expansion gives an extra $q\lambda L h$ term (Ch 18). All expectations are $\mathbb{P}$; $S_t$ may come from a $\mathbb{Q}$ model, but the markout is a $\mathbb{P}$ object.
### 3.2 DTS: spread changes are proportional to spread (measure: none; empirical)
Excess return over duration-matched Treasuries: $r^{\text{xs}} \approx -D^{\text{spr}}\,\Delta s$. If $\Delta s = s\,\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon$ the relative spread change, $\mathrm{sd}(\varepsilon)=\sigma_{\text{rel}}$ stable across sectors and time (Ben Dor p.39: 5% to a bit over 10% per month), then
$$
\sigma(r^{\text{xs}}) \approx D^{\text{spr}}\, s\,\sigma_{\text{rel}} = \text{DTS}\cdot\sigma_{\text{rel}}, \qquad
\text{exposure to a sector's relative move} = \sum_i w_i D^{\text{spr}}_i s_i. \tag{1.3}
$$
Issuer limit: cap $w_i D_i s_i \le \kappa$ (Ben Dor p.61's worked example: 5 = 1% at 100 bp × 5y, 0.33% at 150 bp × 10y). Error spots: spread duration, not modified duration; OAS for callables; state units (bp vs decimal: the relative vol is unitless, DTS is years×spread); the slope steepens in crises (Ben Dor p.26); for bonds quoted on dollar price (distressed), $s$ loses meaning and the risk moves to price/recovery space, which is the Mags objection and the Part IV hand-off.
### 3.3 Distance to default and its mapping (measure: $\mathbb{P}$ for DD/EDF; $\mathbb{Q}$ for $d_2$)
Merton: $E_t = V_t N(d_1) - F e^{-r(T-t)} N(d_2)$, $d_{1,2} = \dfrac{\ln(V_t/F) + (r \pm \tfrac12\sigma_V^2)(T-t)}{\sigma_V\sqrt{T-t}}$. The physical distance to default replaces $r$ with the asset drift $\mu_V$:
$$
\text{DD}_t = \frac{\ln(V_t/F) + (\mu_V - \tfrac12\sigma_V^2)(T-t)}{\sigma_V\sqrt{T-t}}, \qquad
\text{PD}^{\mathbb{P}}_{\text{BSM}} = N(-\text{DD}). \tag{1.4}
$$
Bohn–Stein p.138: most public firms have DD > 4, which Gaussian maps to ~0.00003; MKMV's empirical map turns DD = 4 into an EDF of "almost 1 percent." Error spots: $\sigma_E \ne \sigma_V$ (need $\sigma_E E = \sigma_V V N(d_1)$, Ch 23); $\mu_V$ vs $r$; the default point convention (short-term liabilities plus half of long-term is the commonly reported KMV choice; mark as such); one-year horizon.
### 3.4 The credit triangle (measure: $\mathbb{Q}$)
Constant hazard $\lambda$, flat $r$, continuous premium, recovery $R$ of par at default:
$$
\text{PV}_{\text{prot}} = (1-R)\int_0^T \lambda e^{-(r+\lambda)t}\,dt,\quad
\text{PV}_{\text{prem}} = s\int_0^T e^{-(r+\lambda)t}\,dt
\;\Rightarrow\; s = \lambda(1-R) \tag{1.5}
$$

# Advisor Brief — Chapter 2: The Revolutions in Credit Trading (TREND)
## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for an ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** Systematic credit did not become possible because someone found a better factor. It became possible because the *plumbing* changed: post-trade transparency (TRACE, 2002–2006) created the dataset, electronic RFQ (2000s–2010s) created firm, timestamped, comparable prices, all-to-all (2013 onward) let the buy side *make* prices, automation (2018 onward) let a model send and answer thousands of small tickets, portfolio trading and ETF create/redeem (2018 onward) let dealers price and hedge baskets in seconds, and non-bank market makers (2023 onward) brought balance sheets that are priced by models rather than relationships. Man Numeric's 2023 paper is the spine: the historical barrier to quant credit was "execution and the ability to model transaction costs and liquidity within decades-old manual processes" (`Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023 p.2`), and the revolutions removed it. The chapter's job is to date each revolution, size it with sourced numbers for 2018–2026, and show the causal arrow from market structure to the two things a systematic strategy needs: **certainty of execution** and **a modelable cost** (`Man_Revolutions p.4`).
**The aha for Theo (ex-RSSG).** At PJT the unit of analysis is a document and a process; prices arrive by phone, late, from a dealer who is also your counterparty. Theo's instinct is that a bond's value lives in the indenture. The desk's instinct is that a bond's *tradability* lives in its data exhaust: how many prints per day, how many dealers answer an RFQ, how far the cover is from the winner, whether the bond is in an ETF redemption basket. The aha is that **electronification turned liquidity from a relationship into a measurable, forecastable variable**, and that this variable, not the credit agreement, decides whether an alpha is harvestable. The second, sharper aha: TRACE's `5MM+` cap means the market still hides exactly the trades Theo used to care about (blocks in stressed names), so the RSSG skill set is most valuable precisely where the data exhaust is thinnest.
**One-sentence version for the cold open:** the bond market spent twenty years building the dataset that lets a machine price a bond in three seconds, and the first thing the machine learned is which prints it is not being shown.

## 3. Derivation skeletons
All of this chapter's mathematics is under the physical measure $\mathbb{P}$; there is no pricing measure in market-structure statistics. Say so once, early, because Kaz will ask.
### DER A — The adoption S-curve and the two-point logit fit
Let $e_t \in (0,1)$ be the electronic share of notional in a segment. Model
$$e_t = \frac{K}{1+\exp\{-r\,(t-t_0)\}}, \qquad 0<K\le 1,$$
with $K$ the ceiling (blocks that never leave the phone), $r$ the adoption rate, $t_0$ the midpoint. With $K=1$,
$$\operatorname{logit}(e_t) \equiv \ln\frac{e_t}{1-e_t} = r\,(t-t_0),$$
so two dated observations give a closed-form slope
$$\hat r = \frac{\operatorname{logit}(e_{t_2})-\operatorname{logit}(e_{t_1})}{t_2-t_1}, \qquad \text{odds-doubling time } = \frac{\ln 2}{\hat r}.$$
Plugging Coalition Greenwich's 2015 → 2025 shares (IG 20% → 48%, HY 6% → 32%): $\hat r_{IG}=0.131/\text{yr}$, $\hat r_{HY}=0.200/\text{yr}$; odds-doubling 5.3 years (IG) and 3.5 years (HY); naive $K=1$ projections for 2030 are 64% (IG) and 56% (HY). **Label these *illustrative extrapolations*.** Error-prone spots: (i) $K<1$ is the honest model (≈90% of blocks were still voice/chat in 2023), so the $K=1$ fit overstates the ceiling; (ii) denominators differ (notional vs ticket count vs "customer volume"); Bondwave's PT share of ~20% and Trumid/MarketAxess's ~11–12% of TRACE describe the same market with different denominators; (iii) "processed" trades (voice-negotiated, booked electronically) inflate platform shares; The DESK applies a −40% haircut to some platform figures (fi-desk, Jan 14, 2026); (iv) HY e-share *falls* in stress (29–30% in Feb–Mar 2025), so monthly points are noisy; fit on annual data.
### DER B — From cost to breadth: why cheaper tickets create alpha
Grinold's fundamental law, $IR \approx IC\sqrt{BR}$, with breadth $BR$ the number of independent bets per year. Net alpha per unit of gross exposure is
$$\alpha_{\text{net}} = \alpha_{\text{gross}} - \mathrm{TO}\cdot c,$$
with $\mathrm{TO}$ annual one-way turnover and $c$ the one-way cost (fraction of notional). The break-even turnover is $\mathrm{TO}^* = \alpha_{\text{gross}}/c$, so the feasible breadth of a cost-constrained strategy scales as $1/c$. ## 1. Thesis and the "aha"
**Thesis.** The restructuring banker and the market maker are solving the same inference problem with different clocks. RSSG asks *"Will this company survive, and who gets the keys?"* and answers it over 90 hours with a 13-week cash flow model and a credit agreement. The desk asks *"Given that someone just asked me for a bid, what is the conditional distribution of this bond's value?"* and answers it in three seconds. Both answers are posteriors. The chapter's job is to show that every RSSG skill is a **likelihood ratio** the desk can use, and that the desk's two instruments for measuring whether it was fooled, the Glosten–Milgrom posterior and the markout, are just the banker's "who's on the other side of this exchange offer?" instinct with timestamps.
**The "aha" for an ex-RSSG reader.** Three of them, in escalating order:
1. *A survival question is a degenerate distribution.* Moyer already does this: two pari passu bonds, the near one at 85 and the far one at 60, imply a 62.5% refinancing probability because "the probability-weighted value of each outcome should be equal: 15 × 0.625 = 25 × 0.375" (Moyer p.407). The RFQ is an information exchange before it is a trade. The adverse-selection charge is the price of the information you are about to lose.
3. *The LMT is the purest adverse-selection event in credit.* In Serta (June 22, 2020), 52.6% of the first-lien class knew they were about to prime the other 47.4% (Mayer Brown, July 2026, VERIFIED). Anyone selling first-lien paper to the future participating lenders in May 2020 was the uninformed trader in Glosten–Milgrom. ### 3A. Glosten–Milgrom for an RFQ (measure: $\mathbb{P}$, the desk's physical beliefs)
**Setup.** A client sends a sell RFQ for size $Q$ in bond $i$. Let the dealer's prior on the bond's fair value one holding period ahead be two-point: $V \in \{m+\sigma_V,\, m-\sigma_V\}$ with equal probability, so the prior mean is the mid $m$ and the prior standard deviation is $\sigma_V$. With probability $\pi$ the sender is informed (knows $V$); with probability $1-\pi$ uninformed, selling for liquidity reasons with probability $\tfrac12$ regardless of $V$. An informed sender sells only when $V = m-\sigma_V$.
**Step 1 (likelihoods).** $\mathbb{P}(\text{sell} \mid V = m+\sigma_V) = \tfrac{1-\pi}{2}$ and $\mathbb{P}(\text{sell} \mid V = m-\sigma_V) = \tfrac{1-\pi}{2} + \pi$.
**Step 2 (posterior).** $\mathbb{P}(V = m+\sigma_V \mid \text{sell}) = \dfrac{\tfrac{1-\pi}{2}}{\tfrac{1-\pi}{2} + \tfrac{1-\pi}{2} + \pi} = \dfrac{1-\pi}{2}$.
**Step 3 (zero-profit bid).** $b = \mathbb{E}[V \mid \text{sell}] = (m+\sigma_V)\tfrac{1-\pi}{2} + (m-\sigma_V)\tfrac{1+\pi}{2}$, hence
$$b^{\,\text{GM}} = m - \pi\,\sigma_V, \qquad a^{\,\text{GM}} = m + \pi\,\sigma_V. \tag{3.1}$$
The half-spread equals the informed probability times the prior uncertainty. This is the $\Lambda_{\text{tox}}$ term in Chapter 1's bid equation, now with a formula.
**General (non-symmetric) version** for the writer who wants it: with prior $q = \mathbb{P}(V^+)$ and gap $\Delta = V^+ - V^-$,
$$m - b = \Delta\,\frac{q(1-q)\,\pi}{\tfrac{1-\pi}{2} + \pi(1-q)} \;\approx\; 2\pi\,q(1-q)\,\Delta \quad (\pi \text{ small}).$$
**Step 4 (RFQ features).** Replace the constant $\pi$ with a posterior on the sender given the RFQ's observable vector $x$:
$$\pi(x) = \sigma\!\left(\beta_0 + \beta^\top x\right), \qquad \text{equivalently}\quad \frac{\pi(x)}{1-\pi(x)} = \frac{\pi_0}{1-\pi_0}\prod_k \text{LR}_k(x_k)$$
under conditional independence (naive Bayes), where $x$ includes: $\log Q$ relative to ADV; hours to the next scheduled event (earnings, lender call) and days since the last 8-K; counterparty type (dealer, systematic fund, distressed fund, CLO, ETF AP, retail aggregator); number of dealers in competition $n$; the sender's trailing shrunken markout score (3B); whether the RFQ direction is *against* the dealer's published axe; the issuer's Priming Risk Score $\text{PRS}$ (Ch 13). Each $\text{LR}_k$ is estimated from the desk's own labeled history, where the label is the realized 20-day IDO markout sign.
**Step 5 (many dealers: the winner's curse).** With $n$ dealers quoting on a common value, you trade only when your bid is the highest. Conditional on winning, the other $n-1$ signals were lower than they looked unconditionally, so the equilibrium bid shades further:
$$b_n^{*} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[V \mid s_i,\; \max_{j\neq i} s_j \le s_i\right] < \mathbb{E}[V \mid s_i],$$
with the shading increasing in $n$ (Milgrom–Weber 1982 logic). FGP (2016) find exactly this in BNP Paribas RFQ data: dealer quote distributions become more conservative as the number of requested dealers rises. Also note the empirical twist that part of the effect is *non-answer*: the probability each dealer answers falls with $n$, and clients typically wait for only about two answers. Writer's compact form:
$$b^{*}(x,n) = m - \pi(x)\,\sigma_V - w(n)\,\sigma_V - \tfrac12\gamma\sigma_{\text{IDO}}^2\,(2q + Q),$$
where $w(n)$ is the winner's-curse shading (a monotone increasing function of $n$ calibrated from cover prices) and the last term is the Avellaneda–Stoikov inventory skew previewed from Chapter 18. State loudly that $\sigma_V$ here is the dealer's *physical* uncertainty over the holding horizon, not an implied vol.
**Error-prone spots.** (i) Using $\sigma_V$ in yield or spread units but $m$ in price; convert with $D^{\text{spr}}$. (ii) Conditioning on the trade happening when calibrating $\pi$ from markouts (survivorship: only fills have markouts; use FGP's Done/Covered/Traded-Away outcomes to see the counterfactual). (iii) Treating $n$ as exogenous: FGP show clients choose $n$, and HM Table VI shows IG clients query *fewer* dealers for larger tickets, consistent with leakage rising in size, so $n$ is itself a signal. (iv) Forgetting that the all-to-all responder on the other side may be another client (Open Trading was 37% of MarketAxess eligible credit volume in 2025), so "counterparty type" is a distribution, not a label.
### 3B. Markouts and counterparty toxicity (measure: $\mathbb{P}$)

## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for an ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** "Systematic credit" is not one business. It is at least five businesses that share a word, and the five framework papers on the shelf are five *different answers* to the same six engineering questions: where alpha comes from (alpha families), what you are allowed to know (data stack), how you turn a forecast into positions (portfolio construction), how you get filled (execution), how big you can be before you are the market (capacity), and what you hedge versus what you harvest (risk). Lay the papers on those six axes and you get a map, and the empty cells on that map are the book's exploratory space. The industry is small (systematic strategies run roughly 20% of global equities but under 2% of fixed income, per `Blackstone_Systematic_Credit p.2` and, independently, Bloomberg via BBH, March 2026), it is growing fast (quant-screened IG/HY funds at \$90–140bn per Barclays QPS, cited by Impax, September 2025; "systematic credit funds doubled in size in 2023" per Bloomberg, May 2024, via BBH), and it became possible for one reason: execution became measurable (Man Numeric moved from ~30% HY / ~60% IG electronic order flow to over 90% by May 2023, `Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023 p.3`).
**The aha.** The RSSG reader expects the quant shops to compete on math. **The second aha (the Q-minus-P point).** Every "value" signal in the industry, from DCI's structural-model spread comparison to BlackRock's "default-adjusted spread," is literally the gap between the market's risk-neutral loss rate embedded in the spread ($\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}} L$) and the manager's physical estimate ($\lambda^{\mathbb{P}} L$). ## 3. Derivation skeletons
All expectations below are under $\mathbb{P}$ unless stated. Notation follows the style bible ($s$ spread in decimal, $D^{\text{spr}}$ spread duration, $\lambda$ default intensity, $L=1-R$).
**D1. The fundamental law with issuer clustering.** Grinold's law $IR = IC\sqrt{BR}\cdot TC$ assumes independent bets. Credit has $N$ bonds from $M$ issuers, $m = N/M$ bonds per issuer, with intra-issuer residual correlation $\rho$. For an equal-weighted average of $N$ unit-variance bets,
$$\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum_i \epsilon_i\right) = \frac{1}{N^2}\big[N + N(m-1)\rho\big] = \frac{1+(m-1)\rho}{N},$$
so the effective number of independent bets is
$$BR_{\text{eff}} = \frac{N}{1+(m-1)\rho}, \qquad IR = IC\sqrt{BR_{\text{eff}}}\;TC. \tag{4.1}$$
Boundary checks: $\rho=0 \Rightarrow BR_{\text{eff}}=N$; $\rho=1 \Rightarrow BR_{\text{eff}}=M$ (47 bonds of one issuer are one bet). Paleologo's binomial form (`Paleologo p.149`) $IR=(2p-1)\sqrt{252\,N_e}$ with $N_e = 1/\sum w_i^2$ is the same idea with hitting probability $p$; use it for intuition. Error-prone spots: using bond count as breadth; ignoring that $TC$ (transfer coefficient) collapses when fill probability is below one (`Man_…_2022 p.19`: an unfilled order leaves "unintended and potentially unmodelled risk exposures"); assuming $IC$ is constant across breadth (Paleologo warns it is not).
**D2. Capacity under square-root impact.** Let gross alpha per dollar per year be $\alpha_0$, one-way annual turnover $T$ (fraction of AUM), half-spread cost $s_{\text{tc}}$ per dollar traded, and impact $k\sqrt{A/\mathcal{L}}$ per dollar traded where $A$ is AUM and $\mathcal{L}$ is the annual liquidity actually accessible to the strategy's names. Net alpha per dollar and dollar alpha are
$$\alpha(A) = \alpha_0 - T\Big[s_{\text{tc}} + k\sqrt{A/\mathcal{L}}\Big], \qquad \Pi(A) = A\,\alpha(A).$$
Maximizing $\Pi$: $\Pi'(A) = (\alpha_0 - T s_{\text{tc}}) - \tfrac{3}{2}Tk\sqrt{A/\mathcal{L}} = 0$, hence
$$A^{*} = \mathcal{L}\left[\frac{\alpha_0 - T s_{\text{tc}}}{\tfrac{3}{2}\,T k}\right]^{2}, \qquad \alpha(A^{*}) = \frac{\alpha_0 - T s_{\text{tc}}}{3}. \tag{4.2}$$
The punchline: at the manager's fee-maximizing size, the client keeps one-third of spread-net gross alpha; the client's IR-maximizing size is $A\to 0$. That is the principal–agent gap behind every "capacity" slide. Assumptions to state: impact is square-root in participation; $\mathcal{L}$ is strategy-specific (Dynkin's cap of 5% of issuer market value is the discrete analogue); costs are linear in turnover. Error-prone: mixing one-way and round-trip turnover; using index ADV instead of the ADV of the names the signal actually selects; annual vs per-trade units.
**D3. Dynkin cap weights and the non-invested fraction** (`Dynkin p.207–208`). With $n$ eligible issuers, an issuer concentration limit $w_{\max}$, and a cap of $c$ times issuer market value $MV_i$,
$$w_i = \min\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n},\; w_{\max},\; \tfrac{c\,MV_i}{A}\right), \qquad f_{\text{cash}}(A) = 1 - \sum_i w_i. \tag{4.3}$$
Identity: the portfolio depends on $c/A$ only (a \$500mn book at 5% equals a \$250mn book at 2.5%). As $A$ grows the book drifts from equal-weight to market-value-weight and $f_{\text{cash}}$ rises; Barclays finds a \$1bn three-month-reversal fallen-angel book only 53% invested in August 2004 vs 98% for Buy-All.
**D4. Nuveen's forward-return identity** (`Nuveen p.3`). For sector $j$ over horizon $h$,
$$\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[R_j] = y_j - \underbrace{PD^{\mathbb{P}}_j\,L_j}_{\text{expected credit loss}} - D^{\text{mod}}_j\,\Delta y^{\text{TSY}} - D^{\text{spr}}_j\,\Delta s_j, \qquad \Delta s_j = \kappa_j\,(\bar{s}_j - s_j)\,h. \tag{4.4}$$
Measure: $y_j$ and $s_j$ are market (risk-neutral-ish) quantities; $PD^{\mathbb{P}}$ must be physical (rating-agency or model). Error-prone: using yield-to-worst carry for callable HY while also charging a spread-MTM term (double counting optionality); the z-score mean-reversion assumption when index composition shifts (HY index 51% BB at YE2024 vs 37% in 2004, `Nuveen p.4`). Note for the writer: the PDF extraction scrambles the labels in Figure 3; the arithmetic only works as yield 6.97 − credit loss 2.55 − rates MTM 0.37 − spread MTM 0.49 = **forward return 3.56%** for US HY as at 19 Nov 2025 (CHECK against the figure before quoting).
**D5. "Value" is the Q−P gap.** With the credit triangle $s_i \approx \lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}_i L_i$ and a structural-model physical intensity $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_i$, the default-adjusted spread is
$$V_i = s_i - \lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_i L_i \approx \big(\lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}_i - \lambda^{\mathbb{P}}_i\big)L_i = \text{risk premium}_i + \text{liquidity}_i + \text{mispricing}_i, \tag{4.5}$$
then neutralized cross-sectionally: $\tilde V_i = V_i - \hat{\mathbf{\beta}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i$ where $\mathbf{x}_i$ holds rating, sector, duration and size exposures (Man's three-step "raw → risk-adjust → peer-relative," `Man_…_2022 p.24`, with raw value $\text{OAS}_i / \max(\text{NetDebt}/\text{EBITDA}, 0.1)$). Error-prone: negative or near-zero EBITDA (the clip), $\lambda^{\mathbb{P}}$ from equity vol used without the Merton leverage mapping, and treating $V_i$ as mispricing when it is mostly premium (which is Blackstone's carry argument, not its value argument).
**D6. Why beta-tilt defense loses.** Define upside and downside capture $UC = \sum_{t: r_{m,t}>0} r_{p,t} / \sum_{t: r_{m,t}>0} r_{m,t}$ and $DC$ analogously. For a pure beta tilt $r_p = \beta r_m$, $UC = DC = \beta$ and expected excess return is $(\beta-1)\,\mathbb{E}[r_m] < 0$ whenever the credit premium is positive. BlackRock's numbers (average HY manager $UC \approx 92.2\%$, $DC \approx 89.6\%$, 2005–2025; 168 up months vs 72 down months, `BlackRock p.3–4`) say the average manager is $\beta \approx 0.9$ plus a sliver of selection. Defense that survives the rebound must come from $IC$ on default avoidance, not from $\beta$. Error-prone: capture ratios computed on total vs excess returns; regime bins defined ex post.

# Advisor Brief — Chapter 5: Probability Distributions in Distressed & High-Yield Credit
## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for an ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** A high-yield bond's return is not a noisy number; it is a *mixture*: with probability $1-q$ the bond survives and its return is a small, nearly Gaussian spread wiggle plus carry; with probability $q$ it jumps to recovery. Everything strange about credit distributions (negative skew, enormous kurtosis, bounded price support, "fat" tails that are really a second mode) falls out of that two-line sentence, and the recovery leg is itself a bounded random variable that a Beta distribution describes well *only after you condition on collateral, seniority, debt cushion, and, since 2020, whose side of the cooperation agreement you were on.* Lévy and jump-diffusion processes are the continuous-time grammar for the jump; extreme-value theory is the right tool for *unbounded* objects (spread changes, index and ETF losses) and the wrong tool for a single bond whose loss is capped at 100 points.
**The aha for the RSSG reader.** You have been building recovery distributions for years and calling them "waterfalls." Moyer's Table 10-9 (Moyer p.251) is a recovery *function*: pick an EV multiple, run the absolute-priority waterfall, read off recovery per class. The quant's Beta recovery model is the same object with one extra step: put a probability distribution on the EV multiple and integrate the waterfall over it. Moody's did exactly this in 2006 — firm-wide recovery drawn from a Beta on $[0, 120\%]$ of liabilities with mean 50.21% and standard deviation 26.46%, then pushed through the priority-of-claims waterfall (Moody's LGD methodology, Aug 2006, VERIFIED). Your 13-week cash-flow model is the physical-measure hazard rate for the next quarter (running gag #1 lands here: weeks-of-liquidity is $\Pr(\tau \le 13\text{ weeks})$ wearing a spreadsheet). The thing the RSSG seat gives you that a PhD lacks is the *conditioning set*: you know which covenant flags move the Beta's mean, and you know that "first lien" on a loan-only, cov-lite, sponsor-backed structure with 75%+ of the debt stack pari passu is a label, not a cushion.
Three memorable results the chapter should hang on the wall:
1. **Skew is minus one over root PD.** For a pure survive-or-jump mixture, skewness is $-(1-2q)/\sqrt{q(1-q)}\approx -1/\sqrt{q}$ and excess kurtosis is $\approx 1/q$. A 1% default probability gives skew $\approx -10$ and excess kurtosis $\approx 100$. Kaz's Gaussian has skew 0 and a résumé.
2. **Recovery is U-shaped, not bell-shaped.** Ultimate recoveries pile up at 0 and 100 (Bohn–Stein p.297: "recovery can be an all-or-nothing process"). Method-of-moments Betas fit to Moody's long-run senior-unsecured moments come out with $a<1$ (spike at zero); fit to first-lien loans they come out with $b<1$ (spike at par). The LME era adds a third spike: Serta's plan put participating lenders at 73.7–100% and non-participants at 0.6–2.4% (VERIFIED).
3. **"40% recovery" is a conversion convention, not a forecast.** It is the number the CDS standard model uses to turn spreads into upfronts (Dynkin p.354 calls it "a representative average"); the physical, instrument-level distribution has a standard deviation around 27 points (Schmid p.103) and a mean that has been falling for first-lien loans for a decade (§6).

## 3. Derivation skeletons
### 3.1 The mixture and its moments (measure: $\mathbb{P}$ for risk, $\mathbb{Q}$ for pricing; say which)
Horizon $h$ years, full price $P_0$ per unit par, default indicator $D\sim\text{Bernoulli}(q)$ with $q = 1 - S^{\mathbb{P}}(h)$. Survival return (rates hedged, so excess-return space):
$$X = y\,h - D^{\text{spr}}\,\Delta s + \tfrac12 C^{\text{spr}}(\Delta s)^2,\qquad \Delta s \sim \text{(Gaussian or } t\text{), } \sigma_{\Delta s} = \kappa\, s \text{ (DTS scaling)}.$$
Default return $Y = R/P_0 - 1$ with $R\sim\text{Beta}(a,b)$ (plus optional point masses, §3.4). Mixture $R_h = (1-D)X + DY$. With $J \equiv \mu_X - \mu_Y$:
$$\mathbb{E}[R_h] = \mu_X - qJ,\qquad \operatorname{Var}[R_h] = (1-q)\sigma_X^2 + q\sigma_Y^2 + q(1-q)J^2,$$
$$\mu_3 = -q(1-q)(1-2q)J^3 + 3q(1-q)J(\sigma_X^2-\sigma_Y^2) + (1-q)\mu_{3,X} + q\,\mu_{3,Y},$$
$$\mu_4 = (1-q)\!\left[q^4J^4 + 6q^2J^2\sigma_X^2 + 4qJ\mu_{3,X} + \mu_{4,X}\right] + q\!\left[(1-q)^4J^4 + 6(1-q)^2J^2\sigma_Y^2 - 4(1-q)J\mu_{3,Y} + \mu_{4,Y}\right].$$
Bernoulli limit ($\sigma_X=\sigma_Y=0$): $\text{skew} = -(1-2q)/\sqrt{q(1-q)}$, $\text{exkurt} = (1-6q(1-q))/(q(1-q))$. **Error-prone spots:** (i) using $\mathbb{Q}$ default probabilities (from CDS) in a $\mathbb{P}$ risk report, which overstates $q$ by a risk-premium multiple (Bohn–Stein App. 3B maps PD to PD$^{\mathbb{Q}}$); (ii) forgetting the coupon collected before default; (iii) $J$ must use $R/P_0$, not $R$: a bond bought at 60 that recovers 40 lost 33%, not 60%; (iv) mixing excess and raw kurtosis in sanity checks.
### 3.2 Bounded price support and the three distributions
Callable bond: $P = P^{\text{straight}}(s) - C(s)$, so as $s\downarrow$, $P\to K_{\text{call}}$ and $\partial P/\partial s \to 0$ (negative convexity, same mechanism as Veronesi's prepayment cap p.338–340). At default $P\to R$. Support is $[R, K_{\text{call}}+\epsilon]$, which is why a long-horizon *price* distribution is itself Beta-shaped on a shifted interval, and why "the bond is 102 bid, what's my upside" is a question with a cap. Spread: DTS says $\sigma(\Delta s)\propto s$ (Ben Dor Ch.1), so $\log s$ is roughly homoskedastic and $s$ is right-skewed; the return is then left-skewed by $-D^{\text{spr}}$. Keep three ledgers.
### 3.3 Jump-diffusion and Lévy skeletons (measure: $\mathbb{Q}$ when pricing, $\mathbb{P}$ when simulating risk; parameters differ)
**Merton (1976).** $dV_t/V_{t^-} = (\mu-\lambda k)dt + \sigma dW_t + (e^{Y}-1)dN_t$, $Y\sim N(\mu_J,\sigma_J^2)$, $k = e^{\mu_J+\sigma_J^2/2}-1$. Log-return over $T$ is a Poisson mixture of Gaussians:
$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda T}(\lambda T)^n}{n!}\,\phi\!\left(x;\ (\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2 - \lambda k)T + n\mu_J,\ \sigma^2 T + n\sigma_J^2\right).$$
**Kou (2002) double exponential.** $f_Y(y) = p\,\eta_1 e^{-\eta_1 y}\mathbf{1}_{y\ge0} + (1-p)\,\eta_2 e^{\eta_2 y}\mathbf{1}_{y<0}$, $\eta_1>1$ for finite $\mathbb{E}[e^Y]$. Memorylessness makes first passage analytic (Kou–Wang 2003; Chen–Kou 2009 in a Leland–Toft setting). Two results the writer should derive: (a) instantaneous jump-to-default intensity with barrier $B<V$: $\lambda(1-p)(B/V)^{\eta_2}$, hence the short-maturity spread $s(0^+) = \lambda^{\mathbb{Q}}(1-p)(B/V)^{\eta_2}\,L^{\mathbb{Q}}$, non-zero (the diffusion-only Merton short spread is zero); (b) **recovery given a jump default is the barrier times an exponential undershoot**: $V_\tau = B e^{-O}$, $O\sim\text{Exp}(\eta_2)$, so $\mathbb{E}[V_\tau/B] = \eta_2/(1+\eta_2)$. Fatter downward jumps (small $\eta_2$) mean both more defaults *and* worse recoveries from the same parameter: PD–LGD correlation for free.
**Variance gamma (Madan–Seneta 1990; Madan–Carr–Chang 1998).** $X_t = \theta G_t + \sigma W_{G_t}$, $G_t\sim\Gamma(t/\nu, \nu)$. $\varphi_{X_t}(u) = (1 - iu\theta\nu + \tfrac12\sigma^2\nu u^2)^{-t/\nu}$; $\operatorname{Var}=(\sigma^2+\theta^2\nu)t$; for $\theta=0$, skew $0$ and excess kurtosis $3\nu/t$. **NIG (Barndorff-Nielsen 1997).** $\varphi(u) = \exp\!\big(iu\mu t + \delta t[\sqrt{\alpha^2-\beta^2} - \sqrt{\alpha^2-(\beta+iu)^2}]\big)$, mean $\mu+\delta\beta/\gamma$, variance $\delta\alpha^2/\gamma^3$, skew $3\beta/(\alpha\sqrt{\delta\gamma})$, excess kurtosis $3(1+4\beta^2/\alpha^2)/(\delta\gamma)$, $\gamma=\sqrt{\alpha^2-\beta^2}$. **Error-prone spots:** $\mathbb{P}$ vs $\mathbb{Q}$ parameters of the jump (the risk premium lives mostly in $\lambda$ and the jump mean); Bohn–Stein p.182 on jump-parameter instability; Bolder p.229: gamma and GIG mixing variables have no power tail, so VG/NIG give kurtosis without tail dependence; excess kurtosis of VG decays like $1/t$, so a daily fit says nothing about a quarterly horizon.
### 3.4 Beta recovery: moments, regression, inflation, conditioning, measure
Density $f(r) = r^{a-1}(1-r)^{b-1}/B(a,b)$ on $(0,1)$. $\mu = a/(a+b)$, $\sigma^2 = \mu(1-\mu)/(a+b+1)$, skew $= 2(b-a)\sqrt{a+b+1}/\big((a+b+2)\sqrt{ab}\big)$, excess kurtosis $= 6[(a-b)^2(a+b+1) - ab(a+b+2)]/[ab(a+b+2)(a+b+3)]$. Method of moments (Bohn–Stein p.315): $a = \mu\left[\mu(1-\mu)/\sigma^2 - 1\right]$, $b = (1-\mu)\left[\mu(1-\mu)/\sigma^2 - 1\right]$, feasible only if $\sigma^2 < \mu(1-\mu)$. Hand-computed illustrations from stated moments (*author's arithmetic, illustrative*): senior unsecured bonds $\mu=0.40,\sigma=0.275$ (Schmid p.103 Moody's 1970–2002Q2: mean 40.07%, s.d. 27.50%) give $a\approx0.87$, $b\approx1.30$, a density that *spikes at zero*; a first-lien loan book with $\mu\approx0.70,\sigma\approx0.30$ gives $a\approx0.93$, $b\approx0.40$, a spike at par; Moody's family recovery on $[0,1.2]$ (mean 50.21%, s.d. 26.46%) rescales to $a\approx1.68$, $b\approx2.33$, interior mode.
**Beta regression (Ferrari–Cribari-Neto 2004).** $r_i\sim\text{Beta}(\mu_i\phi, (1-\mu_i)\phi)$, $\operatorname{logit}\mu_i = x_i^\top\beta$, log-likelihood per observation
$$\ell_i = \ln\Gamma(\phi) - \ln\Gamma(\mu_i\phi) - \ln\Gamma((1-\mu_i)\phi) + (\mu_i\phi - 1)\ln r_i + ((1-\mu_i)\phi - 1)\ln(1-r_i)$$
(this is exactly the form on Bohn–Stein p.315–316). # Advisor Brief — Chapter 6: Stochastic Calculus, Intensity Models & Structural Frameworks
## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for an ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** Every credit model is a statement about *when* $\tau$ happens and *what you get* when it does. Structural models (Merton, Black–Cox, KMV) say $\tau$ is the first time a diffusing asset value hits a boundary you can read off a balance sheet; reduced-form models (Jarrow–Turnbull, Lando, Duffie–Singleton) say $\tau$ is a surprise whose arrival rate $\lambda_t$ you read off a CDS curve. The desk needs both, in the right measure, with the right recovery convention, wired into one engine. The chapter's job is to build that engine from Itô's lemma up and to show where each model's assumptions were quietly repealed by the 2020–2026 market (cov-lite, uptiers, distressed exchanges, private credit with no equity ticker).
**The four ahas, in the order they should land on Theo:**
1. **Your 13-week model is a hazard rate wearing a spreadsheet.** The probability of running out of liquidity in week $k$ *given you survived week $k-1$* is $\lambda_k\,\Delta t$. The survival column is $S(t_k)=\prod_{j\le k}(1-\lambda_j\Delta t)\to e^{-\int_0^{t}\lambda_s ds}$. The reader has been building piecewise-constant hazard curves for years without the Greek letter (running gag #1 pays off here).
2. **Equity is a call; the fulcrum is the strike.** Merton (1974): $E_0 = V_0N(d_1)-Fe^{-rT}N(d_2)$; debt $=Fe^{-rT}-\text{Put}$. Every waterfall the reader ever built is a payoff diagram; the "fulcrum security" is the tranche whose strike sits where the asset distribution has mass. Hertz 2020–21 is the diagram made flesh: old equity got \$1.53 cash plus 3% of the new company plus warrants/rights because the call was not, in fact, out of the money (SEC S-1, 2021).
3. **The covenant you spent 90 hours modeling is a Black–Cox barrier, and the LMT you structured moved the barrier for someone else.** Black and Cox (1976) literally called it a "safety covenant." Cov-lite removed the barrier; a non-pro-rata uptier raises the effective default point of the left-behind lenders without touching the issuer's asset process. The modern "default" is a negotiated barrier crossing: distressed exchanges were ~65% of Q4 2024 defaults (S&P, Feb 2025; **CHECK** exact figure, bot-blocked).
4. **The market quotes $\mathbb{Q}$; RSSG trained you in $\mathbb{P}$; the gap is the premium systematic desks harvest.** $DD^{\mathbb{Q}} = DD^{\mathbb{P}} - \rho\,\lambda_{\text{Sharpe}}\sqrt{T}$ (Bohn–Stein App. 3B). Blackstone's "value" signal is exactly spread vs. model PD (Blackstone p.1); BlackRock's PD screen caught 77% of HY defaulters within 24 months (BlackRock p.6). Structural models did not die in 1984; they moved to the systematic desk and got a data feed.

## 3. Derivation skeletons
Notation per style bible: $V_t$ assets, $F$ face, $E_t$ equity, $\sigma_V,\sigma_E$, $r$, $\tau$, $\lambda_t$, $S(t)$, $R$, $L=1-R$, $s$. Say the measure every time.
### 3.1 Itô and GBM ($\mathbb{P}$)
$dV_t=\mu V_t\,dt+\sigma_V V_t\,dW_t$. For $f(t,V)$: $df=\big(f_t+\mu V f_V+\tfrac12\sigma_V^2V^2 f_{VV}\big)dt+\sigma_V V f_V\,dW_t$. With $f=\ln V$:
$$d\ln V_t=\Big(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2\Big)dt+\sigma_V\,dW_t,\qquad V_T=V_0\exp\!\Big[\big(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2\big)T+\sigma_V W_T\Big].$$
The $-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2$ is the tax; it comes from $(dW_t)^2=dt$. Error-prone: forgetting the tax when simulating (drift biased up by $\tfrac12\sigma^2$); using daily $\sigma$ without $\sqrt{252}$.
### 3.2 Merton (1974) ($\mathbb{Q}$ for prices, $\mathbb{P}$ for PDs)
Single zero-coupon debt of face $F$ at $T$; equity $E_T=\max(V_T-F,0)$; debt $D_T=\min(V_T,F)=F-\max(F-V_T,0)$. Replication (Bohn–Stein p.206) gives the Black–Scholes PDE $\tfrac12\sigma_V^2V^2E_{VV}+rVE_V+E_t-rE=0$. Closed forms:
$$E_0=V_0N(d_1)-Fe^{-rT}N(d_2),\qquad d_{1}=\frac{\ln(V_0/F)+(r+\tfrac12\sigma_V^2)T}{\sigma_V\sqrt T},\quad d_2=d_1-\sigma_V\sqrt T.$$
$$D_0=V_0-E_0=Fe^{-rT}N(d_2)+V_0N(-d_1)=Fe^{-rT}-\underbrace{\big[Fe^{-rT}N(-d_2)-V_0N(-d_1)\big]}_{\text{Put}}.$$
Spread (verified signs; define quasi-leverage $\ell=Fe^{-rT}/V_0$):
$$s(T)=-\frac1T\ln\frac{D_0}{Fe^{-rT}}=-\frac1T\ln\!\Big[N(d_2)+\frac{V_0e^{rT}}{F}N(-d_1)\Big]=-\frac1T\ln\!\Big[N(d_2)+\frac{N(-d_1)}{\ell}\Big].$$
Short end: for $V_0>F$, as $T\downarrow0$, $N(d_2)\to1$ and $N(-d_1)\to0$ faster than any power of $T$, so $s(T)\to0$: *a Merton firm cannot default tomorrow unless it is already dead today.* Risk-neutral PD $=N(-d_2)$. Physical PD $=N(-DD)$ with
$$DD=\frac{\ln(V_0/F)+(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2)T}{\sigma_V\sqrt T}.$$
Equity vol link (Itô on $E=f(V,t)$): $\sigma_E E_0=N(d_1)\,\sigma_V V_0$. Error-prone: mixing $\mu$ and $r$ inside one $d$; using $\sigma_E$ as $\sigma_V$ (overstates PD by the leverage factor $V/E\cdot N(d_1)$); calling face value $D$ (reserved for duration).
### 3.3 $\mathbb{P}\to\mathbb{Q}$ (Bohn–Stein App. 3B)
Girsanov: $dW^{\mathbb{Q}}_t=dW_t+\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma_V}dt$. With CAPM $\mu-r=\beta(\mu_M-r)$, $\rho=\text{corr}(r_A,r_M)$ and Sharpe $\lambda_{\text{Sharpe}}=(\mu_M-r)/\sigma_M$:
$$DD^{\mathbb{Q}}=DD-\rho\,\lambda_{\text{Sharpe}}\sqrt T,\qquad PD^{\mathbb{Q}}=N\!\big(N^{-1}(PD^{\mathbb{P}})+\rho\lambda_{\text{Sharpe}}\sqrt T\big)>PD^{\mathbb{P}}.$$
Error-prone: applying the shift to a PD that already came from spreads (double counting); forgetting the $\sqrt T$ (the premium scales with horizon). Bai–Goldstein–Yang (2020) is the reminder that a *jump* risk premium is needed to reconcile historical default rates with IG spreads, so the diffusion-only shift under-prices the premium.
### 3.4 KMV / Vasicek–Kealhofer ($\mathbb{P}$)
Two unknowns $(V_0,\sigma_V)$, two equations: $E_0=\text{BSM}(V_0,\sigma_V)$ and $\sigma_E E_0=N(d_1)\sigma_VV_0$. Newton/fsolve on $(\ln V_0,\ln\sigma_V)$ with the Jacobian; or the VK iteration (Bohn–Stein p.169): 156 weekly equity returns → guess $\sigma_V$ → invert $E=f(V)$ each week → asset returns → new $\sigma_V$ → repeat to convergence. Default point $X=\text{STD}+\tfrac12\text{LTD}$ (Crosbie–Bohn 2003; TITLE-ONLY) after removing deferred taxes/minority interest and *adding* material off-balance-sheet liabilities (Bohn–Stein p.159). KMV's reported DD is the simplified $(V_0-X)/(V_0\sigma_V)$ at a 1-year horizon, mapped to EDF through an empirical, monotone DD→EDF table (≥400 defaults minimum, 1,000 recommended, half-sigma cohorts 0–16; Bohn–Stein p.161–162). Bharath–Shumway (2008): a "naive" DD using $\sigma_V\approx\frac{E}{E+F}\sigma_E+\frac{F}{E+F}(0.05+0.25\sigma_E)$ forecasts about as well as the iterated solution; the functional form, not the solver, carries the information. Error-prone: ill-conditioned when $E\ll F$ (deep distress: $N(d_1)\to0$, Jacobian singular; bound $\sigma_V\in[0.01,3]$ and $V_0\ge E_0$); nonstationary $\sigma_E$ from leverage changes (Bohn–Stein p.136 fn.12); normal DD→PD gives zero PD for 60% of firms (p.160).
### 3.5 Black–Cox first passage ($\mathbb{P}$ or $\mathbb{Q}$ by drift)
Barrier $K_t=Ke^{-\gamma(T-t)}$; default $\tau=\inf\{t:V_t\le K_t\}$. Let $X_t=\ln(V_t/K_t)$, $x_0=\ln(V_0/K_0)>0$, drift $m=\mu-\tfrac12\sigma_V^2-\gamma$ (replace $\mu\to r-q$ under $\mathbb{Q}$ with payout $q$). Reflection principle for Brownian motion with drift:
$$\mathbb{P}(\tau\le T)=N\!\Big(\frac{-x_0-mT}{\sigma_V\sqrt T}\Big)+e^{-2mx_0/\sigma_V^2}\,N\!\Big(\frac{-x_0+mT}{\sigma_V\sqrt T}\Big).$$
Check: $m=0$ gives $2N(-x_0/\sigma_V\sqrt T)=2\,pd$, the "$pd_a=2pd$" line on Bohn–Stein p.151. Equity = down-and-out call; debt = $Fe^{-rT}$ − put + rebate at barrier (Bohn–Stein p.152 boundary conditions). Covenant mapping: a maintenance leverage test $\text{Debt}/\text{EBITDA}\le c$ is a barrier in EBITDA space; translate via $V\approx m_{\text{EV}}\cdot\text{EBITDA}$ so $K=\text{Debt}\cdot m_{\text{EV}}/c$. Cov-lite: $K\to$ payment default only (barrier at coupon/maturity dates, i.e., Geske-style discrete monitoring). Uptier: left-behind lenders' $F$ is effectively subordinated to new super-priority debt $F^{+}$, so *their* strike moves from $F$ to $F+F^{+}$ while $V_t$ is unchanged. Monte Carlo trap: discrete monitoring underestimates hitting; Brownian-bridge correction per step, with log-values $x_i,x_{i+1}$ above log-barrier $b$:
$$p_i=\exp\!\Big(-\frac{2(x_i-b)(x_{i+1}-b)}{\sigma_V^2\Delta t}\Big),$$
draw $U_i\sim\text{Unif}$, hit if $U_i<p_i$ (Glasserman 2004, TITLE-ONLY). Feldhütter–Schaefer (2018): Black–Cox calibrated to historical default rates fits IG spreads; under-prices HY, partly illiquidity.
### 3.6 Cox processes, Jarrow–Turnbull, Duffie–Singleton ($\mathbb{Q}$ unless stated)
Construction (Schmid p.71): $\tau=\inf\{t:\int_0^t\lambda_sds\ge\Theta\}$, $\Theta\sim\text{Exp}(1)$ independent of the factor filtration. Then $S(t,T)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[e^{-\int_t^T\lambda_udu}\,\big|\,\mathcal{F}_t\big]$ and the zero-recovery bond (Lando 1998; Schmid p.129):
$$P^{d,0}(t,T)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\Big[e^{-\int_t^T(r_u+\lambda_u)du}\Big|\mathcal{F}_t\Big].$$
Three recoveries (Schmid p.95; Bohn–Stein p.348): **RMV** (Duffie–Singleton): recover fraction $R$ of pre-default market value → discount at $r_t+\lambda_tL_t$, instantaneous spread $s_t=\lambda_t^{\mathbb{Q}}L_t$; **RFV** (recovery of par, CDS convention): $P^d=P^{d,0}+R\int_t^T P(t,u)\,\big(-\partial_uS(t,u)\big)du$; **RT** (Jarrow–Turnbull): recover $R$ riskless bonds of same maturity, $P^d=P(t,T)\big[R+(1-R)S(t,T)\big]$ under independence. Jarrow–Turnbull (1995) is the constant-$\lambda$, RT special case. Error-prone: converting a 40% RFV quote into RMV for a bond trading at 60 (different numeraire; Schmid p.95); independence of $r$ and $\lambda$ assumed in RT formula. Duffie–Lando (2001) bridge: with noisy accounting, a structural model *acquires* an intensity and short spreads become strictly positive — the "why reduced-form at the short end" argument in one paper.
### 3.7 CIR intensity ($\mathbb{Q}$)

# Advisor Brief — Chapter 7: Machine Learning on Small-Sample, Non-Stationary Credit Data
## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for the ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** Corporate-bond data is the worst machine-learning dataset in liquid finance, and the correct response is not a bigger model but a *smaller, more honest sample*: fewer effective observations (issuer-grouped, uniqueness-weighted), labels that respect how credit actually pays off (path-dependent, DTS-scaled, censored by calls and exchanges), cross-validation that cannot peek (purged, embargoed, grouped), and a performance statistic that already knows how many things you tried (the deflated Sharpe ratio). The 2019–2026 literature is a live demonstration: a *Journal of Financial Economics* bond-factor paper was retracted in 2023 for one-month label misalignment; a *Review of Financial Studies* ML paper needed a corrigendum for look-ahead; and an April 2026 "factor zoo" of 108 bond signals found that only 26 of 432 specifications (6.0%) survive a false-discovery correction, with short-term reversal's premium falling from −0.99% to −0.09% per month once the same noisy price was stopped from appearing in both the signal and the return denominator. Credit ML in 2026 is, first, a data-integrity discipline.
**The aha.** The RSSG reader already does meta-labeling for a living. A restructuring banker never asks "will this bond go up?"; they ask "given that the thesis is *this covenant trapdoor*, how confident am I, and how much do I bet?" That is exactly López de Prado's primary-model/secondary-model split: the primary model supplies the *side* (the credit-agreement read: J.Crew-style drop-down capacity, Serta-style non-pro-rata uptier risk, a 13-week liquidity runway), and the ML secondary model learns only *when the analyst is right*, outputting a probability that becomes bet size. The pure quant cannot build the primary model because the primary model is legal prose turned into state-space. The banker cannot size it because they have never been made to estimate precision and recall. Chapter 7 is where the two halves meet.
Second aha, smaller and sharper: **the issuer is the observation.** Dynkin et al. report that as of 31 May 2018 the US IG index had 629 issuers and the HY index 784 (p.651). Whatever your bond-month count says, your effective cross-section is closer to a thousand names, each with a dozen correlated children. Forty-seven bonds from one issuer are one data point wearing forty-seven hats. Every method in the chapter is a way to stop counting hats.

## 3. Derivation skeletons
All expectations in this chapter are under $\mathbb{P}$ (we are forecasting realized excess returns and labels). The only $\mathbb{Q}$ objects that enter are *features* (OAS, Z-spread, CDS-implied hazard $\lambda_t$, Merton distance-to-default), and the writer should say so once: features may be risk-neutral quantities; labels and losses are physical.
### 3.1 Fractional differentiation (Hosking 1981; AFML ch. 5)
Backshift $B x_t = x_{t-1}$. For real $d>0$,
$$(1-B)^d = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \binom{d}{k}(-B)^k = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} w_k B^k,\qquad w_0 = 1,\quad w_k = -\,w_{k-1}\,\frac{d-k+1}{k}.$$
So $w_1=-d$, $w_2 = d(d-1)/2$, $w_3 = -d(d-1)(d-2)/6$, and for $0<d<1$ the weights alternate in sign only at $k=1$ and then decay hyperbolically in magnitude, $|w_k|\sim k^{-d-1}/\Gamma(-d)$, which is the "long memory" (Hosking's abstract: persistence that fades "much more slowly" than ARMA). Fixed-width window: keep $k \le K(\tau)$ where $|w_k| \ge \tau$ (e.g. $\tau = 10^{-4}$), producing $\tilde x_t = \sum_{k=0}^{K} w_k x_{t-k}$ with a constant lookback so the transformed series is not non-stationary by construction. Choose $d^\star = \min\{d : \text{ADF}(\tilde x^{(d)}) \text{ rejects at } 5\%\}$ and report the correlation $\rho(\tilde x^{(d^\star)}, x)$ as "memory retained."
*Credit-specific:* apply to $x_t = \log s_t$ (log OAS), not $s_t$, because Ben Dor et al. show relative spread volatility is stable (5–10%/month) while absolute spread volatility scales with the level (p.39). Error-prone spots: (i) the first $K$ observations are NaN, and for a bond issued 14 months ago with $K=60$ you have nothing; use issuer-curve spreads for history; (ii) jumps (defaults, LMT announcements) are not made stationary by any $d$ and will dominate ADF; (iii) stale marks already inject spurious autocorrelation, so a "low $d^\star$" on an illiquid bond is a liquidity artifact (Dynkin p.771 on autocorrelation as staleness).
### 3.2 Triple-barrier labels in DTS space
For bond $i$ at formation time $t$ with spread duration $D^{\text{spr}}_i$ and spread $s_{i,t}$, define cumulative excess return over $u$ trading days $X_{i,t}(u) = \sum_{v=1}^{u} r^{\text{xs}}_{i,t+v}$. Ben Dor et al.: $\sigma(r^{\text{xs}}) \approx \text{DTS}_i\cdot\sigma_{\text{rel}}$ with $\text{DTS}_i = D^{\text{spr}}_i s_i$ (p.50; slope estimates 8.8%–9.8% per month per unit DTS, p.57). Barriers:
$$\bar b_{i,t} = +m\,\text{DTS}_{i,t}\,\hat\sigma_{\text{rel},t}\sqrt{h},\qquad \underline b_{i,t} = -m\,\text{DTS}_{i,t}\,\hat\sigma_{\text{rel},t}\sqrt{h},\qquad \text{vertical at } u = h.$$
Label $y_{i,t} = +1$ if $\bar b$ is touched first, $-1$ if $\underline b$, and $\operatorname{sign}(X_{i,t}(h))$ (or 0) at the vertical barrier. Record $t_{1,i} = $ touch time; the label interval is $[t, t_{1,i}]$ and feeds §3.4–3.5. Asymmetry option: $\underline m < \bar m$ because a CCC bond's downside is a jump, so the lower barrier is a trapdoor, not a wall (Ch 5 mixture distributions). *Censoring:* if the bond exits the sample at $t_e < t_{1,i}$ (call, tender, exchange, maturity), close at the exit price and flag `censored=True`; Ben Dor p.338 handles exits by assigning the peer-group return thereafter, which is a defensible imputation for *portfolio* studies but a leak for *labels* (the exit itself is informative: tenders are above market, distressed exchanges below). Error-prone: detecting a touch on a stale mark that then bounces back (the DRR 2026 "bounce-back filter" exists for a reason); using the same month-end price for the feature and the first return (LIB, §3.6).
### 3.3 Meta-labeling and bet sizing
Primary model gives side $\hat y^{(1)}_{i,t}\in\{-1,+1\}$ (e.g. covenant-capacity score, Merton DD, or the analyst). Define the meta-label $y^{(2)}_{i,t} = \mathbf 1\{\hat y^{(1)}_{i,t}\cdot y_{i,t} > 0\}$. The secondary model estimates $p_{i,t} = \Pr(y^{(2)}_{i,t}=1\mid \mathcal F_t)$. Bet size (López de Prado's form): $z = (p - \tfrac12)/\sqrt{p(1-p)}$, $m = 2\Phi(z)-1 \in (-1,1)$, applied as $\text{size} = m\cdot \hat y^{(1)}$. The objective trade-off is precision vs. recall: the primary model is tuned for high recall (never miss a candidate), the secondary for precision (only bet when confident), and the product $F_1$ is what the desk pays for. Error-prone: training the secondary on the primary's *in-sample* hits (leak); letting the secondary see the primary's output as a feature with a non-stationary scale; class imbalance when the primary is right 70% of the time by carry alone, so "always bet" has 70% accuracy — report $F_1$ and the IDO-stripped PnL ("IDO or it didn't happen").
### 3.4 Uniqueness and the issuer-concurrency correction

Average uniqueness of label $i$: $\bar u_i = \frac{1}{t_{1,i}-t_{0,i}+1}\sum_{t=t_{0,i}}^{t_{1,i}} c_t^{-1}$. **Issuer extension:** compute $c^{\text{iss}}_t$ over all bonds of the same ultimate parent, then $\bar u^{\text{iss}}_i$ likewise; final weight $w_i \propto \bar u_i \cdot \bar u^{\text{iss}}_i \cdot \left|\sum_{t} r^{\text{xs}}_{i,t}/c_t\right|$ (return-attribution weighting). # Advisor Brief — Chapter 8: Stripping Beta to Isolate Pure IDO
## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for an ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** A corporate bond's return is a sum of things you did not choose (Treasury carry and rate moves, market-wide spread moves scaled by DTS, sector and rating beta, and the pricing lag of an instrument that trades a few times a week) plus the one thing you are paid for: the idiosyncratic residual, IDO. The whole chapter is a subtraction. Everything before the residual is a hedge-ratio problem, and every hedge ratio in credit is *state-dependent*: it depends on the spread level (DTS), on the sign of the rate–spread correlation (empirical duration), on how stale the marks are (Dimson betas), and on which instruments are actually tradable at 2:58 p.m. (the hedge matrix). The desk motto, "IDO or it didn't happen," is the operational statement of this chapter.
**The aha, in three steps.**
1. *Analytical duration answers a question nobody asked.* $D^{\text{mod}}$ tells you what a bond does if Treasury yields move *and nothing else does*. On a desk, nothing else moving is the one scenario that never occurs. Empirical duration answers the real question: what tends to happen to the bond's price when yields move, given that spreads tend to move too. Ben Dor's formula makes this exact: $D^{\text{emp}} = D\,(1 + s\,\rho_{y,s}\,\sigma_s/\sigma_y)$. For a CCC bond at 900 bp with a rate–spread correlation of $-0.4$, the term in parentheses is near zero or negative. The RSSG instinct that "a stressed name trades on dollar price, not yield" is the same fact seen from the other side: once price is pinned to recovery, $\partial P/\partial r \to 0$ (Ben Dor p.194: "all bonds of a given issuer (at the same seniority level) [may be] marked at the same dollar price, regardless of maturity"). The reader already knows the limit case. The chapter gives them the continuum between par and recovery.
2. *The HY/IG line is a market convention, not a credit fact.* Ben Dor's event study shows a *discontinuity* in rate hedge ratios at the IG/HY boundary that survives controlling for spread level, liquidity, and distressed exclusion (p.195, p.207). The explanation is sociological: IG trades on spread-to-Treasury and is run against excess-return benchmarks; HY trades on price and is run on total return. For an ex-RSSG reader this is liberating: a market's *quoting convention* is a measurable risk factor. (And it is exactly the kind of structural fact a pure-math PhD will not think to look for.)
3. *The hedge you would like and the hedge you can execute are different matrices.* You can't short "the market spread factor." You can short CDX HY, TY/UXY futures, HYG/JNK, or buy SPX/single-name puts. Those instruments are collinear (HYG, JNK and CDX HY are near-substitutes), close at different times (futures settle 3 p.m. Chicago, CDX marks ~5 p.m. New York, bond evaluated prices ~3–4 p.m.), and carry their own basis and roll risk. The minimum-variance hedge is $h^\star = \Sigma_{HH}^{-1}\Sigma_{Hp}$ and $\Sigma_{HH}$ is ill-conditioned, so the honest answer is ridge or a constrained QP, and Paleologo's result that you should *hedge less than the naïve amount* when the hedging instruments carry their own idiosyncratic noise (Paleologo p.196).
**Why it matters more in 2026 than in 2010.** The sign of the rate–spread correlation flipped in 2022 (both legs lost; Bloomberg US Corporate HY TR −11.19% in 2022 — CHECK, secondary source), came back negative in 2024–2025, and sits near zero in 2026 YTD on FRED daily data (COMPUTED, below). Hedge ratios that were "known" in 1998–2009 (Ben Dor Table 7.1: B-rated hedge-ratio limit 0.15, Caa 0.07) are now a time series you must re-estimate every month, and the industry has built products around that problem: CME's credit futures (launched June 17, 2024) include futures on Bloomberg's *duration-hedged* indices, and HYGH (iShares rate-hedged HY) runs at an effective duration of −0.01 years (ishares.com, as of 2026-10-15).

## 3. Derivation skeletons
**3.1 Return decomposition.** Price $P(y)$ with $y = y^{\text{TSY}} + s$. Second-order Taylor:
$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D^{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y^{\text{TSY}} - D^{\text{spr}}\,\Delta s + \tfrac12\,C\,(\Delta y)^2 + y\,\Delta t + \text{roll} .$$
Excess return (index convention): $R^{\text{xs}}_t = R^{\text{tot}}_t - \sum_k w_k R^{\text{TSY}}_{k,t}$ where the $w_k$ match the bond's key-rate durations (Ben Dor p.298; Man 2022 p.14). With DTS: $-D^{\text{spr}}\Delta s = -\text{DTS}\cdot(\Delta s/s)$. Assumptions: small moves, no default within $\Delta t$, option-adjusted $D$ for callables. Measure: this is accounting, measure-free, but the *expected* excess return is a $\mathbb{P}$ object and OAS is a $\mathbb{Q}$-consistent spread; do not call OAS "expected excess return." Error-prone: sign of $-D\Delta y$ (ROBO will add); bp vs decimal ($D \cdot \Delta y$ needs $\Delta y$ in decimal); yield carry vs OAS carry (yield carry includes the Treasury leg, excess-return carry is $s\,\Delta t$); callable HY where "OAS carry" is partly the short call decaying.
**3.2 Empirical duration (Ben Dor eq. 7.1–7.5, rebuilt).** Two-factor model $R_i \approx -D_i\Delta y - D_i s_i \Delta s^{\text{rel}}$, $\Delta s^{\text{rel}} = \Delta s/s$. Projecting $\Delta s^{\text{rel}}$ on $\Delta y$: $\partial \Delta s^{\text{rel}}/\partial \Delta y \approx \rho_{y,s}\sigma_s/\sigma_y$. Hence
$$D^{\text{emp}}_i = -\frac{\partial R_i}{\partial \Delta y} = D_i\Big(1 + s_i\,\rho_{y,s}\,\frac{\sigma_s}{\sigma_y}\Big), \qquad H_i \equiv \frac{D^{\text{emp}}_i}{D_i} = 1 + s_i\,\rho_{y,s}\,\frac{\sigma_s}{\sigma_y}.$$
Estimable form: $R_i = \beta_i D_i \Delta y + \gamma_i D_i s_i \Delta y + \epsilon_i$, $H_i(s) = \beta_i + \gamma_i s_i$ ($\gamma<0$). Units: $s$ decimal, $\sigma_s$ volatility of *relative* spread changes (per period), $\sigma_y$ volatility of yield changes in decimal (same period). Sanity: Ben Dor p.192 Table 7.1(B): $\sigma_s \approx 1.6$–$2.5\%$/day, $\sigma_y = 6.4$ bp/day, $\rho = -0.14$ (Aaa–Aa) to $-0.56$ (B); linkage factor $\rho\sigma_s/\sigma_y$ from $-4.9\%$ to $-19.4\%$ per 1% of spread; theoretical $H$ at average spread 0.95/0.93/0.88/0.24/−0.04/−0.23. Measure: $\rho,\sigma$ are $\mathbb{P}$ sample moments; $D_i$ is a derivative of a $\mathbb{Q}$-consistent price. The gap is comovement, not mispricing. Error-prone: (i) horizon — daily $\rho$ differs from monthly (Dynkin p.436 uses monthly carry-adjusted returns); (ii) extrapolating $H(s)$ beyond the sample spread range (p.196: crisis spreads were outside the fit); (iii) the min-variance hedge $H<1$ *also hedges your spread view* (p.219); (iv) in distress, price is pinned to recovery and $D \to 0$ regardless of $\rho$ (p.194) — the recovery route; (v) price-level/coupon effect (Dynkin Table 10.2: discount bonds +1.67 to falling yields, −1.09 to rising).
**3.3 Stale-price betas.** Observed return $r^{o}_t = \sum_{k=0}^{q}\theta_k r_{t-k}$, $\sum\theta_k = 1$, $\theta_k \ge 0$ (Getmansky–Lo–Makarov smoothing). If $r_t = \alpha + \beta f_t + \epsilon_t$ with i.i.d. $f_t$, then $\mathrm{Cov}(r^o_t, f_t) = \theta_0\beta\sigma_f^2$ so $\hat\beta_{\text{OLS}} \to \theta_0\beta < \beta$, and $\mathrm{Var}(r^o) = \sigma_r^2\sum\theta_k^2 < \sigma_r^2$ (volatility understated; Dynkin p.663). Dimson: regress $r^o_t$ on $f_{t-L},\dots,f_{t+L}$ and sum, $\hat\beta_{\text{D}} = \sum_{k=-L}^{L}\hat\beta_k$; under the MA model and uncorrelated $f$, $\hat\beta_k \to \theta_k\beta$ so the sum recovers $\beta$. Scholes–Williams: $\hat\beta_{\text{SW}} = (\hat\beta_{-1}+\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_{+1})/(1+2\hat\rho_f)$ with $\hat\rho_f$ the first autocorrelation of the factor. De-smoothing: $\hat r_t = (r^o_t - \sum_{k\ge1}\hat\theta_k \hat r_{t-k})/\hat\theta_0$ with $\theta$ from an MA($q$) fit. Volatility correction for monthly data: $\sigma_{\text{ann}} = \sigma_m\sqrt{12 + 2\cdot 11\cdot \text{AR}_1}$ (Dynkin p.129). Measure: $\mathbb{P}$ throughout. Error-prone: (i) lags absorb genuine information diffusion (equity → credit; Dynkin p.606: D10−D1 equity-momentum spread in bond excess returns ≈ +4.0% vs −4.7%/yr, 1993–2017), so an equity Dimson beta is contaminated by a *signal*; (ii) each extra lag adds variance — shrink the lag coefficients toward zero or cap $L$ at 2–3 for daily, 1 for monthly; (iii) evaluated (matrix) prices smooth differently from TRACE last-trade prices — estimate $\theta$ per price source; (iv) overlapping multi-day returns induce MA errors — Newey–West.
**3.4 DTS.** $\sigma(\Delta s_i) \approx \kappa\, s_i$ ⇒ $\sigma(R^{\text{xs}}_i) \approx \text{DTS}_i\,\kappa$, $\kappa \approx 8.8\%$/month IG (p.51), $\approx 9.8\%$ agencies (p.57); floor of ~3 bp/month "structural" vol at very low spreads (p.57). Error-prone: DTS uses *spread* duration, not Treasury duration; negative-DTS artefacts for negative-OAS bonds; the slope $\kappa$ is regime-dependent (crisis chapters).

# Advisor Brief — Chapter 9: Factor Investing in Corporate Credit
## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for an ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** A credit factor is a *rule for sorting bonds* that, after you strip out the one thing every sort secretly loads on (spread risk, measured as DTS), still earns money net of a transaction cost that is five to twenty times larger than in equities. Everything in the chapter is a consequence of three facts that have no equity analogue:
1. **Spread is both the signal and the risk.** In equities, a value sort is not a beta sort. In credit, every sort on anything spread-adjacent (value, carry, "cheapness") is mechanically a sort on DTS, and DTS *is* beta: under Ben Dor's proportional-spread law, $\beta_i \approx \text{DTS}_i/\text{DTS}_m$. Market-value-neutral long-short credit factors are therefore long-beta portfolios wearing a hedge-fund costume. Dynkin's Table 19.4 shows the costume coming off: ESP's correlation with the IG index falls from +44% to −22% once you DTS-match the legs (Dynkin p.741).
2. **Costs are first-order, not a footnote.** Barclays' top-decile equity-momentum portfolio made 6.34%/yr over the index with IR 1.90 *before* costs and was *negative after* costs at 73%/month turnover (Dynkin p.633). Factor investing in credit is a turnover-budgeting problem with a signal attached.
3. **The data lie, and they lie asymmetrically.** TRACE prices carry measurement error that sits in both the sorting signal and the return denominator; filtering returns after seeing them embeds look-ahead. Those two mechanisms inflated the published literature so badly that a JFE paper was retracted (Bai–Bali–Wen 2019, retraction notice JFE 150(3), 2023), only ~23% of literature factors survive Dick-Nielsen–Feldhütter–Pedersen–Stolborg's clean framework (QuantPedia summary, VERIFIED), and the April 2026 Dickerson–Robotti–Rossetti paper finds most of 108 signals have no bond-CAPM alpha after correction (arXiv 2604.07880, VERIFIED).
**The RSSG "aha."** The reader has spent years deciding whether *one* bond is cheap given its covenant package, collateral, and the sponsor's appetite for an uptier. A credit factor model is that same judgment run on 10,000 CUSIPs with the covenant read replaced by three ratios (leverage, net debt/EBITDA, interest coverage) inside a peer group (rating × sector × maturity). The aha is twofold: (a) the ESP regression residual is literally "the spread the market is paying you that the fundamentals don't explain," which is the RSSG analyst's day job expressed as $\epsilon_i$; and (b) the factor zoo's biggest documented alpha-killer is not competition but *bad prices*, which the reader already knows from marking a B3 loan to a stale dealer run. Man's remark that value is "particularly CUSIP-specific" (Man 2022 p.25) is the invitation: within a capital structure, after controlling for subordination and duration, residual mispricing remains, and the person who can read *why* (a guarantor gap, a springing lien, an LMT-blocker) has a feature nobody else's regression contains. Chapter 13's Priming Risk Score is the obvious extra regressor; set that hook here.
**The trend spine.** Three dated arcs run through the chapter: the 2019–2026 replication crisis (a trend in *epistemics*); the collapse of transaction costs from Chen–Lesmond–Wei's 25bp/180bp world to LCS of ~0.6% round-trip (a trend in *implementability*, which is why systematic credit is $90–140bn and growing); and the regime arc from 2020's spread spike through 2022's rates shock to Sept-2025 IG OAS at ~72bp, the tightest since 1998 (SSGA, VERIFIED), which compresses the value dispersion every factor feeds on.

## 3. Derivation skeletons
All expectations state their measure. Factor construction is under $\mathbb{P}$ (we forecast realized excess returns); only the structural fair-spread leg of value touches $\mathbb{Q}$.
### 3.1 DTS and the hidden beta (§9.2)
Excess return over duration-matched Treasuries, one period, bond $i$:
$$
r^{xs}_{i,t+1} \;\approx\; \underbrace{s_{i,t}\,\Delta t}_{\text{carry}} \;-\; D^{\text{spr}}_{i,t}\,\Delta s_{i,t+1} \;+\; \text{(convexity, default jump)}.
$$
Ben Dor's empirical law: spread changes are proportional to spread level, $\Delta s_i = s_i\,\eta_i$ with $\eta_i$ the *relative* spread change. Then
$$
r^{xs}_{i} \approx s_i\Delta t - \text{DTS}_i\,\eta_i,\qquad \text{DTS}_i \equiv D^{\text{spr}}_i\, s_i,\qquad
\sigma(r^{xs}_i)\approx \text{DTS}_i\,\sigma_\eta .
$$
(Ben Dor p.34 eq. 1.4; p.51: regression of excess-return vol on DTS has 98% fit and slope 8.8%/month in that sample.) Decompose $\eta_i = \eta_{m} + \tilde\eta_i$ (common relative move plus idiosyncratic). Market excess return $r_m \approx -\text{DTS}_m\,\eta_m$ (carry aside). Therefore
$$
\beta_i \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(r^{xs}_i, r_m)}{\operatorname{Var}(r_m)} \;=\; \frac{\text{DTS}_i}{\text{DTS}_m}.
$$
**Consequence for a factor sort.** A long-short portfolio with legs $L,S$ and market-value weights has $\beta_{LS} = (\text{DTS}_L - \text{DTS}_S)/\text{DTS}_m$. Any sort correlated with spread (value, carry, "cheap vs. rich") has $\text{DTS}_L > \text{DTS}_S$, hence positive beta: the "alpha" is partly carry on borrowed beta. **DTS-neutral construction:** scale leg $k$'s returns by $\text{DTS}^\ast/\text{DTS}_{k,t}$ with a common target (Barclays uses 12, the IG index's time-average DTS in years × %) (Dynkin p.739). Equivalent weight statement: choose $w$ with $\sum_{i\in L} w_i\text{DTS}_i = \sum_{i\in S} w_i\text{DTS}_i$, ideally per rating × sector cell.
**Error-prone spots.** (i) Units: DTS in years × percent vs years × bp differs by 100; say which. (ii) Using OAS duration vs. modified duration on callables. (iii) DTS-matching on *beginning-of-month* DTS only; re-scaling with end-of-month DTS is look-ahead. (iv) In HY the proportionality weakens near distress because the default jump is not a relative spread change: cap DTS-based scaling for $s > $ ~1,000bp or treat distressed names separately (Ch 5/14). (v) Within-issuer: Man p.13–14 shows bonds of one issuer diverge massively; DTS-neutral at issuer level is not DTS-neutral at bond level.
### 3.2 Value (§9.3)
**ESP (Barclays).** For peer group $g$ = rating × sector × maturity bucket, run monthly
$$
s_{i,t} = a_{g(i),t} + b_{1,t}\,\text{Lev}_{i,t} + b_{2,t}\,\frac{\text{ND}}{\text{EBITDA}}_{i,t} + b_{3,t}\,\text{ICov}_{i,t} + \epsilon_{i,t},
\qquad \text{ESP}_{i,t} = \operatorname{rank}_{\text{index}}(\hat\epsilon_{i,t})\in\{1,\dots,10\}.
$$
(Dynkin p.692–693 gives exactly these three ratios; financials use LT debt / LT assets only.) High residual = cheap. Dynkin p.528 decile table with leverage: D1 −1.10%/yr (IR −0.27), D10 +3.29%/yr (IR 0.53); L/S IR > 1.5 (p.545).
**SPiDER (Barclays).** Model-free, aggregable across time and sectors:
$$
\text{SPiDER}_{i,t} = \frac{s_{i,t}}{\text{Debt}_{i,t}/\overline{\text{EBITDA}}^{\text{LT}}_{i,t}} .
$$
Man's raw value signal is the same idea with a floor: `OAS / (Net Debt/EBITDA).clip(lower=0.1)` (Man p.24), then risk-adjust (size, PD, vol) and compare within rating/sector.
**Structural fair spread (Correia–Richardson–Tuna 2012; CHECK details).** From a Merton/KMV physical PD, map to $\mathbb{Q}$ and to spread:
$$
\text{PD}^{\mathbb{Q}}_T = N\!\left(N^{-1}(\text{PD}^{\mathbb{P}}_T) + \lambda_{\text{SR}}\sqrt{T}\right),\qquad
s^{\text{fair}}_i \approx -\frac{1}{T}\ln\!\left(1-\text{PD}^{\mathbb{Q}}_T\,L\right)\approx \lambda^{\mathbb{Q}} L,
$$
value $= s_i - s^{\text{fair}}_i$. **Measure trap:** if you skip the $\mathbb{P}\to\mathbb{Q}$ step you call every bond cheap (physical PDs are too small). A cross-sectional *rank* is unaffected by a monotone mapping, but any *level* comparison (e.g., "spread per unit of expected loss") is not. Dynkin p.553 states the trade-off plainly: regression fair spreads overfit and have unstable coefficients; structural ones are parameter-hungry and opaque; SPiDER is neither.
**Error-prone spots.** Negative/tiny EBITDA (hence the clip); book vs market leverage; point-in-time fundamentals (lag ≥ 3 months, no restatements); OAS vs Z-spread on callable HY (use OAS or you "find" value in every make-whole); peer cells with < ~15 bonds → pool or shrink $b$ toward the global estimate; winsorize residuals at 1/99 before ranking; and the biggest: residual = cheapness *or* information you lack (an LMT, a guarantee gap). That is the RSSG edge and the Ch 13 hook.
### 3.3 Momentum (§9.4)

# Advisor Brief — Chapter 10: Trend-Following & Time-Series Momentum in Credit (TREND)
## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for the ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** Trend-following in credit is not a different religion from the RSSG seat; it is the stop-loss the reader has been running by hand for years, written down, sized by DTS, and executed in the one credit instrument that actually trades like a future: the on-the-run CDX/iTraxx index. Everything that makes trend hard in credit (negative skew of long credit, spread-level-dependent volatility, stale cash prices, jumps from LMTs and defaults, a V-shaped Fed put) is a direct consequence of credit's payoff shape, and every one of those consequences is derivable.
**Three "aha" moments to build the chapter around:**
1. **Volatility scaling in credit *is* DTS scaling.** Ben Dor et al. show spread volatility is linear in spread level with a zero intercept (`Ben_Dor p.74`). Therefore $\sigma(r^{ex}) \approx D^{\text{spr}} \cdot \beta s = \beta \cdot \text{DTS}$, and the Moskowitz–Ooi–Pedersen position $w_t = \sigma_{\text{tgt}}/\sigma_t$ becomes $w_t \propto 1/\text{DTS}_t$. A credit trend follower automatically de-risks as spreads widen and re-risks as they tighten. That is the same mechanical behavior as a rating-based or DTS-based stop-loss, which Ben Dor warns "does well in trending markets and poorly in choppy markets" and can feed "a vicious cycle" (`Ben_Dor p.292`). The reader's old job was to be on the other side of that cycle (buying fallen angels from forced sellers, `Dynkin` Chs 2–3). Now they are specifying the seller. Kim–Tse–Wald's critique that much of TSMOM's alpha comes from vol scaling rather than the sign (Quantpedia summary, VERIFIED) lands with special force in credit.
2. **Carry and trend disagree exactly when it matters.** Under a spread process with both mean reversion and short-run inertia, $\Delta s_{t+1} = \kappa(\bar s - s_t) + \phi\,\Delta s_t + \epsilon_{t+1}$, expected excess return is $s_t\Delta t - D^{\text{spr}}[\kappa(\bar s - s_t) + \phi\Delta s_t]$. The carry/value term ($s_t$, $\kappa$) says buy after widening; the trend term ($\phi$) says sell. 2008 and 2022 were $\phi$-years; March 2020 and April 2025 were $\kappa$-weeks. The chapter's job is to let the reader see both coefficients and size them, not to pick a tribe. Man 2022 says it plainly: carry "can have fantastic years and also very poor years" (`Man_Quantitative p.26`).
3. **Equity leads credit, so the best credit trend signal is often not a credit price.** Hilscher–Pollet–Wilson (JFQA 2015, VERIFIED) find equity returns lead CDS at daily and weekly horizons, not the reverse; Dynkin's Equity Momentum in Credit (EMC) earns 6.34%/yr (IR 1.56) in IG and 15.9%/yr in HY before costs, with an 83% chance the top-decile signal decays within three months (`Dynkin pp.607, 610, 628`). The RSSG reader already watches the equity for the companies whose debt they trade; this chapter turns that reflex into a specification.
**The PJT bridge in one line:** a restructuring banker knows that a bond at 60 "has momentum" toward a process, not toward 40 or 80. Trend-following formalizes the first half of that sentence and is silent on the second; the chapter must show where the jump (the LMT, the filing, the DIP) breaks the diffusion assumption and how to specify a trend engine that knows it.

## 3. Derivation skeletons
All signal construction and performance statistics are under $\mathbb{P}$. The only $\mathbb{Q}$ object is the CDS risky annuity used to convert spread changes to P&L; say so explicitly in the chapter.
### D1. TSMOM signal and position (Moskowitz–Ooi–Pedersen 2012, adapted)
Lookback-$h$ sign signal on instrument $i$ with excess return $r^{ex}_{i,t}$:
$$
\text{sig}^{(h)}_{i,t} = \operatorname{sign}\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{h-1} r^{ex}_{i,t-k}\Big), \qquad
w_{i,t} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{tgt}}}{\hat\sigma_{i,t}}\,\text{sig}^{(h)}_{i,t}, \qquad
r^{\text{TSMOM}}_{t+1} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{i,t}\, r^{ex}_{i,t+1}.
$$
$\hat\sigma_{i,t}$ is an exponentially weighted annualized volatility; MOP use a 60-day center of mass and $\sigma_{\text{tgt}}=40\%$ per instrument (state as their choice, not a law). Smoother alternative: EWMA crossover $\text{sig}_t = \tanh\!\big((\text{EWMA}_{f}(P_t)-\text{EWMA}_{s}(P_t))/(\hat\sigma_t\sqrt{h})\big)$, with SG's 20d/120d crossover as the industry's "Trend Indicator" benchmark (SG note, May 2025, VERIFIED).
**Credit excess return.** For a funded long-risk position in an index CDS with running coupon $c$ (100 bp IG, 500 bp HY by convention; CHECK current contract terms before stating), par spread $s_t$ and risky annuity $A_t$ (RPV01, a $\mathbb{Q}$ quantity from the ISDA standard model):
$$
r^{ex}_{t+1} \;\approx\; \underbrace{s_t\,\Delta t}_{\text{carry}} \;-\; \underbrace{A_t\,(s_{t+1}-s_t)}_{\text{mark-to-market}} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\,\Gamma_t (s_{t+1}-s_t)^2}_{\text{convexity}},
$$
and for a cash index, $r^{ex}_{t+1} \approx s_t\Delta t - D^{\text{spr}}_t\,\Delta s_{t+1}$ over duration-matched Treasuries (Dynkin's data definition, `p.605`). **Error-prone spots:** (i) the sign of the mark-to-market term; (ii) CDX HY is price-quoted, so build the return from price plus accrued coupon, not from a converted spread; (iii) upfront vs running conventions on the roll; (iv) splicing on-the-run series at the semiannual roll (roll notional is 25–50% of a quarter's volume per ISDA footnote 8) creates artificial returns if you difference across series; (v) ACT/360 accrual for $\Delta t$; (vi) do not feed index OAS levels into a sign rule without converting to returns, because a 12-month *level* comparison and a 12-month *return* sum differ by carry, which has the opposite sign of the spread move.
### D2. Volatility scaling in credit is DTS scaling
Ben Dor's spread-volatility law: $\sigma(\Delta s_i) = \alpha + \beta\, s_i$ with $\alpha \approx 0$ (`Ben_Dor p.74`). With $\sigma(r^{ex}_i) \approx D^{\text{spr}}_i\,\sigma(\Delta s_i)$:
$$
\sigma(r^{ex}_{i,t}) \approx \beta\, D^{\text{spr}}_{i,t}\, s_{i,t} = \beta\,\text{DTS}_{i,t}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
w_{i,t} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{tgt}}}{\beta\,\text{DTS}_{i,t}}\,\text{sig}_{i,t}.
$$
Consequences to derive and state: position in spread-DV01 terms is constant *per unit of spread*, so dollar notional shrinks as spreads widen; realized trend P&L decomposes into a "sign" component and a "vol-timing" component (Kim–Tse–Wald: 1.27% vs 0.41% monthly alpha with and without scaling on 55 futures, 1985–2013, Quantpedia summary). Assumptions: $\beta$ stable within rating bucket; Ben Dor's $\gamma$ nonlinearity at low spreads (vol roughly constant below ~20 bp) breaks the proportionality for tight IG. **Measure:** $\mathbb{P}$. **Ways This Blows Up:** vol estimated from stale cash prices is biased low (then the position is too big); Paleologo's dispersion result (`p.98`) shows vol scaling is most dangerous when the universe's volatilities are heterogeneous, which is exactly a cross-rating credit book.
### D3. Trend plus carry under an OU-with-inertia spread process
Model the (log or level) spread as
$$
\Delta s_{t+1} = \kappa(\bar s - s_t) + \phi\,\Delta s_t + \epsilon_{t+1}, \qquad \epsilon_{t+1}\sim(0,\,\beta^2 s_t^2\Delta t)\ \text{under }\mathbb{P}.
$$
Then
$$
\mathbb{E}_t[r^{ex}_{t+1}] = s_t\Delta t - D^{\text{spr}}\big[\kappa(\bar s - s_t) + \phi\,\Delta s_t\big].
$$
Carry ($s_t\Delta t$) and value ($-D\kappa(\bar s - s_t)$, positive when $s_t > \bar s$) favor buying wide spreads; trend ($-D\phi\Delta s_t$) favors selling after widening when $\phi>0$. The optimal linear combination weights each predictor by its coefficient over its forecast variance; show that with $\phi$ small and $\kappa$ small, carry dominates at monthly horizon, and with $\phi$ large (2008, 2022) trend dominates. Koijen et al. (JFE 2018, VERIFIED) define carry as the return if prices do not change and find it predicts returns in credit among other classes; Baz et al. (Man AHL 2015, Quantpedia summary) find time-series momentum and carry are complementary and best combined. **Error-prone:** estimating $\kappa$ and $\phi$ jointly on monthly OAS with a 25-year sample gives standard errors as large as the coefficients; the book should say so and show the confidence region rather than a point estimate. Also, Nuveen's house assumption that "spreads tend to mean revert" (`Nuveen p.4`) is a prior on $\kappa$, not evidence against $\phi$.
### D4. Equity → credit lead–lag
Predictive regression at issuer or index level:
$$
r^{\text{cr}}_{t+1} = a + b\, r^{\text{eq}}_{t} + c\, r^{\text{cr}}_{t} + \varepsilon_{t+1},
$$
with Granger tests in a VAR(1). Dynkin's EMC signal: equal-weighted 1-, 3- and 6-month equity returns "rescaled for different lengths of the respective formation periods" (`p.606`, footnote 9; the exact rescaling is not on the page, CHECK the appendix; propose dividing each horizon's return by $\sqrt{h}$ as the vol-neutral default). Sector translation: $\approx$ 5–8 bp/month of excess return per 1%/month of equity momentum (`Dynkin p.644`). **Error-prone:** stale bond prices manufacture a spurious lead (Dynkin tests this by sorting on Trade Efficiency Scores, `p.621`, and by lagging the signal 1–3 days, `p.630`); non-synchronous closes between US equity and European CDS; the credit exposure of a momentum long–short flips sign across the cycle (`p.613`), so a "lead–lag alpha" that is not beta-neutralized is mostly timing. # Advisor Brief: Chapter 11, Defensive Systematic High Yield
## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for an ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** High yield is the asset class where the payoff is a put you sold: upside capped near par plus carry, downside a jump to recovery. Every HY manager knows this, and the industry's answer for twenty years has been to run a beta of 0.9 and call it "defense." BlackRock's eVestment study (2005–2025) shows the average manager captured 89.56% of down months and 92.24% of up months: that is not skill, it is a dimmer switch (BlackRock p.3). The systematic answer is to *buy the asymmetry back at the issuer level*: rank thousands of issuers daily by a probability of default built from balance sheets, equity and bond prices, exclude the worst tail, tilt toward low-risk (short, higher-rated) bonds on a DTS-neutral basis, and keep the beta at 1.0 so the rebound is not forfeited. The chapter's math is the arithmetic of capture ratios, the conversion of distance-to-default into a calibrated PD, the hazard-model evolution from Altman (1968) to gradient boosting, the power-curve validation of a "77% of defaults identified within 24 months" claim, and the capacity economics that decide whether any of it scales.
**The aha for the RSSG reader.** You already *are* a default-prediction model. The 13-week cash flow, the liquidity runway, the springing covenant math, the "will the sponsor fund the equity cure" judgment: those are the covariates. What you lacked was *breadth*: you covered 8 names a year with a 300-page memo each; the systematic desk covers 2,000 issuers every night with a 20-line hazard function. The surprising part is which covariates win. Shumway (2001) and Campbell, Hilscher and Szilagyi (2008) found that market variables (excess equity return, idiosyncratic equity volatility, relative size) dominate the accounting ratios RSSG lives by, and Alanis, Chava and Shah (2022) find the same with modern ML: once distance-to-default, accounting and market variables are in, "textual features derived from corporate filings do not improve performance materially" (arXiv:2212.12051). Your edge is not the memo; it is knowing (a) which *label* counts as default in the LMT era (a distressed exchange is a default that files its own press release as "liability management"), (b) which structural features (priming capacity, uptier risk, drop-down baskets) are missing from every vendor PD, and (c) when the model's "avoid" signal is really a CLO CCC-bucket forced seller you should be *buying from*.
**The second aha: defense that is not a beta tilt.** Downside capture below 100% with upside capture at 100% requires *convexity* in the active return, which can only come from security selection (avoid the jump) or regime-aware exposure (add risk when spreads overshoot fundamentals). A beta tilt gives you symmetric capture by construction; that is the Achilles' heel BlackRock diagnoses in both average and top-quartile managers (p.2, p.5).

## 3. Derivation skeletons
### 3.1 The mixture return and its skew ($\mathbb P$)
One-period excess return of a bond with carry $c$, spread duration $D^{\text{spr}}$, spread change $\Delta s$, default indicator $\mathbb 1_{\tau\le h}$ with $\mathbb P$-probability $p$, price $P_0$ and recovery $R$:
$$r = (1-\mathbb 1_{\tau\le h})\,(c - D^{\text{spr}}\Delta s) + \mathbb 1_{\tau\le h}\,\frac{R-P_0}{P_0}.$$
With $L_P \equiv (P_0-R)/P_0$ and the surviving component having mean $m$ and variance $v$:
$$\mathbb E[r] = (1-p)m - pL_P,\qquad \text{Var}[r] = (1-p)v + p(1-p)(m+L_P)^2,$$
$$\text{Skew of the jump component} = -\frac{1-2p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}\;\;(\approx -4.1 \text{ at } p=0.05,\; -2.1 \text{ at } p=0.20).$$
The credit-triangle approximation $\mathbb E[r^{xs}] \approx s - \lambda^{\mathbb P}L$ follows for small $p$ with $c\approx s$. **Error-prone spots:** mixing $\lambda^{\mathbb Q}$ (from CDS) into a $\mathbb P$ expected-return formula; using the 40% CDS recovery convention as a $\mathbb P$ recovery (Moody's recession mean 27.85%, Schmid p.103); forgetting that $L_P$ is measured from *price*, not par, so a 60-dollar CCC bond has far less jump loss than its spread implies.
### 3.2 Capture ratios and the beta-tilt identity
Up months $\mathcal U=\{t: r_{b,t}>0\}$, down months $\mathcal D$. Arithmetic capture (eVestment uses geometric annualized means; the algebra is the same to first order):
$$U = \frac{\bar r_p^{\,\mathcal U}}{\bar r_b^{\,\mathcal U}},\qquad D = \frac{\bar r_p^{\,\mathcal D}}{\bar r_b^{\,\mathcal D}}.$$
If $r_{p,t} = \alpha + \beta r_{b,t} + \epsilon_t$ with $\epsilon$ orthogonal to the regime, then
$$U \approx \beta + \frac{\alpha}{\bar r_b^{\,\mathcal U}},\qquad D \approx \beta + \frac{\alpha}{\bar r_b^{\,\mathcal D}},\qquad U - D \approx \alpha\left(\frac{1}{\bar r_b^{\,\mathcal U}} - \frac{1}{\bar r_b^{\,\mathcal D}}\right) > 0 \text{ iff } \alpha>0.$$
A pure beta tilt ($\alpha=0$) gives $U=D=\beta<1$: downside "protection" paid for one-for-one with upside. BlackRock's average manager ($U=92.24\%$, $D=89.56\%$) is consistent with $\beta\approx 0.9$ and a small positive $\alpha$ *(illustrative decomposition; the paper reports only the capture ratios)*. Convex defense is the asymmetric-beta model
$$r_{p,t} = \alpha + \beta^+ \max(r_{b,t},0) + \beta^-\min(r_{b,t},0) + \epsilon_t,$$
where "good defense" means $\beta^- < \beta^+$ (positive convexity, Henriksson–Merton flavour). **Error-prone spots:** computing capture on total returns when the mandate is excess-return; regime defined on the benchmark, never on the portfolio; small-sample $\bar r_b^{\mathcal D}$ near zero blows up $D$ (require $|\bar r_b^{\mathcal D}|$ above a floor). Measure: all $\mathbb P$, realized.
### 3.3 Distance-to-default ($\mathbb P$ for PD, $\mathbb Q$ for pricing)
Merton equity as a call on assets with face $F$ at $T$:
$$E = V N(d_1) - F e^{-rT} N(d_2),\qquad d_{1,2} = \frac{\ln(V/F) + (r \pm \tfrac12\sigma_V^2)T}{\sigma_V\sqrt T},$$
$$\sigma_E E = N(d_1)\,\sigma_V V \quad(\text{Itô: } dE = \partial_V E\, dV + \dots).$$
Solve the two equations for $(V,\sigma_V)$ by fixed-point iteration (or the KMV time-series iteration). Physical distance-to-default uses the *physical* drift $\mu$:
$$\text{DD}_T = \frac{\ln(V/F) + (\mu - \tfrac12\sigma_V^2)T}{\sigma_V\sqrt T},\qquad \text{PD}^{\mathbb P} = g(\text{DD}) \neq N(-\text{DD}),$$
with $g$ an empirical monotone map (Bohn–Stein p.161–163: median default frequency per half-sigma DD cohort, fit with a Weibull or polynomial, cap PD near 20–40% for DD < 0.5, need ≥400 and ideally ≥1,000 defaults). Default point for a coupon-paying firm: $F = \text{STD} + \tfrac12\text{LTD}$ (Vasicek–Kealhofer convention). **Error-prone spots:** $N(-\text{DD})$ gives PD $\approx 0$ for most public firms because most have DD > 4 (Bohn–Stein p.138: "a DD of 4 converts into an EDF of almost 1 percent" under the empirical map); unannualized $\sigma_E$; equity vol used as asset vol; sponsor-owned HY issuers have no $E$ (fall back to bond-implied or accounting-only PD and *flag the model regime in the feature set*); an uptier or drop-down changes $F$ for the bond you hold without changing firm-level DD, which is exactly the Ch 13 Priming Risk Score's job.
### 3.4 Dynamic logit hazard, calibration and clustering ($\mathbb P$)
Firm-month panel, covariates lagged by filing availability:
$$h_i(t\mid x_{i,t-\ell}) = \frac{1}{1+\exp\!\big(-(\alpha_0 + \beta' x_{i,t-\ell} + \gamma \ln t)\big)},$$
estimated as a logit on firm-months (Shumway's equivalence to a discrete-time hazard, Bohn–Stein p.237). CHS-style covariates: $\text{NIMTAAVG}$, $\text{TLMTA}$, $\text{EXRETAVG}$, $\text{SIGMA}$, $\text{RSIZE}$, $\text{CASHMTA}$, $\text{MB}$, $\text{PRICE}$ (Campbell, Hilscher and Szilagyi 2008). Add the RSSG covariate: liquidity runway in weeks (Theo's 13-week model reborn). Prior-shift recalibration from sample default rate $\pi_s$ to population $\pi$:
$$\text{logit}\,\hat p^{\text{pop}} = \text{logit}\,\hat p^{\text{s}} + \ln\frac{\pi(1-\pi_s)}{\pi_s(1-\pi)}.$$
Test statistics: divide logit chi-squares by average firm-months per firm (Shumway's adjustment, Bohn–Stein p.237), or cluster by issuer. **Error-prone spots:** paired samples (Bohn–Stein p.226); accounting data dated at fiscal period end instead of filing date (look-ahead); treating a distressed exchange or LME as survival (Moody's: ~65% of 2025 defaults were distressed exchanges, so the "no" label is contaminated); 47 bonds per issuer (one issuer is one observation); overlapping 12-month horizons inflate significance; calibrating on 2010–2019 (benign) and deploying into 2026.

# Advisor Brief — Chapter 12: Multi-Asset Credit Allocation (Nuveen framework)
## 1. Thesis and the "aha"
**Thesis.** Multi-asset credit (MAC) is not "buy the sector with the biggest yield." It is a three-layer engineering problem: (1) a *return forecast* that is an accounting identity, not an opinion: yield minus expected credit loss minus rate mark-to-market minus spread mark-to-market (Nuveen p.3); (2) a *covariance* that is not stationary: one common spread factor explains most of everything, and in stress the sector correlations go to one while the private sleeve's reported correlation stays politely near zero because nobody marked it; (3) a *liquidity-tier constraint* that decides which rotations are real. Nuveen's efficient portfolios (p.6) differ by 110 bp of forward return across the whole frontier (5.11% to 6.20%) while the forward Sharpe ratios run 0.26 to 0.40; the allocation decision moves less than the error bars on the inputs. The chapter's job is to make the inputs honest, make the regime explicit, and make the constraints binding.
**The aha for an ex-RSSG reader.** Three things the restructuring seat already knows become the alpha here:
- *Most MAC "alpha" is credit beta.* AQR's regression of 445 Core/Core-Plus, 44 Global Aggregate and 114 Global Unconstrained managers (Nov 1997–Jun 2018) finds R² of 90%, 61% and 77% against traditional premia, credit-loading t-stat of 14.9 for US Aggregate managers, and residual alphas of 0.10%, 0.01% and 0.09% a year, none significant (AQR Alternative Thinking 4Q18, Exhibit 3; published as Brooks, Gould & Richardson, *Journal of Fixed Income* 29(4), 2020). "IDO or it didn't happen" applies to sector rotation too: a sector tilt is alpha only after you strip the DTS beta it carries.
- *Sectors are not asset classes; they are points in (seniority, recovery, liquidity, duration) space.* The RSSG reader prices a first-lien loan, a HY bond, an EM sovereign, a CLO BBB and a direct loan by where each sits in a waterfall and what it recovers. The Fed's own numbers make the point: post-default value of about 33% for direct loans versus 52% for syndicated loans and 39% for HY bonds (Cai & Haque, FEDS Notes, Feb 23 2024). "Expected credit loss" in the Nuveen identity is therefore *the restructuring banker's variable*, and it is the one most MAC shops take from a rating-agency table.
- *Private credit volatility is a reporting artifact until you desmooth it.* Nuveen proxies middle-market direct lending with the S&P UBS Leveraged Loan B index (p.9) because the sleeve has no honest return series. Feed appraisal-smoothed returns into an optimizer and it does what ROBO does with "minimize drawdown": piles into the thing that never appears to move. Getmansky–Lo–Makarov (JFE 2004) is the correction; the May 2026 FSR box on BDC redemptions is the consequence.

## 3. Derivation skeletons
**(a) The forward-return identity (Nuveen p.3).** Let $P(y)$ be the price of a sector proxy with yield-to-worst $y = y^{\text{TSY}} + s$. A one-period expansion gives
$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[r] \;\approx\; y \;-\; \underbrace{\text{PD}^{\mathbb{P}}\,(1-R)}_{\text{expected credit loss}} \;-\; D^{\text{mod}}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\Delta y^{\text{TSY}}] \;-\; D^{\text{spr}}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[\Delta s] \;+\; \tfrac12 C\,\mathbb{E}[(\Delta y)^2] \tag{12.1}
$$
with the convexity term dropped by Nuveen. Measure: $y$ and $s$ are market quantities (they embed $\mathbb{Q}$ compensation); the loss term converts to a physical expectation only if $\text{PD}^{\mathbb{P}}$ and $R^{\mathbb{P}}$ are physical (agency or structural-model, not CDS-implied). Error-prone spots: (i) mixing CDS-implied PD (a $\mathbb{Q}$ object) into the loss term double-counts the risk premium; (ii) floating-rate loans have $D^{\text{mod}} \approx 0.25$ but $D^{\text{spr}} \approx 4$, so the two MTM terms must use different durations; (iii) yield-to-worst for callable HY and for preferreds already prices the call, so the spread MTM must use option-adjusted spread duration; (iv) for CLO tranches the expected loss is a waterfall output, not a rating-table lookup. **Nuveen Figure 3 (HY, 5-year, 19 Nov 2025):** the five printed numbers are 6.97, 2.55, 0.37, 0.49, 3.56 and satisfy $6.97 - 2.55 - 0.37 - 0.49 = 3.56$; the text extraction leaves the assignment of 2.55 and 0.49 between "expected credit loss" and "Δspread MTM" ambiguous. The economically consistent reading is yield 6.97%, expected credit loss 2.55%/yr, Δrates MTM 0.37%, Δspread MTM 0.49%, forward return 3.56% (CHECK against the chart in the PDF; state the ambiguity rather than guess).
**(b) Spread mean reversion and the transition matrix (Nuveen p.4).** Model log-spread or spread as Ornstein–Uhlenbeck, $ds_t = \kappa(\theta - s_t)\,dt + \sigma s_t^{\beta}\,dW_t$. Then $\mathbb{E}[s_h \mid s_0] = \theta + (s_0 - \theta)e^{-\kappa h}$, half-life $\ln 2/\kappa$, and the z-score $z = (s_0 - \theta)/\sigma_s$ is a sufficient statistic only if $\theta$ is constant. Nuveen's "credit spread transition probability" is the discretized version: bucket spreads, estimate $P_{ij} = \Pr(s_{t+1}\in j \mid s_t \in i)$, and read the $h$-step distribution off $P^h$. The structural-break warning (HY index now 51% BB; CMBS office reset) means $\theta$ is regime- and composition-dependent: estimate it on a composition-adjusted spread (reweight to a fixed rating mix) before computing $z$.
**(c) DTS as the cross-sector risk unit (Ben Dor p.50).** Excess return $r^{x}_i \approx -D^{\text{spr}}_i\,\Delta s_i = -\text{DTS}_i\,(\Delta s_i/s_i)$, so $\sigma(r^x_i) \approx \text{DTS}_i\,\sigma(\Delta s_i/s_i)$ and the sector covariance is $\Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(\text{DTS})\,\Omega\,\mathrm{diag}(\text{DTS})$ with $\Omega$ the covariance of *relative* spread changes, which is far more stable across regimes than the covariance of absolute changes. EWMA (Nuveen's "weighted covariance"): $\Omega_t = \lambda\Omega_{t-1} + (1-\lambda)\,u_t u_t^{\top}$, $u_t = \Delta s_t / s_{t-1}$, with effective window $1/(1-\lambda)$. Shrink toward a one-factor target (Ledoit–Wolf 2004): $\hat\Omega = \delta F + (1-\delta)\Omega$.
**(d) Desmoothing the private sleeve (Getmansky–Lo–Makarov 2004).** Reported returns $r^o_t = \sum_{j=0}^{k}\theta_j r_{t-j}$, $\sum_j\theta_j = 1$, $\theta_j \ge 0$. Then $\sigma^2(r^o) = \sigma^2(r)\sum_j\theta_j^2$ and $\beta^o = \beta \sum_j \theta_j^2$-scaled in the same way for contemporaneous betas, so both volatility and HY beta are understated by the smoothing factor $\xi = \sum_j\theta_j^2 \le 1$. AR(1) shortcut: $\hat r_t = (r^o_t - \phi\, r^o_{t-1})/(1-\phi)$. Error-prone: desmoothing a quarterly series then mixing it with monthly public data without aligning frequencies; applying it to a BDC *price* series (already marked by the market) instead of the NAV series.
**(e) Two-state Gaussian HMM.** Hidden state $z_t\in\{1,2\}$ with transition matrix $P$; observation $r_t \mid z_t = k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \Sigma_k)$ over the sector excess-return vector. Filter: $\xi_{t\mid t} \propto \xi_{t\mid t-1}\odot f_t$, $\xi_{t+1\mid t} = P^{\top}\xi_{t\mid t}$; log-likelihood $\sum_t \ln \mathbf{1}^{\top}(\xi_{t\mid t-1}\odot f_t)$; EM updates via the smoothed $\gamma_t(k)$ and $\xi_t(j,k)$. Expected regime duration $1/(1-P_{kk})$. Regime-mixture capital market assumptions for horizon $h$: $\mu^{(h)} = \sum_k \pi^{(h)}_k \mu_k$, $\Sigma^{(h)} = \sum_k \pi^{(h)}_k \Sigma_k + \sum_k \pi^{(h)}_k(\mu_k-\mu^{(h)})(\mu_k-\mu^{(h)})^{\top}$ with $\pi^{(h)} = (P^{\top})^h\xi_{t\mid t}$. Error-prone: (i) using *smoothed* probabilities $\gamma_t$ in a backtest leaks the future; use filtered $\xi_{t\mid t}$ with a one-period action delay (Nystrup 2014 tested exactly this); (ii) $K>2$ on ~300 monthly observations overfits; (iii) label switching between runs; (iv) fitting on the smoothed private series makes the "calm" state absorb the illiquid sleeve.
**(f) Optimizers.** Mean-variance with Nuveen's box constraints (p.5): $\max_w\; w^{\top}\mu - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}w^{\top}\Sigma w$ s.t. $\mathbf{1}^{\top}w = 1$, $l \le w \le u$, $w^{\top}\mu \ge \mu^{\star}$. Liquidity tiers: $\sum_{i\in\text{illiquid}} w_i \le 0.15$, $\sum_{i\in\text{HL}} w_i \ge \text{HLIM}$, and a days-to-liquidate budget $\sum_i w_i\,\tau_i \le \bar\tau$; tactical tilt budget $\|w - w^{\text{SAA}}\|_1 \le \kappa$ with transaction cost $\sum_i |\Delta w_i|\,\text{LCS}_i/2$. # Advisor Brief — Chapter 14: The Default & Recovery Cycle in the LMT Era (TREND)
## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for an ex-PJT-RSSG reader
**Thesis.** Between roughly 2020 and 2026 the corporate default process changed shape, not just level. Default stopped being one absorbing state ("Chapter 11, see you in 18 months") and became a *multi-state, repeated* process: a distressed exchange or LME, then a re-default clock, then (one time in four) a hard default with a recovery that depends less on your seniority than on *which side of the lender table you sat on*. Moody's puts distressed exchanges at ~63% of all defaults in 2024 and ~65% in 2025, the highest shares on record (VERIFIED, Moody's Asset Management Research, 6 Mar 2025 and 28 Apr 2026). The quant consequence is that the two objects every credit model consumes, the default intensity $\lambda$ and the recovery $R$, each have to be re-specified: $\lambda$ splits into competing cause-specific hazards (soft vs. hard), and $R$ becomes a *mixture over coalition membership* rather than a Beta indexed by seniority. The chapter models the whole thing as a regime shift in the joint law of $(\tau, R)$ and shows how to estimate, simulate, and trade it.
**The aha for the RSSG reader.** You already know the mechanics: Serta, Mitel, Incora, the co-op, the "open market purchase." What you have not been made to do is *integrate over them*. On a systematic desk the question is not "is this uptier legal?" but "what is the cumulative incidence of hard default within 24 months conditional on an LME, and how much of the recovery variance is explained by a single binary variable, `in_majority_group`?" The answer, from Moody's own 1,173-borrower study, is that a quarter of soft credit events end in hard default and 70% of those happen inside two years (VERIFIED, Hamilton, Moody's, 18 May 2026). From Fitch's 2024 emergence sample, first-lien recoveries were 23% for issuers that had done an LME versus 53% for those that had not (CHECK: PitchBook summary of the Fitch report; PitchBook page returned 403). Your RSSG instincts about *who* gets primed are a covariate the PhDs don't have. This chapter turns them into a hazard-rate covariate and a recovery-mixture weight.
**The one-sentence version for the monitor:** "Default rate" is now two numbers, "recovery rate" is now two humps, and the gap between each pair is where the alpha lives.

## 3. Derivation skeletons
All physical-measure ($\mathbb{P}$) unless stated. Time $t$ in years. Recovery $R$ as fraction of par (RFV convention unless stated).
### 3.1 Competing risks: splitting the default intensity
Let $\tau$ be the time of the *first* credit event and $K\in\{H,D\}$ its type (hard default, distressed exchange). Cause-specific hazards:
$$\lambda_k(t)=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{P}(t\le\tau<t+h,\ K=k\mid \tau\ge t)}{h},\qquad \lambda(t)=\lambda_H(t)+\lambda_D(t).$$
Survival $S(t)=\exp\!\big(-\int_0^t\lambda(u)\,du\big)$. Cumulative incidence (sub-distribution) functions:
$$F_k(t)=\mathbb{P}(\tau\le t,\ K=k)=\int_0^t S(u)\,\lambda_k(u)\,du,\qquad F_H(t)+F_D(t)+S(t)=1.$$
With constant hazards:
$$F_D(t)=\frac{\lambda_D}{\lambda_H+\lambda_D}\big(1-e^{-(\lambda_H+\lambda_D)t}\big),\qquad \text{DE share}=\frac{\lambda_D}{\lambda_H+\lambda_D}.$$
A 65% DE share implies $\lambda_D\approx1.86\,\lambda_H$. From Moody's June 2025 numbers (5.8% including DEs, 2.1% excluding), $\lambda\approx-\ln(1-0.058)=0.0597$, split $\lambda_D\approx0.038$, $\lambda_H\approx0.022$ *(approx.; converting rates to hazards)*.
Nonparametric estimation: Nelson–Aalen for each cause, $\hat\Lambda_k(t)=\sum_{t_i\le t} d_{k,i}/n_i$, and the Aalen–Johansen CIF $\hat F_k(t)=\sum_{t_i\le t}\hat S(t_{i}^-)\,d_{k,i}/n_i$.
**Error-prone spots.** (a) Treating $1-\text{KM}$ with the other cause censored as a probability: it overstates $F_k$ because it pretends the competing event never happens. (b) Regressing on covariates: a Cox model on $\lambda_D$ tells you about the *rate* of DEs among survivors, not about the *probability* of eventually having a DE; for the latter use Fine–Gray (1999) on the subdistribution hazard. (c) Agency default rates are issuer-count rates over trailing 12 months, not hazards; convert before mixing with CDS-implied intensities. (d) Dollar-weighted vs. count-weighted DE shares differ materially; state which.
### 3.2 The re-default clock: a mixture-cure Weibull after a DE
Let $U$ be time since the DE. Moody's (1979–2026, 1,173 borrowers): ~1/4 of soft events end in hard default; >70% of those within two years; after year five the hazard adds <0.5 pp/yr; ~2/3 clean for a decade (VERIFIED). A mixture-cure model matches all four facts:
$$\mathbb{P}(U>u)=(1-p)+p\,\exp\!\big[-(u/\eta)^k\big],\qquad p\in(0,1)\ \text{the "susceptible" fraction}.$$
Population hazard $h(u)=\dfrac{p\,f_0(u)}{(1-p)+p\,S_0(u)}$ with $f_0,S_0$ the Weibull density/survival. Calibration: set $p=0.25$ for hard default (or $\approx0.35$–$0.40$ for "hard default or another DE"). The condition $F(2)/p=0.70$ gives $(2/\eta)^k=-\ln 0.30=1.204$. With $k=1$, $\eta\approx1.66$ years (baseline hazard among the susceptible $\approx0.60$/yr); with $k=0.7$ (decreasing hazard, the empirically plausible case), $\eta\approx1.53$. Initial population hazard at $u=0^+$ for $k=1$: $0.25\times0.60=0.15$/yr, decaying toward zero, which is exactly the "<0.5 pp after year five" shape.
Covariates enter through $p$ (logistic) and $\eta$ (log-linear): `new_money_fraction`, `maturity_extension_years`, `liquidity_runway_weeks` (the 13-week model, reborn), `sponsor_backed`, `loan_only_structure`, `uptier_vs_dropdown_vs_A&E`, `coop_in_place`, `docs_vintage`.
**Error-prone spots.** (a) Right-censoring: 2024–2025 LMEs have at most 1–2 years of follow-up; a naive estimate of $p$ is biased low. ROBO's literal implementation sets the hazard to zero after the last observed event. (b) Identifiability of $p$ vs. $\eta$ with short follow-up: fix $k$ or put a prior on it. (c) Left truncation if the sample is "issuers that were still around when the database started."
### 3.3 Expected loss with a soft-default path
Horizon $T$. Let $L_H=1-R_H$ be LGD on a hard default with no prior LME, $L_D$ the immediate economic haircut at the DE (depends on coalition side), $q$ the probability of hard default within the remaining horizon given a DE, and $L_{H\mid D}$ the LGD on a hard default *after* an LME. Then
$$\mathrm{EL}(T)=F_H(T)\,L_H+F_D(T)\Big[L_D+(1-L_D)\,q\,L_{H\mid D}\Big].$$
*Illustrative* inputs: $\lambda_H=0.022,\lambda_D=0.038,T=3$ ⇒ $F_D(3)\approx0.102$, $F_H(3)\approx0.058$; $L_H=0.47$ (Fitch no-LME 53% recovery, CHECK); $L_{H\mid D}=0.77$ (Fitch 23%, CHECK); $q=0.25$. Majority-side holder ($L_D=0.26$, the Serta exchange ratio): EL $\approx6.8\%$. Excluded holder ($L_D=0.75$, the \$0.25 closing-date price in the Serta remand): EL $\approx10.9\%$. Naive single-state model with 47% LGD on the total 16% three-year default probability: $7.5\%$. The spread between 6.8% and 10.9% is the coalition variable. Mark all three *illustrative*.
### 3.4 Recovery as a coalition mixture
$$R\mid\text{default}\sim\pi\,\mathrm{Beta}(a_{in},b_{in})+(1-\pi)\,\mathrm{Beta}(a_{out},b_{out}),\quad \pi=\mathbb{P}(\text{in majority group}).$$
$\mathbb{E}[R]=\pi\mu_{in}+(1-\pi)\mu_{out}$ and
$$\mathrm{Var}[R]=\pi\sigma_{in}^2+(1-\pi)\sigma_{out}^2+\pi(1-\pi)(\mu_{in}-\mu_{out})^2.$$
Serta numbers *(from the remand opinion; the value interpretation is illustrative)*: $\pi\approx0.526$ (participating lenders held ~\$992mm of a \$1.887bn class), $\mu_{in}\approx0.74$, $\mu_{out}\approx0.25$ ⇒ $\mathbb{E}[R]\approx0.51$ and a between-group standard deviation of $\approx0.245$ *from membership alone*. That is the whole historical cross-sectional dispersion of recoveries (Schmid p.103: std ≈27%) generated by one binary. Method-of-moments Beta fits per group; the Bohn–Stein two-stage model gets a new first stage: $\pi$ from a logistic on holdings concentration, CLO share, co-op membership, and fund type.
Link to Ch 5: the zero- and one-inflated Beta remains; the inflation masses now have coalition-specific weights.
### 3.5 Regime switching in $(\lambda_D/\lambda,\ \mathbb{E}[L])$
Two-state hidden Markov model, states $s_t\in\{0,1\}$ ("pre-LME regime", "LME regime"), transition matrix $P$. Observables: annual DE share $y_t$ (or monthly DE counts as binomial draws) and mean LGD. Hamilton filter: with $\eta_t=[f(y_t\mid s_t=0),f(y_t\mid s_t=1)]^\top$,
$$\hat\xi_{t\mid t}=\frac{\hat\xi_{t\mid t-1}\odot\eta_t}{\mathbf 1^\top(\hat\xi_{t\mid t-1}\odot\eta_t)},\qquad \hat\xi_{t+1\mid t}=P^\top\hat\xi_{t\mid t},$$
log-likelihood $\sum_t\log\mathbf 1^\top(\hat\xi_{t\mid t-1}\odot\eta_t)$. Recovery regime: Beta mean $\mu_s$ with $\mu_0\approx0.43$ (expansions) and $\mu_1\approx0.28$ (recessions) from Schmid p.103 as a *macro* regime; the LME regime is a second, orthogonal switch on $\pi$ and $\mu_{out}$.
