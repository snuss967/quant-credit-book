## 1. Verdict and the three biggest problems

**Verdict.** Where the book touches the documents, it is better than most restructuring associates: §13.2 gets the Serta section numbers right (§2.18 sharing, §9.02(b) sacred rights, §9.05(g) buybacks), the Buccola–Nini blocker data is correctly used as a vintage covariate, the §1123(a)(4) indemnity excision and the Louisiana-headquarters footnote are the right things to quote, Rule 3001(e)'s publicly-traded-notes carve-out is stated exactly, the §502(b)(6)/(b)(7)/503(b)(9)/*World Imports* block in §16.5.2 is accurate, the Hertz Docket Diary is right on impairment, the federal judgment rate and the Third Circuit's solvent-debtor holding, and the Sound Point and Marathon MNPI orders are the right regulatory anchors for the information wall. A partner would trust those pages.

What a partner would *not* trust is the book's picture of the case itself. Edition 1 knows the LMT zoo and the post-reorg aftermarket intimately and knows almost nothing about the eighteen months in between. The corpus has 3 hits for §1129, 1 for cramdown, 0 for "fair and equitable," "unfair discrimination," "best interests," "new value," "liquidation analysis," "credit bid," "stalking horse" (outside the glossary), "KEIP/KERP," "Purdue," "third-party release," "§506," "§1111(b)," "§547/preference," "equitable subordination," "Part 26A," "WHOA," "scheme of arrangement" and "sauvegarde," while Altice France, Thames Water and Chapter 15 are *mentioned* and never explained. "DIP" appears 46 times and is never priced; "roll-up" appears 7 times and is never sized. The planned 12-B state machine is the right skeleton, but at 8–11k words it cannot carry plan valuation, DIP and exit economics, confirmation law, claims trading, tax and cross-border as well. Those need their own homes or they will be a paragraph each, which is worse than absent because it reads as naive.

**The three biggest problems.**

1. **The plan is a black box between "LMT" (Ch 13–14) and "effective date" (Ch 16).** Nothing in the book explains how plan value is *set* (the disclosure-statement range, the expert fight, the market test), how the fulcrum *moves* while the case runs (DIP priming, roll-ups, adequate-protection claims, post-petition interest, administrative burn, the §1129(a)(9) cash wall), or what §1129(b) actually tests (fair and equitable per class type, unfair discrimination, the §1129(b)(2)(A) cram-down rate). Ch 16 says "plan equity value is a negotiated number" and stops; a desk model needs the *distribution* of that number and the identities of who sets it.
2. **The desk never learns the economics of the room it is modeling.** Ad hoc groups are a running gag (64 hits) and a Rule 2019 panel (56 hits), but the book never explains why groups form (fee reimbursement, backstop premiums, consent fees, exit-roll economics), what the RSA/TSA binds (milestones, fiduciary outs, termination events, lock-ups and the "joinder" dynamics that make the co-op counter move), how information walls actually operate (restricted vs public side, cleansing 8-Ks, big-boy letters), or how advisers are paid and what their retention signals. The Priming Risk Score has no covariate for any of this, and every one of those is public and dated.
3. **The book is confidently US-loan-centric at the exact moment the market is not.** Altice France (a French sauvegarde accélérée with a cross-class cram-down fight), Thames Water (Part 26A, Court of Appeal), Ardagh and Intrum (Part 26A / Swedish and US Chapter 11 recognition) are the 2024–26 cases that moved European credit; none has a forum section. The court-as-random-variable framework of §14.6 is excellent and has no non-US column. Likewise the tax line (§382, COD/§108 attribute reduction) is in the corpus 49 times as an acronym and never once as arithmetic, and it is the line that decides whether an exchange is done as a bankruptcy or out of court.
