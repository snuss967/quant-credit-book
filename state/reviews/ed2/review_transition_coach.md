# Edition 2 Review: The Transition Coach — Does This Book Get the Reader From RSSG to Productive?

*Fable transition-coach review, 18 Oct 2026. Role: career-transition coach and learning designer who has moved restructuring bankers into systematic credit and market-making seats. Inputs: `state/digests/ed1_review_digest.md`, `state/digests/book_outline.md`, the summary sections of `state/reviews/ed1/engagement.md` and `foundations.md`, the two Edition 2 sibling reviews (`review_desk_head.md`, `review_rssg_partner.md`), corpus-wide `grep` counts over `book_src/`, and ten opened sections: the preface ("Who this is for", "Three ways through the book", "How a chapter is built"), the Part I opener, §1.1, §3.5, §4.1, §26.9, §28.8, App A §A.9, and the exercise sets of Ch 5 and Ch 18. No whole chapters were read; no web research. This review does not repeat the engagement review (figures, canonical case homes, cold-open labels, running gags) or the foundations review (primer chapters 4-B, 6-B, 7-B, 12-B, App D, App E, reading-order fixes), all of which Edition 2 is already executing. It asks a different question: if a PJT RSSG associate buys this book in October 2026, will it get them a seat on a systematic desk and make them useful in it?*

---

## 1. Verdict and the three biggest problems

**Verdict.** As a *reference* for the transition, the book is already the best thing in print: Chapter 3 ("the chapter the title of this book promises") rewrites the banker's questions as distributions, the Edge Ledger (§3.5) is the right object, the cast makes the culture clash legible, and the 153 code blocks with asserts are a real curriculum in disguise. As a *course*, it is not yet one. The preface promises that "the math is taught from first principles up through stochastic calculus and machine learning," and the foundations primers will make that true. But teaching is not only building concepts in the right order; it is telling the learner where they are, what they must be able to do before moving on, how to check themselves, and what to do with the result. On those four counts the corpus is close to empty: "self-assessment" and "quiz" 0 hits across 474k words; "Fermi" and "mental math" 0; "interview" 6 lines in 5 files, none about getting hired; "30/60/90" one passing mention; no solutions to any of the roughly 160 exercises (the only inline checks are in Ch 3, 4, 5, 7, 10, 22, 25, 26); no project list; no instructions for running the book's own code outside three passing references to `tools/check_book.py`. Theo is "three weeks into a systematic credit desk," and the book never says how he got there.

**Problem 1: the exercises are a syllabus without a grader.** Twenty-five chapters carry five or six exercises each, and they are uniformly excellent and uniformly hard: every one is a derivation or a build (Ch 18 #2 asks the reader to re-derive the jump-to-default reservation price with a Beta recovery and sign the Jensen effect; Ch 5 #4 asks them to show ROBO's kurtosis mandate is "a coin flip with a coupon"). There are no warm-ups, no difficulty tags, no time estimates, and no solutions. Ch 5 and Ch 3 show the right pattern (a "Check:" with the number in parentheses) and twenty chapters ignore it. Ch 6-B, 7-B, 12-B and App D currently have no exercises at all, which is backwards: the primers are where the ex-banker most needs to practice. A reader who cannot tell whether they got Ch 13 #1 right (the unblended Serta lien split) cannot tell whether they are ready for Ch 14. Learning design calls this a missing feedback loop; a desk calls it trading without a markout.

**Problem 2: there is a learning path but no ramp, no gates and no dictionary.** The preface offers three paths "one per tribe," and Path 1 sends the banker Part I → Part IV → Part II, which means they meet Kaplan–Meier, Cox, competing risks, Beta regression and the Hamilton filter (Ch 13–15) before Ch 5, 7-B or 12-B build them. That path now contradicts the foundations review's own reading-order fixes and must be rewritten around the primers. More fundamentally, nothing in the book is marked by difficulty, no Part says what the reader must be able to *do* before leaving it, and the "phrasebook" the Part I opener promises does not exist: App A §A.9 is a 92-entry glossary of *definitions* (what a 363 sale is), not a *translation* (what the banker's word "cushion" becomes on the desk: distance to a Black–Cox barrier, §6.5). The RSSG reader's hardest problem in month one is not any equation; it is that they already have a word for everything and every word is subtly wrong.

**Problem 3: the book skips the two ends of the transition, getting the seat and surviving the first year.** The desk-head review has already proposed `03_ch03b_your_first_year_on_the_desk.md` with a first-ninety-days checklist, and I endorse it; this review covers what sits *before* day one. Market-making and systematic shops hire through a canon the book never mentions: probability and expected-value puzzles, conditional probability with a live counterparty (the interviewer trades against you and you must update), "make me a market on X" games with the bid–ask and a confidence-interval variant, betting and Kelly sizing, mental math under a clock, Fermi estimates, pandas screens on a tape, and, for the ex-banker specifically, the thirty-minute credit pitch that must now be delivered as a distribution with a horizon and a markout rather than as a memo. Every one of those maps onto a section the book has already written (§3.2 is the adverse-selection game; §28.4 is the Kelly question; §3.4 is the Bayes update; §18.4 is "make me a market"), and the book never says so. Likewise, the portfolio: a career-changer is hired on proof of work, and the book's 28 engines are twelve portfolio projects waiting to be named, with public data in App B and an agent prompt in every Spec box. Nobody has drawn the line from one to the other.

## 3. Red-pen items

1. **§1.1, line 29 (`01_ch01_anthology_and_landscape.md`).** "Time moves in weeks; alpha is extracted by structuring a private non-pro-rata transaction where your client captures a 20-point priming premium over left-behind minority lenders." RSSG is an adviser: it earns a fee for a negotiated outcome; the *client* captures the premium; and the sentence tells the target reader that their old job was priming minorities. A restructuring associate reading page one of the book written for them will stop trusting it here. Rewrite: the unit of account is the negotiated outcome; the reader's asset is knowing exactly which clause makes the 20 points possible, on whichever side they sat.
2. **§1.1, "Why the Hybrid…" (lines 53–57).** "Pure quantitative researchers trained in equity stat-arb fail in distressed…"; "a standard Kalman filter or GARCH model cannot anticipate"; "The ultimate alpha in modern credit belongs to the architect…". Marketing register and a straw man (a jump-diffusion or the book's own §12.4 regime filter is the quant's answer). The book already has the evidence that makes the point: the Barclays aligned-signals numbers in §28.8 (58% → 66% when analyst and quant agree) and the \$90–140bn of quant-screened discretionary money in §4.1. Replace the assertion with the numbers and a forward reference.
3. **§1.1, lines 31 and 73–75.** "Susquehanna International Group (SIG) in Bala Cynwyd, or a systematic credit pod at Citadel or DE Shaw"; the table assigns "SIG, Jane Street, Citadel Securities, Flow Traders" to "Milliseconds to 10 Days" and "DE Shaw, Saba Capital, Elliott (Trading), Millennium pods" to capital-structure arbitrage, with no source. The preface's own rule is "Real firms and real people appear only with verifiable facts attached." This is also the one table a job-seeker will read as a map of employers, so it is the costliest place to be approximately right. Source it or make it generic until 4-C's sourced table exists.
4. **§3.5, line 373 (`03_ch03_pjt_rssg_cognitive_bridge.md`).** "…exactly the kind of feature that an AI coding agent can help scale once a human has specified it correctly, which is the subject of Chapter 25 and the reason this book teaches you to write specs." Resolve or cut before Edition 2 ships; a reader deciding which of the five businesses to interview with is reading this row.

Path 1 routes the banker into Part IV before the survival analysis, Beta regression and option payoffs it uses are built (now in 7-B, 5, 6-B, 12-B); see gap 9.

## 5. The five things that would most raise the book's value for this reader

1. **Write the solutions (App Z) and tag the exercises.** This is the single largest gain per hour of authoring. The exercises are already the course; without checks and solutions they are decoration, and a career-changer studying alone has no other way to know whether they are ready. Start with the primers and Part I, since that is where the reader is in month one.
2. **Write the dictionary (App A §A.10).** One hundred and twenty rows, each translating a word the banker already uses into the object the desk means, with the section and the trap. It is the cheapest page in the book and the one the reader will open most, and it converts the Part I opener's "phrasebook" from a promise into a page.
3. **Write Chapter 4-C.** The reader has to get the seat before Chapters 5–28 matter, and the interview canon of market-making firms is a set of games the book has already derived (§3.2, §3.4, §18.4, §18.7, §28.4) without telling anyone they are also the interview. The chapter is mostly a re-indexing of existing material plus sixty problems, and it is the chapter the title's reader buys the book for on the day they decide to leave.
4. **Write the learning path (`00_02`) with tiers, gates and the "run the book" page.** Edition 2's primers fix the order in which concepts are built; this file fixes the order in which the reader meets them, tells them what they must be able to do before moving on, and makes the 153 code blocks runnable by someone who was not in the room when `check_book.py` was written. Rewrite the preface paths around it.
5. **Write the portfolio (App I) and the study-with-the-robot protocol.** Proof of work gets career-changers hired, and the book's engines are the proof, provided the reader builds them in a way an interviewer can distinguish from a prompt. The protocol (build it yourself at Tier 2; let ROBO build Tier 3, then break it; never let the agent see the exercise first; the asserts are the grade) is also the honest answer to the question every reader in 2026 is already asking, and it turns the book's defining device, the Spec You Hand the Robot, into its study method.

*Measured against the reader in the title, Edition 1 is a superb desk reference and about half of a course. The five items above are what turn it into the other half, and none of them requires new research: they are the book's own content, re-sorted for the person who has not yet been hired.*
