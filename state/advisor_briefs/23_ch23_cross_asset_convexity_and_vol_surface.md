- **Carr & Wu (2011), confirmed via IDEAS/RePEc:** Peter Carr and Liuren Wu, "A Simple Robust Link Between American Puts and Credit Protection," *Review of Financial Studies* 24(2), 473–505, 2011, DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhq129. Abstract (paraphrase): stock stays above barrier $B$ before default and falls below lower barrier $A<B$ after default, creating a "default corridor" $[A,B]$ the stock never enters; a spread of two co-terminal American puts struck inside the corridor replicates a pure credit contract paying if default occurs before expiry. Follow-up noted by search: Cruz López & Ibáñez (QJF 2021) argue the replicating put is European in an endogenous-default model (**CHECK** title).
- **Yu (2006), confirmed via IDEAS/RePEc:** Fan Yu, "How Profitable Is Capital Structure Arbitrage?", *Financial Analysts Journal* 62(5), 47–62, Sept/Oct 2006, DOI 10.2469/faj.v62.n5.4282. Uses CreditGrades; 135,759 daily CDS spreads on 261 North American obligors, 2001–2004; individual trades can lose heavily because CDS and equity are weakly correlated at short horizons; an equally weighted portfolio of trades has Sharpe ratios comparable to other fixed-income arbitrage strategies; speculative-grade monthly index Sharpe 0.75, uncorrelated with equity and bond factors.
- **Kapadia & Pu (2012), confirmed via IDEAS/RePEc:** Nikunj Kapadia and Xiaoling Pu, "Limited arbitrage between equity and credit markets," *Journal of Financial Economics* 105(3), 542–564, 2012, DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2011.10.014. Short-horizon equity–credit pricing discrepancies are common and a significant fraction are anomalous (weak integration); a statistical integration measure varies with firm-specific frictions (liquidity in both markets, idiosyncratic risk); helps explain why Merton hedge ratios match observed elasticities even though the model struggles with integration.

- **CreditGrades Technical Document, May 2002 (PDF text-extracted; page numbers are the document's printed pages).** Survival probability, eq. (2.11), p.8: $P(t)=\Phi\!\left(-\tfrac{A_t}{2}+\tfrac{\log d}{A_t}\right)-d\,\Phi\!\left(-\tfrac{A_t}{2}-\tfrac{\log d}{A_t}\right)$ with $d=\tfrac{V_0 e^{\lambda^2}}{\bar L D}$ (2.12) and $A_t^2=\sigma^2 t+\lambda^2$ (2.13). Derived by approximating $X_t=\sigma W_t-\lambda Z-\sigma^2 t/2-\lambda^2/2$ with a Brownian motion started at $-\lambda^2/\sigma^2$ ("replaces the uncertainty in the default barrier with an uncertainty in the level of the asset value at time 0"); the doc itself notes the "counterintuitive result that there is a non-zero probability of default at t = 0." CDS par spread (2.15), p.9: $c^*=r(1-R)\,\dfrac{1-P(0)+e^{r\xi}(G(t+\xi)-G(\xi))}{P(0)-P(t)e^{-rt}-e^{r\xi}(G(t+\xi)-G(\xi))}$, $\xi=\lambda^2/\sigma^2$, $G$ from Rubinstein–Reiner (1991), eq. (2.16). Calibration, pp.10–11: $\sigma_S=\sigma\,\tfrac{V}{S}\tfrac{\partial S}{\partial V}$ (2.17); boundary conditions give $V_0=S_0+\bar L D$ (2.25) and $\sigma=\sigma_S\,\tfrac{S}{S+\bar L D}$ (2.26); verbatim: "This dependence of equity volatility on the stock price is evident in a pronounced volatility skew in equity option markets, especially for high yield names." Reference-price variant (2.27). Parameters p.12–13: $\bar L=0.5$, $\lambda=0.3$ from PMD/S&P data on ~300 non-financial U.S. defaults 1987–1997; "A lower λ is expected for the financial sector." Debt-per-share $D$ includes financial debt, capital leases, under-funded pensions, preferreds; excludes payables/deferred taxes. Implied asset vol defined p.13 ("can be used similarly to an implied volatility in the equity derivatives market"). Greeks p.14: CreditGrades delta = "number of shares of the equity required to offset small price moves"; gamma rises as stock falls; vega per 1% asset vol. Empirics ch.3: 6,194 CDS quotes, 122 U.S. firms, through Aug 2001; implied asset vol "quite stable"; "a simple measure of volatility based on the last 1000 days of equity returns produces good estimates of the volatility implied by the 5-year CDS quotes," 88% within 10%. Also tested ATM implied vol of longest tenor (p.~31).

- **Nagel & Purnanandam (2020), confirmed via EconPapers:** "Banks' Risk Dynamics and Distance to Default," *RFS* 33(6), 2421–2467, DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhz125. Bank assets are risky debt claims with concave payoffs ⇒ lognormal structural models misfit; bank asset vol rises after negative shocks; constant-vol structural models "can seriously understate banks' default risk" in good times; bank equity vol responds far more to negative shocks than standard models predict.

- **Culp, Nozawa & Veronesi (2018), confirmed via IDEAS:** "Option-Based Credit Spreads," *AER* 108(2), 454–488, DOI 10.1257/aer.20151606. "Pseudo firms" holding traded assets financed by equity and zero-coupon debt; pseudo-bond yield = Treasury yield minus put price on pseudo-assets; pseudo-bond spreads are large, countercyclical, forecast slower growth; illiquidity, PD overestimation, and corporate frictions do not explain the excess ⇒ tail-risk/idiosyncratic-asset-risk premium drives corporate spreads.
