## 1. Thesis and the "aha"

**Thesis.** Chapters 1–27 are about *finding* IDO. Chapter 28 is about *not giving it back*. The desk head's scarce resource is not capital but risk (Paleologo p.88), and the job is to allocate that risk along four axes under one constraint. The axes: (1) **signal horizon** (fast CDX/ETF/futures signals vs. slow cash-bond value and carry; the aim portfolio tilts toward slow alpha decay, Gârleanu–Pedersen 2013); (2) **issuer jump-to-default** (Kelly with a jump term: the growth-optimal fraction is bounded by $1/L$, and in credit $L = 1-R$ is the number the ex-RSSG analyst estimates better than anyone); (3) **time** (drawdown control, the two-threshold "cut at $x/2$, stop at $x$" rule that Paleologo simulates and that the multi-manager platforms reportedly run as 5%/7.5%); (4) **people** (a team is a breadth multiplier only if the added coverage keeps the hit rate; Paleologo p.149). The constraint: liquidity is stochastic and procyclical. The round-trip cost of an IG index bond went from 0.59% to 3.40% between January 2007 and November 2008 (Ben Dor p.112–113), a 5.8× widening; IG transaction costs tripled and block costs rose more than sixfold in three weeks of March 2020 (Sharpe & Zhou, FEDS 2020); EUR IG bid-ask spiked 64% above its January average in March 2026 and took 4–13 weeks to normalize (MarketAxess via The DESK). The TOC's "bid-ask widens 5×" is not a stress scenario. It is the 2008 base case, and the chapter's engineering problem is to put that 5× *inside* the sizing, the leverage band, and the stop-loss before it happens, not after.

**The one equation to tape to the monitor.** Paleologo's leverage band (p.176, eq. 10.3–10.4), rewritten for credit:
$$\frac{\sqrt{N_{\text{eff}}}}{\sigma_{\text{DTS}}}\cdot\frac{r_{\min}}{s}\;\le\;L\;\le\;\frac{\sqrt{N_{\text{eff}}}}{\sigma_{\text{DTS}}}\cdot\frac{D_{\max}-\text{COL}(L)}{\kappa z_{p} - s},$$
where $N_{\text{eff}}$ is issuer-clustered effective breadth (not the bond count), $\sigma_{\text{DTS}} = \text{DTS}\times\sigma_{\text{rel}}$ is the DTS-scaled idiosyncratic volatility (Ben Dor p.50), $\text{COL}(L)$ is the Bangia-style cost of liquidating the gross book (which grows with $L$, so the ceiling is a fixed point), $\kappa\in[1,3]$ is the stressed-to-predicted volatility multiplier (Paleologo p.175), and $z_p=-F^{-1}(p_{\text{loss}})$. Every section of the chapter supplies one term of this inequality.

**The "aha" for the ex-RSSG reader (five of them).**

1. *The 13-week model is the PM dashboard.* The running gag resolves here: liquidity runway $=$ (cash $+$ unencumbered liquid sleeve) $/$ (redemption or margin burn rate) is the same object the RSSG banker built for the borrower. Third Avenue Focused Credit Fund died of a 13-week problem: \$1.1bn of 2015 outflows through December 9, "more than 145% of its remaining net asset value" (SEC IC-31943). Runway under one quarter is the restructuring banker's "going concern" flag, applied to the fund.
2. *Kelly in credit is a binary bet with recovery, and $L$ is the RSSG variable.* With carry $c$ on survival and loss $L$ on default, $f^* = p/L - q/c$. Nothing in the formula moves faster than $L$: first-lien distressed-exchange recoveries went from 55% (2023) to 71% (end-2024) (Moody's via Bloomberg Law, in the LMT dossier), and the Fed puts post-default value for direct loans at about 33% against 52% for syndicated loans (Cai & Haque, FEDS 2024, Ch 12 brief). The PhD takes $L$ from a rating-agency table; the analyst who has read the intercreditor agreement knows whether the "first lien" is first.
3. *The pod rule is an option, and mark-to-bid is a premium you pay in advance.* A PM paid on annual P&L holds a call (Paleologo p.164); the drawdown rule is the strike the platform imposes. Credit's twist: when bid-ask widens 5×, marking the book to bid consumes $\tfrac12\,\text{LCS}\times\text{GMV}$ of drawdown budget with no change in fundamentals. If you did not budget LVaR inside the stop-loss ex ante, the liquidity shock triggers the rule at the bottom, which is precisely when Blackstone's optimizer "algorithmically increases credit exposure" (Blackstone p.1) and when BlackRock finds managers "gave up more excess return in subsequent 'Very Positive' regimes than was mitigated on the downside" (BlackRock p.5).
4. *Horizons sort themselves by liquidity tier.* Fast signals (trend on CDX, Ch 10; ETF premium/discount, Ch 20) live in instruments with 10–20-day regulatory liquidity horizons; slow signals (value, carry, LMT-event alpha) live in cash bonds with FRTB horizons of 40 (IG) and 60 (HY) days (MAR33.12). "Aim in front of the target" (GP 2013) in credit means: the aim portfolio for cash bonds is almost all slow alpha, and the fast alpha is executed in the overlay. Man's experience is the proof of concept: >90% electronic flow allowed "more frequent, smaller trades" (Man 2023 p.4).
5. *Breadth is a skill multiplier only if the hit rate survives the hire.* $IR = (2p-1)\sqrt{252\,N_{\text{eff}}}$ (Paleologo p.148). Adding ex-RSSG analysts adds breadth exactly where quant IC is lowest and least crowded (LMT candidates, post-reorg equity, docket-driven events), which is why Barclays QPS finds IR rising from 1.09 to 1.71 when analyst and quant signals agree (industry dossier). The chapter's team section is a production function, not an HR memo.

**What is new relative to the shelf.** Paleologo is written for equity long/short with Gaussian-ish idio returns and continuous liquidity. The chapter's additions are: issuer-clustered breadth, DTS-scaled volatility, a jump term in Kelly, a size-dependent liquidity cost inside the leverage ceiling, a mark-to-bid shock inside the stop-loss simulation, and the 2018–2026 trend evidence that the platform model (multi-strategy AUM $+$\$500bn in a year, Point72 at 190 pods, pass-through fees with a 4.53% median) has made these rules the operating system of the industry.

**Paleologo (the spine)**

- Paleologo_Advanced_Portfolio_Management p.88 (why Sharpe, why leverage): "Return on GMV is performance relative to a capital budget. But the real scarce resource is not capital; it is risk." → Opens §28.2: the desk head allocates *risk*, not dollars; dollars are a consequence.
- Paleologo p.91 (sizing objectives): "If your goal were to maximize expected returns and your expected returns forecasts were accurate, the investment decision would be straightforward: the portfolio would hold a single stock, the one with highest expected returns. Those who follow this rule need to read this book the most, but probably don't have enough money to buy it." → Joke that teaches; the credit version is "hold the one CCC with the fattest spread."
- Paleologo p.92 (sizing rules): "The realized Sharpe Ratio is consistently higher for risk parity and for the proportional ratio than for the mean-variance." → Why the chapter prefers proportional/risk-parity sizing of IDO ideas over full MV in credit (ill-conditioned covariances, Ch 8).
- Paleologo p.100, Insight 6.2: "Using target positions that are proportional to the forecasted expected returns of a stock beats other common methods." → Also Procedure 6.3 (p.101): regress $\alpha$ on loadings $B$, standardize residuals, size to GMV — the factor-neutralization step that makes "IDO or it didn't happen" operational.
- Paleologo p.148 (the fundamental law in hit-rate form): "(Inf. Ratio) = [2 × (hitting probability) − 1] × √(252 × (effective number of stocks))" → Base equation for §28.3 breadth; credit needs the *issuer-clustered* effective number.
- Paleologo p.149 (breadth): "The power of diversification is great. It is not a skill: it is a skill multiplier." and "Beyond a certain threshold, coverage and breadth increase at the expense of accuracy." → The team-design section: adding ex-RSSG analysts adds breadth without diluting hit rate only if their hit rate matches the PM's.
- Paleologo p.150, Procedure 8.1: "H = sum[%GMV²(stock i)] … (effective number of stocks) = 1/H" → The Herfindahl breadth; we extend to issuer/sector/factor clustering.
- Paleologo p.150 (measurement noise): "The standard error associated to this yearly hitting rate is 0.33%. This is a very large value, comparable to the 'edge' of 0.5%." → Why one year of pod P&L cannot distinguish skill from noise; feeds the drawdown-rule critique.
- Paleologo p.163 (stop-loss taxonomy): "Stop-and-Shutdown … Stop-and-Restart … Within the same firm, a PM with a long track record is stopped and then restarted, while a new one is not given the benefit of the doubt." and "There is the stop-loss that you have, and the one you don't know you have."
- Paleologo p.164 (PM option): "Stop-loss rules are a partial hedge for the PM call option. … it is in the interest of the manager to run with the highest possible volatility, independently of her skill." → The incentive derivation in §28.6.
- Paleologo p.166 (opportunity cost): "Stop-loss exists to ensure survival of a firm, not to improve the performance of a strategy. There is a price to pay."
- Paleologo p.167 (simulation design): "a two-threshold one (cut capital in half when loss reaches (x/2)%, and liquidate at x% loss). - Paleologo p.174 (leverage identity): "(return) = s × σ/√n × L (10.1)" and "L ≥ √n/σ × (min return)/s (10.2)".
- Paleologo p.175 (the κ fudge): "in large drawdown events, it is always the case that the realized volatility is a multiple of the predicted volatility; investors experience two, three, even six sigma events. … we … assume that realized portfolio volatility is inflated by a positive factor κ … with values between 1 (if we really believe in models) and 3 (if we are skeptical)."
- Paleologo p.176 (leverage ceiling): "L ≤ √n/σ × (max d.d.%)/(−κF⁻¹(p_loss) − s) (10.3) … In practice, we want to set −κF⁻¹(p_loss) between 4 and 6." → In credit, $\sigma$ is spread vol × DTS, $n$ is issuer-effective breadth, and the drawdown must include the LVaR add-on.
- Paleologo p.178 (idio share): "When the percentage idio p_idio increases, it works its magic on both sides of the equation: the lower admissible leverage decreases and the highest admissible leverage increases."
- Paleologo p.131 (multi-PM platforms): "If you are managing a multi-PM hedge fund … You may still decide to maintain a strict autonomy among PMs, essentially managing the platform as a fund of funds. The hidden cost in this choice is the opportunity cost of not adopting more advanced techniques."
