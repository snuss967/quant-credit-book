## 1. Thesis and "aha"

**Thesis.** A systematic credit desk runs on two clocks, and the whole architecture falls out of refusing to confuse them. The *quote clock* ticks in seconds to minutes: an RFQ arrives, a composite price is looked up, a model adds inventory skew and adverse-selection margin, a trader (or an auto-responder) answers inside a venue timer that is measured in minutes, and the public tape reports the print up to 15 minutes later, capped at \$5mm (FINRA Notice 25-17; §6.1). The *hedge clock* ticks in microseconds: the moment a bond quote is live on an all-to-all venue or a streaming portal, it is a free option written to anyone who sees Treasury futures, HYG/LQD, CDX or a credit index future move first. Latency-arbitrage races in equities last 5–10 microseconds at the mode (Aquilina, Budish and O'Neill, FCA OP50, 2020; §6.4), and the instruments a credit desk hedges with trade on exactly those venues. So the correct design is not "make everything fast" (expensive, pointless for RFQs) and not "credit is slow, relax" (that is how you get picked off on every streaming quote). It is a **two-speed system**: a slow, rich, auditable path for pricing and order management, and a thin, dumb, provably bounded fast path whose only jobs are (i) pull or widen exposed quotes when a precomputed linear trigger fires, and (ii) stop the machine. The FPGA does not price bonds. It compares numbers that the slow path computed and wrote into a table. That is the entire "ASICs/FPGAs" story for credit, and it is more interesting than the equity version because the table's rows are duration-times-beta sensitivities to *other* markets, not the bond's own book.

**The aha for Theo.** At PJT the dangerous moment was the lender call; on the desk the dangerous moment is the 400 milliseconds after TY ticks and before your streaming quote in a BBB 10-year updates. Theo already knows how to read a document for the one clause that reprices everything; the chapter asks him to read a *system* the same way. Every component (feed handler, security master, pricing engine, OMS/EMS, risk, kill switch) is a party to a contract with latencies, failure modes and consent rights, and the kill switch is the sacred right that cannot be amended by a majority. Three sub-ahas, in the order the chapter should land them:

1. **The security master is the credit agreement of the system.** Every LMT, exchange offer and reorganisation creates new identifiers, new rankings and new issuer–guarantor maps (Serta 2020: one loan becomes first-out, second-out and left-behind; §6.6). A master keyed on CUSIP alone breaks the day the exchange closes, and the identifier itself is a licensed, litigated monopoly (Dinosaur v. S&P Global, S.D.N.Y. 1:22-cv-01860). The right design is a bitemporal table with an internal surrogate key, "valid time" (when a fact was true) and "knowledge time" (when the desk learned it), because that is the only data structure that lets a backtest honestly answer "what did we know at 2:58 p.m. on June 22, 2020?" Look-ahead bias is a data-layer bug before it is a research bug (Ben Dor p.102, S16).
2. **Latency is a queue, not a number.** The chapter should teach Little's law and Kingman's formula so that "our pricing engine takes 40 ms" becomes "at 70% utilisation with CV² of 4 in service times, the p99 is 600 ms, and the p99 is what the sniper trades against." Tail latency amplifies with fan-out (Dean and Barroso 2013): a 150-line portfolio trade that fans out to 150 pricing calls, each slow one time in a hundred, is slow 78% of the time. This is why the 90-second PT budget in Chapter 19 is an engineering constraint before it is a pricing one.
3. **A kill switch is a stopping rule with a measure attached.** Knight lost \$460mm in 45 minutes with 97 unread emails because its alerts were not designed as alerts and nothing was designed to *stop* (SEC Release 34-70694; §6.2). Citi's 2022 fat finger went out because the only control was a pop-up (§6.2). The chapter should formalise the kill-switch hierarchy as nested stopping times: hard limits (position, notional, message rate, price collar) that fire in hardware or in the gateway; sequential tests (CUSUM on markouts) that fire in seconds; and the Paleologo stop-loss (S10) that fires in days. Each has a false-alarm rate and a detection delay, and the desk head chooses both.

**One-sentence version for the cold open:** the fastest thing on a credit desk should be the thing that says no.

**Trend angle (the book's mandate).** Three structural trends make this chapter a 2026 chapter and not a 2012 equity retread: (a) the arrival of a listed hedging layer for credit (CME Bloomberg credit futures since June 2024, more than 580,000 contracts by Oct 2025; Cboe IBHY/IBIG blocks above \$1bn per month in mid-2026; §6.5), which for the first time puts a credit-beta hedge on an exchange matching engine with microsecond order entry; (b) the automation of the quote clock itself (Trumid 88% no-touch, Tradeweb AiEX above 40%, Vanguard about 70% automated; §6.1), which makes the auto-responder's parameter file the credit desk's trigger table; and (c) the consolidation of the composite-price and venue layer (ICE–MarketAxess, 2026-07-29), which makes "which composite priced this quote, at what staleness" a provenance field the architecture must carry. The trend/momentum link: a trend signal's edge decays with implementation lag, so the latency budget is literally a term in the trend strategy's Sharpe ratio (DER E in §3).

### 6.2 The kill-switch canon: incidents and rules

- **Knight Capital, 2012-08-01.** SEC Order, Release No. 34-70694, File No. 3-15570, dated 2013-10-16 (VERIFIED-URL: https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf; PDF metadata confirmed; body text extracted below in this subsection once parsed). Canonical facts to confirm from the order text: about 45 minutes, about \$460mm loss, new RLP code deployed to 7 of 8 servers, the repurposed flag that reactivated dormant "Power Peg" code, 97 pre-open automated emails that nobody treated as alerts, and the \$12mm penalty under Rule 15c3-5 (CHECK each number against the extracted text before printing).
- **Knight, confirmed from the order text (VERIFIED-URL, SEC Release 34-70694, 2013-10-16; text extracted with pdftotext).** ¶1: "On August 1, 2012, Knight Capital Americas LLC ('Knight') experienced a significant error in the operation of its automated routing system for equity orders, known as SMARS. While processing 212 small retail orders ... SMARS routed millions of orders into the market over a 45-minute period, and obtained over 4 million executions in 154 stocks for more than 397 million shares." Net long about \$3.5bn in 80 stocks and net short about \$3.15bn in 74 stocks; "Knight lost over \$460 million." ¶13–14: the new RLP code reused a flag that formerly activated "Power Peg," functionality Knight "had discontinued using many years earlier" (ceased 2003); in 2005 Knight moved the cumulative-fill tracking earlier in SMARS and "did not retest the Power Peg code." ¶15–16: the deployment missed one of the servers, which ran the dormant Power Peg code and sent child orders without the fill counter. Rule 15c3-5 initial compliance date 2011-07-14 (fn). (Penalty and the "97 emails" count: see the follow-up extraction note below in this subsection; print only what the text shows.)
- **Citigroup Global Markets, 2022-05-02 fat finger; FCA fine 2024-05-22 (web search 2026-10-17; sources: FCA final notice coverage by Foot Anstey, Investment Executive, Financial Planning Today; URLs in §9; CHECK against the FCA final notice itself).** Trader meant to sell a \$58mm basket; an OMS input error produced a \$444bn basket. Controls blocked \$255bn; \$189bn went to an algorithm; about \$1.4bn of shares sold on European venues before the trader cancelled, coinciding with a short, sharp drop in several European indices. ### 6.3 Clocks, timestamps and the public tape

- **FINRA Rule 4590 (VERIFIED-URL: https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/4590).** Computer clocks: one-second general tolerance; clocks recording events in NMS securities and OTC equities "must be synchronized within a 50-millisecond tolerance of the NIST clock." Manual time stamps: one second. The tolerance covers NIST-to-provider difference, transmission delay and drift. Amended by SR-FINRA-2016-005 effective 2016-08-15. Consequence for the chapter: the regulator's clock is 50 ms for equities and 1 s for the rest of the book (TRACE events are not NMS events); the desk's internal clock (PTP, sub-microsecond) is three to six orders of magnitude finer than anything a regulator will ever ask for, and the gap is where markout analysis lives.

### 6.4 FPGAs, ASICs and the arms race

- **STAC-T0 tick-to-trade record (web search 2026-10-17; AMD blog "AMD Sets STAC Benchmark World Record for Fastest Electronic Trade Execution", 2024; AMD press release 2024-10-14 on Alveo UL3422; URLs in §9; fetch pending for exact wording).** AMD with Exegy reported a minimum 13.9 ns "actionable latency" on the Alveo UL3524 under STAC-T0, down from the prior record of 24.2 ns (about 49%). Alveo UL3422 claims less than 3 ns FPGA transceiver latency. These numbers used Exegy's application IP, not an open-source design. The "Accelerated Algorithmic Trading" open-source reference design was NOT confirmed by search; treat as CHECK / TITLE-ONLY.
- **Dean & Barroso, "The Tail at Scale," Communications of the ACM, vol. 56 (2013), pp. 74–80 (VERIFIED-URL: https://research.google/pubs/the-tail-at-scale/; abstract only).** The canonical fan-out result (a 1-in-100 slow server makes 63% of 100-way fan-out requests slow) is from the full paper; state it as the paper's example (CHECK exact numbers from the paper body).
- **Aquilina, Budish & O'Neill, "Quantifying the High-Frequency Trading 'Arms Race': A Simple New Methodology and Estimates," FCA Occasional Paper 50, January 2020 (VERIFIED-URL: https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-50.pdf; abstract extracted verbatim). Later published in the QJE (2022; volume/pages CHECK).** Abstract: "latency-arbitrage races are very frequent (one per minute for FTSE 100 stocks), extremely fast (the modal race lasts 5-10 millionths of a second), and account for a large portion of overall trading volume (about 20%). Race participation is concentrated, with the top-3 firms accounting for over half of all race wins and losses. Our main estimates suggest that eliminating latency arbitrage would reduce the cost of trading by 17% and that the total sums at stake are on the order of \$5 billion annually in global equity markets." Use: the "modal race lasts 5–10 µs" line is the single most useful number for explaining why the credit desk's hedge leg (futures, ETFs) must be on the same clock as the equity HFTs, even though its RFQ leg is measured in minutes.
- **OP50 race-duration and impact numbers (same text, §5 of the paper).** "The average race duration in our data, as measured by the time from the first success message to the first fail message is 79 microseconds ... The mode of the distribution is between 5-10 microseconds, and the median is 46 microseconds ... the 90th percentile is about 200 microseconds." "Price impact from trading in races is about 31% of all price impact, and about 33% of the effective spread." Data: LSE message data under FSMA s.165, FTSE 350, 9 weeks in Fall 2015, "time-stamped with accuracy to the microsecond" at the exchange's "outer wall." Full FPGA citation from its bibliography: Lockwood, Gupte, Mehta, Blott, English and Vissers (2012), "A Low-Latency Library in FPGA Hardware for High-Frequency Trading (HFT)," IEEE [HOTI], pp. 9–16 (TITLE-ONLY; venue name CHECK).
- **Greenwich auto-quoter post confirmed (VERIFIED-URL: https://www.greenwich.com/blog/what-buy-side-wants-their-bond-dealers, Kevin McPartland, 2024-02-13).** Survey of 51 corporate bond investors, Q4 2023; "improved auto-quoting" topped the list; in March 2020 "several of the largest banks famously shut down their auto-quoters" because the pricing algorithms could not handle the volatility; the lesson let them "handle more recent volatility" (the post does not name 2023; do not print "regional bank crisis" as the post's words).
- **OP50 further numbers (same extracted text).** Dataset: LSE message data, all FTSE 350 stocks, 44 trading days 2015-08-17 to 2015-10-16; message timestamps accurate to the microsecond (CHECK exact wording at p.~10 of the paper). The "latency arbitrage tax" is "0.42 basis points if using total trading volume, and 0.53 basis points if" using non-race volume, against an average value-weighted effective spread "just over 3 basis points." The paper also notes CME's Market Segment Gateway architecture (2015) and cites Lockwood et al. (2012) on FPGA trading hardware and the Hibernia trans-Atlantic cable. Use: a 0.4–0.5 bp tax on a 3 bp spread is the equity benchmark for what a stale quote costs; the credit analogue, computed in §27.6, is larger because the half-spread is larger but the hedge-instrument move per RFQ timer is also larger.
- **Arista 7130 series (VERIFIED-URL: https://www.arista.com/en/products/7130-series).** "Layer 1+ network devices with port-to-port latencies as low as 4 nanoseconds"; FPGA applications (MetaWatch, MetaMux, MultiAccess, MetaProtect, Exchange, Switch) with "ultra-low latency multiplexing in as low as 39 ns" and "sub-nanosecond precise timestamping." Use: the ASIC/L1 layer of the latency budget; 4 ns is the floor you buy, not build.
- **AMD/Exegy STAC-T0 (web search only; AMD blog and press release returned 503 this session; CHECK before printing exact numbers).** 13.9 ns minimum actionable latency on Alveo UL3524 vs prior 24.2 ns; UL3422 (announced 2024-10-14) claims under 3 ns transceiver latency.
- **Budish, Cramton & Shim (2015), "The High-Frequency Trading Arms Race: Frequent Batch Auctions as a Market Design Response," Quarterly Journal of Economics 130(4) (TITLE-ONLY; Chicago Booth PDF link returned 404 this session).** Cite for the ES–SPY correlation breakdown at millisecond horizons and the "sniping" mechanism, which is exactly the stale-quote option the credit desk writes every time it streams a firm price on an all-to-all venue while HYG and TY move.
