## 1. Thesis and "aha"

**Thesis.** Everything that comes out the back of a Chapter 11 — new common stock, warrants, subscription rights, a backstop commitment, a 503(b)(9) claim, a general unsecured claim — is a contingent claim on the same two random variables the RSSG banker already models: distributable value and the size of the denominator it gets divided by. What makes these instruments *tradeable alpha* rather than merely *analyzable* is that each one is held, at birth, by someone who is contractually or psychologically forbidden from holding it: a CLO that must carry equity at zero in its OC test, a bank that must dispose within two years, a vendor who wants cash this quarter, a retail shareholder who was promised the stock would be worthless and is now being handed a 30-year warrant. The chapter's job is to turn "forced seller" from a war-story noun into a measurable supply process with a decay rate, and to price the option-like pieces (rights, backstops, warrants, claim allowance, pool-size uncertainty) with the same tools as Chapters 5–6, under the correct measure, with the correct denominators.

**The "aha" for the ex-RSSG reader.** You spent three years building the plan. You know the equity value in the disclosure statement is a negotiated number, not an estimate; you know who in the ad hoc group cannot hold stock; you know which trade creditors shipped goods on the 19th day and which on the 21st. A pure quant sees "post-reorg equity" as a small-cap stock with no price history and a scary ticker. You see a *known supply schedule* (the CLO overhang), a *known valuation anchor* (the plan value the sponsors lowballed), and a *known denominator problem* (how many of the \$2bn of filed claims will be allowed). The chapter teaches you to write each of those down as a variable, then shows that the historical "post-reorg premium" (Eberhart–Altman–Aggarwal's 25% in 200 days) decayed to zero after 2010 exactly as institutions learned to do that — so the surviving edge is in the right tail, the OTC segment, and the *timing* of flow, not in buy-and-hold.

**Three sentences for the monitor.**
1. A backstop fee is the premium on a put you sold on the reorganized equity, struck at the rights price; the Hertz 10% fee is roughly the Black–Scholes price of that put at 70% vol over six months — plus a 25–50% discount you also got, which is why Buccola et al. find backstop parties recovering 21c vs. 8c for the identical claim.
2. A GUC recovery is $\min(1, D/C)$ with *both* $D$ and $C$ random; Jensen says pool-size variance destroys expected recovery, so the person who can estimate $C$ (rejection damages, §502(b)(6) caps, litigation claims) is paid for the variance everyone else is afraid of. A 503(b)(9) claim is the same instrument with $C$ replaced by the statutory requirement of 100% cash on the effective date — the pair trade isolates pool risk.
3. Forced selling is a supply process $Q_t$ with a decay rate $\theta$; expected post-emergence drift is proportional to $\kappa\theta Q_t/K_t$ where $K_t$ is arbitrage capital. That is a time-series momentum signal with a built-in expiry — the same mechanism as Ben Dor's fallen-angel reversal (price pressure peaks ≈3 months after the event, recovers over 1–2 years) transposed from bonds to the equity the bonds became.

**Why Hertz 2020–2026 is the spine.** It is the only case that runs the whole circuit: retail-mania equity in a bankrupt company (June 2020, \$29mm sold before the SEC flinched) → a competitive plan auction in which old equity received cash + 3% + 30-year warrants or rights at \$10 (May–June 2021) → a re-IPO at \$29 that made the warrants worth 1.8× the plan's estimate (Nov 2021) → the Third Circuit forcing the solvent debtor to pay >\$270mm of make-wholes and contract-rate interest because it had paid "lower priority equityholders four times that amount" (Sep 2024) → a record 41% one-day drop on a PIK-convert-plus-borrow financing (Jun 2026) → PJT hired to extend the runway while revolver and secured groups lawyer up (Oct 2026). One company, every instrument in the chapter, and the lesson that a 30-year warrant is mostly a bet on not needing Chapter 11 again before 2051.

## 3. Derivation skeletons

All four derivations are under the physical measure $\mathbb{P}$ unless stated; none of these instruments has a liquid hedge at birth, so any "$\mathbb{Q}$" is nominal (a traded proxy's measure: when-issued stock, peer equities, the HY index). Say so in the text every time; Kaz will try to write $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}$ out of habit.

### 3.1 Forced selling as a decaying supply process (§16.2)

**Setup.** Fundamental log value $v_t$ (a $\mathbb{P}$-martingale plus a risk premium we ignore over 90 days). Overhang $Q_t$ = shares that constrained holders are *required* to sell (CLOs carrying equity at zero in OC tests, banks under a two-year clock, insurers). They execute at rate $\theta$:
$$dQ_t = -\theta Q_t\,dt, \qquad Q_{t_0} = Q_0 \;(\text{released at the effective date, or at lock-up expiry } t_L).$$
Arbitrage capital $K_t$ arrives slowly (Duffie 2010): $dK_t = \rho (K^* - K_t)\,dt$. Observed log price carries a liquidity discount proportional to overhang per unit of capital:
$$p_t = v_t - \kappa\,\frac{Q_t}{K_t}.$$
**Expected excess drift** (the tradeable object):
$$\mu_t \equiv \frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[p_t - v_t] = \kappa\left(\theta\frac{Q_t}{K_t} + \frac{Q_t\dot K_t}{K_t^2}\right) > 0 .$$
With $K$ constant: $p_t - v_t = -\tfrac{\kappa Q_0}{K}e^{-\theta(t-t_0)}$; cumulative abnormal return from $t_0$ is $\kappa Q_0/K$; half-life $\ln 2/\theta$. If supply is released on a schedule $Q_t = Q_0 \int_0^t g(u)\,du$ (plan distributions, lock-up ladders, index deletions at month-end), the discount peaks when $\dot Q_t = \theta Q_t$, i.e. *after* the event — Ben Dor p.27's "about three months after the downgrade."

**Mapping to the shelf.** Dynkin p.151: fallen angels −15.09% into the event vs. +5.66% for upgrades, with *no* reversal for upgrades: the asymmetry identifies constraint ($Q_0>0$ only on one side), not overreaction. Dynkin p.158: reversal +5.75%, +1.04%, +0.86% over three successive six-month windows ⇒ $\theta$ fit from the ratio $5.75/1.04$ gives $e^{-\theta\cdot 0.5}\approx 0.18$, $\theta\approx 3.4$/yr, half-life ≈ 2.4 months (*illustrative fit to published aggregates*).

**Momentum link (Ch 10).** The signal is $\hat\mu_t = \hat\kappa\hat\theta\,\hat Q_t/\hat K_t$ with $\hat Q_t$ from holder registries (Rule 2019, 13D/G, Form 4, plan distribution tables) minus executed volume attributable to constrained holders. It is time-series momentum with an expiry: once $\hat Q_t \approx 0$ the drift is zero and you are long a small-cap with no analyst coverage.

**Error-prone spots.** (i) Using plan value as $v_t$: Moyer p.355/p.369 say plan values are biased *low* by controlling creditors, so the "discount" at emergence can be negative and the fit explodes; use the when-issued price or a peer-multiple band. (ii) OTC stale prices and bid-ask bounce in equal-weighted portfolios inflate alpha (Blume–Stambaugh); Jiang–Wang–Yang's alpha is OTC-concentrated for partly this reason. (iii) Survivorship: Spirit went to zero in five months; include delisting returns. (iv) Fundamental news confounds the fit: regress on peer returns first (Ch 8's IDO strip). (v) $\theta$ is not a constant: sellers accelerate into liquidity (volume ×4 with *wider* spreads, Dynkin p.140).

### 3.2 Rights offerings and the backstop put (§16.3)

**Setup.** Plan shares $n_0$ (distributed to creditors and direct investors), rights offering of $m$ new shares at price $K$ raising $mK$. Pre-money reorganized equity $E$ is random at funding; per-share $x \equiv E/n_0$. Post-money per-share value:
$$S^{\text{post}} = \frac{E + mK}{n_0 + m}, \qquad S^{\text{post}} > K \iff x > K .$$
**Value of a right** (per eligible plan share, $m/n_0$ rights each):
$$\text{Right} = \frac{m}{n_0}\,(S^{\text{post}}-K)^+ = \frac{m}{n_0+m}\,(x-K)^+ ,$$
a call on *pre-money* per-share value times one dilution factor. (TERP is just $S^{\text{post}}$ evaluated at today's $x$.)

**The backstop is a short put.** The committing party buys all unsubscribed shares at $K$. Rational holders subscribe iff $x>K$, so in the bad state the backstop takes all $m$ and loses $m(K - S^{\text{post}}) = \tfrac{m n_0}{n_0+m}(K-x)$:
$$\text{Backstop payoff} = -N_{\text{eff}}\,(K-x)^+,\qquad N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{m n_0}{n_0+m}.$$
Fair fee as a fraction of capital raised, with $P(K;\sigma,T)$ the put on $x$ over the horizon $T$ from commitment to funding:
$$f^* = \frac{N_{\text{eff}}\,P(K;\sigma,T)}{mK} = \frac{n_0}{n_0+m}\cdot\frac{P(K;\sigma,T)}{K}.$$
**Worked number** (*illustrative; code confirms*): $x_0 = 1$, $K = 0.75$ (Buccola's 25% plan discount), $\sigma = 0.70$, $T = 0.5$, $r=0$: $d_1 = 0.829$, $d_2 = 0.334$, $P = 0.75\,\Phi(-0.334) - \Phi(-0.829) \approx 0.0734$, so $P/K \approx 9.8\%$; with Hertz's $n_0/(n_0+m) \approx 291.3/454.8 \approx 0.64$, $f^*\approx 6.3\%$ — Seth's 6% average fee is almost exactly the Black–Scholes put at 70% vol. Hertz's 10% was rich on this metric, and richer still because the true $T$ (Mar–May 2021 commitment → 30 Jun 2021 funding) was ≈0.1–0.25y.

**The all-in transfer** (why Buccola finds 20.1% and 21c vs. 8c): fee $f mK$ *paid in shares* (worth $f m S^{\text{post}}$, not $f m K$) + the discount $(x_0 - K)$ on the backstop's own subscription + the **exclusivity option**: unsubscribed shares that arrive even when $x > K$ (irrational non-exercise, retail, ineligible holders) are a free call. Hertz: 36.1mm backstopped shares at \$10 were worth \$29 by November (*ex post*, ≈\$686mm, approx.); the 16.35mm fee shares ≈\$474mm at \$29.

**Error-prone spots.** (i) Mean of $x$: use the sponsors' *purchase* price as the anchor only if you believe it is fair; the plan equity value is a negotiated number. (ii) Apply the dilution factor once. (iii) $T$ is commitment-to-funding, not case length. (iv) Fee in shares ≠ fee in cash. (v) The ConvergeOne/Serta regime: an exclusive backstop now carries a legal-reversal hazard; model $\Pr(\text{fee survives}\mid\text{circuit, market test})$ as a Bernoulli multiplier on the fee leg, with 25 Sep 2025 as a jump date.

### 3.3 Claims with a random denominator (§16.5)

**Setup.** Distributable value to the class $D$, allowed pool $C$, recovery $R = \min(1, D/C)$. Let $Y = \ln(D/C) \sim N(\mu_Y, s_Y^2)$ (both lognormal; correlation enters $s_Y$). Then
$$\mathbb{E}[R] = \Pr(Y\ge 0) + \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{Y}\mathbf 1_{\{Y<0\}}\right] = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\mu_Y}{s_Y}\right) + e^{\mu_Y + s_Y^2/2}\,\Phi\!\left(-\frac{\mu_Y}{s_Y} - s_Y\right),$$
by completing the square (the exponential tilt shifts the mean by $s_Y^2$; this is the Black–Scholes bookkeeping with $D/C$ in the role of $S/K$). With $D$ fixed and $C$ lognormal with mean $m_C$ and log-sd $s$: $a = (\ln(D/m_C) + s^2/2)/s$, $\mathbb{E}[R] = \Phi(a) + (D/m_C)e^{s^2}\Phi(-a-s)$. **Jensen penalty:** at $D = m_C$, $s = 0.30$: $\mathbb{E}[R] \approx 0.917$ vs. $\min(1, D/m_C) = 1$ (*illustrative*). Eight points of recovery evaporate purely because the denominator is uncertain; the RSSG reader who can shrink $s$ (rejection-damage schedules, §502(b)(6) caps, WARN and litigation claims, duplicate and amended claims) is paid those eight points.

**Claim price.** With allowance probability $p_A$, random effective date $T$ (hazard $h$, so $\mathbb{E}[e^{-rT}] = h/(h+r)$ for exponential $T$), illiquidity premium $\psi$, and no coupon (§502(b)(2); Moyer p.413 n.26):
$$\pi^{\text{GUC}} = p_A\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-(r+\psi)T}\right]\mathbb{E}[R] \;+\; \underbrace{p_A\,\Pr(\text{solvent})\,\mathbb{E}[\text{post-petition interest}\mid\text{solvent}]}_{\text{the Hertz option, usually } \approx 0}.$$
**503(b)(9).** Same bond, different face: $R=1$ in cash on the effective date (§1129(a)(9)(A)) unless administratively insolvent:
$$\pi^{(9)} = p_A^{(9)}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{-(r+\psi)T}\right](1 - p_{AI}L_{AI}),\qquad p_A^{(9)} = \Pr(\text{goods, ordinary course, physical receipt within 20 days}),$$
with *World Imports* setting the "physical possession" rule. The **priority spread** $\ln\pi^{(9)} - \ln\pi^{\text{GUC}} = \ln(p_A^{(9)}/p_A) + \ln(1-p_{AI}L_{AI}) - \ln\mathbb{E}[R]$ holds duration fixed and isolates pool risk: long 503(b)(9) / short GUC (or short senior unsecured, Moyer p.269) is a short position in $s_Y$.

**Denomination risk (FTX).** If the claim is fixed in petition-date dollars, $R$ is computed against $C$ valued at $t_0$ prices while the holder's reference asset is worth $A_T$: in-kind recovery $= R\cdot A_{t_0}/A_T$. A 120% cash recovery on BTC fixed at ≈\$17k (11 Nov 2022) is a large in-kind loss at 2025 prices; the claim holder is short a call on the underlying with zero premium.

**Error-prone spots.** (i) $D$ and $C$ are *negatively* correlated in liquidation branches (more lease rejections ⇒ smaller $D$, larger $C$): $s_Y$ is larger than either marginal. (ii) Asserted ≠ scheduled ≠ allowed; duplicates and amendments inflate $C$ by multiples early in a case. (iii) Convenience-class elections truncate the distribution. (iv) Transfers under Rule 3001(e) have 21-day objection windows; "unreported trades may exceed \$100mm" (FTX). (v) Estimation under §502(c) is a court's point estimate, not a mean.

### 3.4 Warrants with dilution; the plan's implied volatility (§16.4)

Warrant count $n_w$, shares $n_0$, $q = n_w/n_0$. Exercise delivers new shares, so the warrant is a call on the *diluted* post-exercise price. Standard fixed point:
$$W = \frac{1}{1+q}\,C_{BS}\!\left(S + qW,\;K,\;T,\;\sigma,\;r\right),$$
solved by iteration from $W^{(0)} = C_{BS}(S,K)$ (contraction for $q<1$). Compound-option view: in Merton (Ch 6) $S$ is itself a call on $V_t$ with strike $F$, so the warrant is a call on a call and inherits the leverage-driven skew; for a 30-year tenor the price is dominated by $\Pr(\text{no second Chapter 11 before 2051})$ and vega, not delta.

**Hertz back-out** (*hand-computed, approx.; the spec's `implied_vol_warrant` confirms*): plan value \$769mm / 89.05mm warrants = \$8.63; $K = 13.80$, $T = 30$, $r \approx 2\%$. At $S = 10$ (consistent with the plan's \$141mm for 3% ⇒ \$4.7bn ⇒ \$10/share), $\sigma \approx 53\%$ before dilution; at $S = 13.80$ (the \$6.5bn "warrant basis"), $\sigma \approx 26\%$. The plan's own numbers are only mutually consistent at the lower spot, which is the honest one — and 53% is a reasonable long-dated vol for a levered rental-car equity, so the \$769mm was not a gift; the gift was the \$29 print four months later.

**Error-prone spots.** (i) Forgetting dilution lowers the vol you back out by a few points. (ii) The 30-year rate and dividend assumptions dominate rho; state them. (iii) Warrants are "freely tradable when issued but generally less liquid" (Moyer p.373): HTZWW quotes are not HTZ quotes. (iv) Do not use Black–Scholes without a jump-to-zero: add a Poisson default intensity $\lambda$ (Ch 6 notation) so $W$ carries $e^{-\lambda T}$; with $\lambda = 2\%$/yr over 30 years that is a 45% haircut on survival alone.
