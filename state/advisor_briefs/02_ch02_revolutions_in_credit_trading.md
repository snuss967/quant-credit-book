## 1. Thesis and the "aha" for an ex-PJT-RSSG reader

**Thesis.** Systematic credit did not become possible because someone found a better factor. It became possible because the *plumbing* changed: post-trade transparency (TRACE, 2002–2006) created the dataset, electronic RFQ (2000s–2010s) created firm, timestamped, comparable prices, all-to-all (2013 onward) let the buy side *make* prices, automation (2018 onward) let a model send and answer thousands of small tickets, portfolio trading and ETF create/redeem (2018 onward) let dealers price and hedge baskets in seconds, and non-bank market makers (2023 onward) brought balance sheets that are priced by models rather than relationships. Man Numeric's 2023 paper is the spine: the historical barrier to quant credit was "execution and the ability to model transaction costs and liquidity within decades-old manual processes" (`Man_Revolutions_in_Credit_Trading_2023 p.2`), and the revolutions removed it. The chapter's job is to date each revolution, size it with sourced numbers for 2018–2026, and show the causal arrow from market structure to the two things a systematic strategy needs: **certainty of execution** and **a modelable cost** (`Man_Revolutions p.4`).

**The aha for Theo (ex-RSSG).** At PJT the unit of analysis is a document and a process; prices arrive by phone, late, from a dealer who is also your counterparty. Theo's instinct is that a bond's value lives in the indenture. The desk's instinct is that a bond's *tradability* lives in its data exhaust: how many prints per day, how many dealers answer an RFQ, how far the cover is from the winner, whether the bond is in an ETF redemption basket. The aha is that **electronification turned liquidity from a relationship into a measurable, forecastable variable**, and that this variable, not the credit agreement, decides whether an alpha is harvestable. The second, sharper aha: TRACE's `5MM+` cap means the market still hides exactly the trades Theo used to care about (blocks in stressed names), so the RSSG skill set is most valuable precisely where the data exhaust is thinnest.

**One-sentence version for the cold open:** the bond market spent twenty years building the dataset that lets a machine price a bond in three seconds, and the first thing the machine learned is which prints it is not being shown.

## 3. Derivation skeletons

All of this chapter's mathematics is under the physical measure $\mathbb{P}$; there is no pricing measure in market-structure statistics. Say so once, early, because Kaz will ask.

### DER A — The adoption S-curve and the two-point logit fit

Let $e_t \in (0,1)$ be the electronic share of notional in a segment. Model
$$e_t = \frac{K}{1+\exp\{-r\,(t-t_0)\}}, \qquad 0<K\le 1,$$
with $K$ the ceiling (blocks that never leave the phone), $r$ the adoption rate, $t_0$ the midpoint. With $K=1$,
$$\operatorname{logit}(e_t) \equiv \ln\frac{e_t}{1-e_t} = r\,(t-t_0),$$
so two dated observations give a closed-form slope
$$\hat r = \frac{\operatorname{logit}(e_{t_2})-\operatorname{logit}(e_{t_1})}{t_2-t_1}, \qquad \text{odds-doubling time } = \frac{\ln 2}{\hat r}.$$
Plugging Coalition Greenwich's 2015 → 2025 shares (IG 20% → 48%, HY 6% → 32%): $\hat r_{IG}=0.131/\text{yr}$, $\hat r_{HY}=0.200/\text{yr}$; odds-doubling 5.3 years (IG) and 3.5 years (HY); naive $K=1$ projections for 2030 are 64% (IG) and 56% (HY). **Label these *illustrative extrapolations*.** Error-prone spots: (i) $K<1$ is the honest model (≈90% of blocks were still voice/chat in 2023), so the $K=1$ fit overstates the ceiling; (ii) denominators differ (notional vs ticket count vs "customer volume"); Bondwave's PT share of ~20% and Trumid/MarketAxess's ~11–12% of TRACE describe the same market with different denominators; (iii) "processed" trades (voice-negotiated, booked electronically) inflate platform shares; The DESK applies a −40% haircut to some platform figures (fi-desk, Jan 14, 2026); (iv) HY e-share *falls* in stress (29–30% in Feb–Mar 2025), so monthly points are noisy; fit on annual data.

### DER B — From cost to breadth: why cheaper tickets create alpha

Grinold's fundamental law, $IR \approx IC\sqrt{BR}$, with breadth $BR$ the number of independent bets per year. Net alpha per unit of gross exposure is
$$\alpha_{\text{net}} = \alpha_{\text{gross}} - \mathrm{TO}\cdot c,$$
with $\mathrm{TO}$ annual one-way turnover and $c$ the one-way cost (fraction of notional). The break-even turnover is $\mathrm{TO}^* = \alpha_{\text{gross}}/c$, so the feasible breadth of a cost-constrained strategy scales as $1/c$. Man's reported fall in trading cost from 12 to 7.5 bp per million (Trace/ICE BofA, as at Sept 30, 2025) raises feasible turnover by 60% for the same gross alpha. Tie to `Man_2022 p.7`: quant strategies imply "larger amounts of smaller-sized trades." Error-prone: $\mathrm{TO}$ must be one-way if $c$ is half-spread, two-way if $c$ is LCS (round trip). Momentum has the highest turnover (`Man_2022 p.19`), so it benefits most from the cost decline: a bridge to Ch 10.

### DER B′ — LCS and the Man TC decomposition (units!)

Barclays LCS for a spread-quoted bond: $\mathrm{LCS}_i = D^{\text{spr}}_i\,(s^{\text{bid}}_i - s^{\text{ask}}_i)$; for a price-quoted bond: $(P^{\text{ask}}_i-P^{\text{bid}}_i)/P^{\text{bid}}_i$ (`Dynkin p.702`). Derive the equivalence from $\Delta P/P \approx -D^{\text{spr}}\Delta s$: the bid is the *higher* spread, so $s^{\text{bid}}>s^{\text{ask}}$ and LCS is positive. Man's actionable cost: $\mathrm{TC} = \text{inside market spread} + \text{slippage} + \text{spread calibration}$ (`Man_2022 p.21`), where spread calibration measures whether dealers fade or improve their runs, estimated from TRACE. Error-prone: spread in decimal vs bp; spread duration vs modified duration; LCS is a *round trip*; trader runs are indications, not firm (`Ben_Dor p.120`), which is exactly what RFQ and streaming fixed.

### DER C — The RFQ as an auction: order statistics of dealer quotes

Let the $n$ responders' quote distances from fair mid be i.i.d. with CDF $F$ on $[0,\infty)$. The expected best quote is
$$\mathbb{E}\big[\delta_{(1)}\big] = \int_0^\infty \big(1-F(x)\big)^n\,dx .$$
For exponential distances with mean $\theta$: $\mathbb{E}[\delta_{(1)}]=\theta/n$, and by memorylessness the expected winner-to-cover gap is $\theta/(n-1)$. (Monte Carlo check this session: $n=3,5,8,12$ gave $0.331, 0.198, 0.126, 0.084$ vs $0.333, 0.200, 0.125, 0.083$.) Interpretation: Tradeweb's "median distance to cover" falling to a record low in 2024 is what you expect when $n$ rises and dispersion $\theta$ falls. **Caveats that make it a derivation and not a slogan:** (i) quotes are not independent when every dealer anchors to the same composite (CP+/Ai-Price), so the *effective* $n$ is smaller; (ii) in a common-value setting (the RFQ comes from someone who knows about an LMT), rational dealers shade *more* as $n$ grows (winner's curse), so the best quote can worsen with competition: this is the hinge to Ch 3 and Ch 18; (iii) Hendershott–Madhavan's endogenous venue choice: easy trades go electronic, so measured e-savings are selection-biased; (iv) non-response is censoring, not a zero.

### DER D — Portfolio trade pricing (skeleton; the engine is Ch 19)

Dealer's bid for basket $w$ (signed notionals):
$$B_{\text{PT}} = \sum_i w_i P_i^{\text{mid}} - \sum_i |w_i|\,c_i^{\text{line}} + \underbrace{\Pi(w)}_{\text{netting/ETF credit}} - \tfrac{\gamma}{2}\,T_{\text{liq}}\; w^\top \Sigma_\epsilon w .$$
$\Pi(w)$ is the value of being able to deliver the basket into an ETF creation or net it against inventory; $\Sigma_\epsilon$ is residual (post-hedge) covariance over the liquidation horizon $T_{\text{liq}}$. The 2022 lesson in `Man_Revolutions p.3` (PT costs rose when custom baskets were "lost") is $\Pi(w)$ collapsing. Bondwave's execution-quality percentile for PTs drifting from 57.3 (May 2023) to 50.3 (Feb 2026) is the arbitrage compressing toward median. Error-prone: benchmark time (nearly half of PT volume prints at or after the 4 p.m. index mark); "mystery box" adverse selection; $\Sigma_\epsilon$ ill-conditioned for stale bonds (ridge/Ledoit–Wolf).

### DER E — The ETF discount as the price of immediacy (skeleton; engine is Ch 20)

$P^{\text{ETF}}_t - \mathrm{NAV}_t = -\,\mathcal{C}^{\text{redeem}}_t + \eta_t$, where $\mathcal{C}^{\text{redeem}}$ is the cost of liquidating the redemption basket at the bid and $\eta_t$ is the ETF's information lead over stale NAV marks. In March 2020 both terms were large and of the same sign. Kargar et al. estimate customers' willingness to pay for immediacy rose by about 200 bp per dollar at the peak; Todorov reports bond-ETF tracking errors above 200 bp against a historical 0.7 bp. Error-prone: NAV is bid-side for many bond ETFs; TRACE marks lag; HY vs IG discounts inverted the usual ranking (Haddad–Moreira–Muir: safer ETFs discounted more).

## 4. Code spec: `ch02_electronification_trend.py`

**What it computes.** A small, runnable reference engine with three pieces.

1. `fit_adoption(years, shares, K=None)`: inputs two `np.ndarray` of shape `(N,)`; clips shares to `[1e-4, 1-1e-4]`; returns `(r, t0)` from OLS on `logit(share)` when `K` is `None` or `1.0`, else fits the 3-parameter logistic by `scipy.optimize.curve_fit` with bounds `K∈(0.05,1.0]`, `r∈(0,2]`, falling back to the logit-OLS solution if the optimizer fails. Also returns `odds_doubling_years = ln 2 / r` and a projection table for 2026–2030. Seeded data table inline: IG `{2015:0.20, 2024:0.48, 2025:0.48}`, HY `{2015:0.06, 2024:0.33, 2025:0.32}` (sources in §6), flagged as the sourced observations the writer may extend.
2. `rfq_best_of_n(theta, n, n_sims=20000, seed=7)`: Monte Carlo mean of the minimum of `n` exponential draws vs the closed form `theta/n`; also returns the mean winner-to-cover gap vs `theta/(n-1)`.
3. `lcs_spread_quoted(D_spr, s_bid, s_ask)` and `lcs_price_quoted(P_bid, P_ask)`: returns LCS as a fraction of price; asserts `s_bid >= s_ask`, `P_ask >= P_bid`, and checks that the two conventions agree to first order when `P_ask - P_bid ≈ P_bid · D_spr · (s_bid - s_ask)`.

**Built-in sanity checks (asserts, printed as a table).** (a) With exactly two observations, the OLS slope equals the closed-form two-point slope to `1e-12`. (b) With `K` fixed at `1.0`, `curve_fit` reproduces the logit-OLS `(r, t0)` within `1e-6`. (c) Projections are monotone in `t` and lie in `(0, K)`. (d) `rfq_best_of_n` within 2% of `theta/n` for `n∈{3,5,8,12}`. (e) LCS conventions agree within 5% for a 5-year spread-duration bond with a 10 bp bid-ask in spread.

**Schemas and shapes.** `observations: DataFrame[year:float, segment:str, share:float, source:str]`; `projection: DataFrame[year:int, segment:str, share_hat:float, K:float]`. No tensors beyond `(N,)`. **Edge cases:** shares of 0 or 1 (clip, warn); a single observation (refuse to fit; return `None`); non-monotone series (fit anyway, print residuals). **Numerical guards:** `np.log` on clipped values only; `curve_fit` with `maxfev=10000` and explicit `p0=(0.9, r_ols, t0_ols)`. **ROBO literalism line for the box:** asked to "project electronic share to 2030," ROBO returns 103% because nobody told it $K\le1$.

### 6.1 TRACE: the regulatory history (VERIFIED-URL: FINRA TRACE Fact Book 2025, https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/TRACE_FactBook_2025.pdf; FINRA reporting-timeframes page, https://www.finra.org/filing-reporting/trade-reporting-and-compliance-engine-trace/trace-reporting-timeframes)

- Sept 1998: SEC asks NASD for corporate-debt transparency (`Bohn_Stein p.91`, fn 6). **July 1, 2002:** TRACE launches; 75-minute reporting; Phase I disseminates IG issues ≥\$1bn and the "FIPS 50" HY names; ≈520 securities disseminated by end-2002.
- **March 3, 2003** Phase IIa (AAA/AA/A ≥\$100mm); **April 14, 2003** Phase IIb (120 BBB + 50 HY); ≈4,650 bonds. **Oct 1, 2003:** 45 minutes. **Oct 1, 2004:** Phase IIIa; 30 minutes. **Feb 7, 2005:** Phase IIIb; ≈99% of public transactions and 95% of par disseminated immediately. **July 1, 2005:** 15 minutes. **Jan 9, 2006:** immediate dissemination of all public TRACE-eligible corporates.
- Later: Nov 3, 2008 equity-CUSIP debt; Mar 1, 2010 agency debentures and primary-market trades; May 16, 2011 ABS/MBS reportable; Nov 12, 2012 TBA disseminated; July 22, 2013 specified pools; **June 30, 2014** Rule 144A trades disseminated; June 1, 2015 ABS; Mar 20, 2017 CMOs; **July 10, 2017** Treasuries reportable (regulatory only); Sept 1, 2022 covered depository institutions; **May 15, 2023** portfolio-trade modifier required on corporate bond reports, plus 60-minute Treasury reporting; Nov 6, 2023 USD foreign sovereigns.
- Dissemination caps today: `5MM+` IG, `1MM+` HY (corporates and agencies); ABS `10MM+`; TBA good-delivery `25MM+`; on-the-run Treasuries end-of-day with `250MM+/150MM+/50MM+` caps (FINRA page, last updated 3/29/24).

### 6.2 The one-minute rule that took three years to not happen (VERIFIED-URL: SEC Release 34-101121, Sept 20, 2024, https://www.sec.gov/files/rules/sro/finra/2024/34-101121.pdf; Mayer Brown Free Writings, Feb 10, 2025, https://www.freewritings.law/2025/02/finra-will-not-set-an-effective-date-for-amendments-to-trace-rules-establishing-one-minute-reporting-requirement-finra-to-prepare-new-filing-for-public-comment/; FINRA Regulatory Notice 25-17, Dec 4, 2025, https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/25-17)

- FINRA filed SR-FINRA-2024-004 on Jan 11, 2024 (Federal Register Jan 25, 2024): cut the 15-minute outer limit to **one minute**, with a manual-trades exception (five-minute outer limit phased in over three years) and a de minimis exception (<4,000 trades/yr, representing 1.41% of trades and 0.43% of par).
- FINRA's own statistics in the order: **82.9% of trades** executed 8:00 a.m.–6:15 p.m. ET were already reported within one minute in 2022; **90.6% within three minutes**; the rule would move "up to 16.4% of current annual trading volume, or up to 6.1 million trades and 20 trillion dollars in par value" to faster reporting.
- SEC approved Sept 20, 2024. **Feb 5, 2025:** FINRA CEO Robert Cook said FINRA would not set an effective date. **Dec 4, 2025 (RN 25-17):** FINRA "is not moving forward" with one-minute reporting; SR-FINRA-2025-008 rescinds the 2024 changes; the 15-minute standard remains; a separate allocation-reporting option (Rule 6730.08) takes effect **June 8, 2026**.
- Desk meaning: the regulator's own data say the market is already a one-minute market for ~83% of tickets; the fight was over the ~17% that are manual, which is where blocks and distressed live. Model the latency of a print as a mixture: a fast electronic component and a slow manual tail.

### 6.3 Electronic share and volumes, 2015–2026 (Coalition Greenwich unless noted)

## 7. Case studies

**Case 1 — March 2020: the plumbing's first stress test (dates in §6.6).** Sequence: Feb 24–Mar 23 selloff; IG/Treasury ETFs at discounts larger than HY (Haddad et al.); willingness to pay for immediacy +≈200 bp (Kargar et al.); tracking errors >200 bp (Todorov); Mar 23 SMCCF; Apr 9 expansion; purchases May–Dec 2020; all sold by Aug 31, 2021. What a systematic desk would have flagged: the ETF–NAV basis as a regime variable; a jump in Man-style spread calibration (dealers fading runs); e-share rising as voice capacity vanished (`Man_Revolutions p.5`). Trade: route to A2A, shrink tickets, lean on ETF create/redeem as the hedge; the chapter should be honest that the *announcement*, not the purchases, did the work (Haddad et al.).

**Case 2 — The portfolio-trading cycle, 2021–2026.** 2021 boom (`Man_Revolutions p.2`), 2022 cost reversal and the loss of custom baskets (`p.3`), Man's >90% migration to electronic RFQ with "significant savings" vs PT (`p.5`); then the 2024–26 resurgence: 7–8% (2023) → 11.4% of TRACE (Dec 2024) → 12% on the \$89.7bn record day (Apr 8, 2025) → MarketAxess US credit PT share 19% (2025) and US HY PT 23% (H2 2025) → 19.8% of customer volume (Q1 2026, Bondwave) with execution quality converging to the median. Lesson: protocol share is a cost-minimizing choice that cycles with dealer balance sheet and ETF-basket economics; model it as regime-switching, not a trend.

**Case 3 — April 2025: the tariff shock the new plumbing absorbed.** \$89.7bn of corporates on Apr 8 (PT 12%); \$92bn on Apr 30; \$98.9bn of US fixed-income ETF volume on Apr 7 with 1-cent ETF spreads; \$2.4tn of Treasuries on Apr 9; all-to-all 11% of April market volume; IG e-share 45–52% through spring, HY dipping to ≈29–30%. No facility, no discounts of the March 2020 kind. The contrast with 2020 is the chapter's "trend proof": the same shock class, five years later, cleared through electronic channels. (Optional mini-case: Citadel Securities' entry, mid-2023 → \$2bn/day and ≈15% of long-dated blocks by 2025.)
