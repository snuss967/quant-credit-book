## 1. The spine

The cold open already asks the right question and the chapter already answers it on one line, (18.9). What Edition 1 does not do is make the reader *feel* it before the derivations arrive. The feeling to land by the end of the cold open: **the model priced the part it was asked about, and nobody priced the rest.** Kaz's auto-quoter bid 90.875 on an \$8mm sell of Halvorsen with the lender call eighteen minutes away; Ines bid 90.25 and lost; the bond printed 86. The reader should finish the opener wanting to know what the other 0.65 points *were*, who should have known them, and how a machine could be taught to.

The chapter then answers in the order the desk would discover it. Roll (§18.2) is how you see a spread when nobody shows you one. Glosten–Milgrom and Kyle (§18.3) are the suspicion charge: the client knew. Avellaneda–Stoikov (§18.4) is Kaz's quote, and the twist is that it is 94% greed and 6% fear, "a monopolist with a nervous tic". GLFT (§18.5) is the dealer with no 4 p.m. and a risk limit. Jump-to-default (§18.6) is where the real fear lives, and Altice France is the day it was 21 points. The hit-ratio section (§18.7) is the price of being asked again tomorrow, and it closes the loop by re-pricing the cold open term by term. TRACE (§18.8) and BWICs (§18.9) are the data exhaust and the auction turned inside out, with the LDI week as the auction in which every bidder knew more lists were coming. By the Debrief the reader should be able to look at any bid and name five numbers.

The two long scenes are Altice France, 20 March 2024 (the RV beat, PB-14, seen from the dealer's chair as two RFQs forty minutes apart) and the LDI BWIC week, 27 September to 7 October 2022 (a first-price auction with a leak, bid blind).
