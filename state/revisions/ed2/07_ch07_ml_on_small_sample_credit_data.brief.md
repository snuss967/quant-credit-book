## 1. The spine

The cold open already has the right engine (Ines: "is it a good hand for a guy who looked at three thousand of them?"), but the reader is handed the chapter's answer in the next paragraph as a bolded question plus an eight-item syllabus. Edition 2 should make the reader *feel* one question before any answer arrives:

**"How much evidence do I actually have, and how would I know if the answer were none?"**

Theo's instinct from RSSG is the key: in a restructuring nobody asks whether a bond will go up; they ask whether the thesis is right, how sure you are, and how much to put on. The chapter answers the spine question four times, each time at a different layer, and the writer should make that architecture visible as a story rather than a list: (1) count the hats, not the rows (§7.1, §7.6); (2) make the label honest about scale, path, exits, and carry (§7.4); (3) let the model peek at nothing (§7.7, §7.9); (4) deflate for the hands you folded (§7.8). Meta-labeling (§7.5) is the reward: once the evidence is counted honestly, the banker's covenant read becomes the primary model and the machine learns only when to trust it. The Desk Debrief already closes the loop (AUC 0.61 falls to 0.52; "mostly carry and one company"); keep it, and make the two long scenes below earn it.

The two real events that carry the chapter are the **Bai–Bali–Wen retraction (2019–2023)** and the **2026 short-term-reversal autopsy** (Dickerson–Robotti–Rossetti). Both are already in §7.2, §7.9, §7.11 as compressed case notes. Edition 2 turns them into scenes the desk lives through, with the fictional cast arguing and changing their minds, and real people saying only what the public record has them saying. The dossier is explicit on tone: state the retraction and the errors, say nothing about intent (`state/research/papers_factor_replication_crisis_and_bond_data.md` §4.1).
