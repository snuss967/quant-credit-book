## 1. The spine

The question the reader should feel at the end of the cold open is Ines's, sharpened: **on this desk, who is allowed to say yes, who is allowed to say no, and how fast must each of them be?** Theo has just learned that for a second and a half his streaming bid was a free put written to anyone with a futures screen. The reader's instinct (and ROBO's) is "make it faster." The chapter's answer, earned over eight sections, is that speed is a property of *refusal*, not of pricing: the slow path (prices, security master, OMS, humans) is the only thing allowed to say yes, and it may be slow, clever and audited; the fast path (trigger table, hard limits, kill switch) may only say no, and it must be dumb, bounded and first. Every tool is one clause of that answer: the e-folding time tells you which clock a decision lives on; the bitemporal master tells you what you knew when you said yes; the queue tells you how late your yes really arrives; the trigger table is the fastest no; CUSUM and the tier hierarchy are the slower, statistical no's; and the two long scenes (Knight 2012, the Citi/Revlon wire 2020) are what happens when a machine or a screen can say yes and nothing can say no. The closing line stays: **the fastest thing on a credit desk should be the thing that says no.**

The chapter already has a strong cold open and good derivations. Its Edition 1 faults are the ones the user named: case studies of 150–250 words that state the record and map it to tiers; four theorems and three derivations arriving before intuition; tables and bold-lead paragraphs doing the work of scenes; and no pod, no vignette, no other seats. Fix those; keep everything else.
