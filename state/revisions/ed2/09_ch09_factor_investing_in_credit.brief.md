## 1. The spine

The cold open already asks the right question and must keep asking it louder: **"So what's left, after you take the spread risk out?"** The reader should feel, by the end of §9.0, that a 1.9 Sharpe is a number with three tenants living inside it, beta, carry and cost, and that the landlord has not yet checked whether anyone else is home. The chapter answers in four moves, and every scene is one of them. (1) The beta tenant: DTS is beta, so neutralize risk, not dollars (§9.2); the cold open's 1.9 becomes 0.0 before a signal has done anything. (2) The dealer tenant: in credit the cost is the wrong sign, not a footnote (§9.4.2, §9.8), so a factor is a turnover budget with a signal attached. (3) The ghost tenant: the price itself lies, and lies into both the sort and the return (§9.9), so half the published factors were the noise applauding itself. (4) What remains is a residual inside a peer cell, and the residual is either cheapness or information the model cannot read (§9.3.4), which is the RSSG reader's seat. The live record (§9.10, Robeco since 2015, State Street since 2024) is the receipt: budget for a net IR near 0.5, not 2.

Extend the cold open to about 1,200 words, not by adding exposition but by letting the argument run one more round: Kaz defends ("value is in every paper"), Ines asks the poker question a second time ("who at this table knows more than you?" and the answer is the dealer, who sets the bid–ask), and Mags says the word nobody has said yet, "marks": "Those month-end prices. Who made them?" Three tenants named in dialogue, none explained. Then cut the "By the end of this chapter you will be able to" bullets into one paragraph of what the desk will have on the screen a week later (the Debrief's 0.9 gross, 0.7 net, beta 0.02).
